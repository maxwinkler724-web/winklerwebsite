Data Center Cooling: Hot and Cold Aisles, CRAC vs CRAH and Inlet Temperatures

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A data center is mostly a machine for turning electricity into heat and moving that heat outside. The cooling job is to put cold air where the servers breathe in and to carry the hot exhaust away without letting the two mix. This lesson covers the layout, the two kinds of room cooling units, the inlet temperatures the references aim for, and a worked example you can repeat on any load.

For a closet-sized room, start with Cooling a Small Server Room or Network Closet. This page picks up where that one stops, with rows of racks and room-level units.

Hot aisle, cold aisle

The U.S. Department of Energy's data center design guide describes the basic layout: racks sit back to back in rows, with cold aisles on the intake side and hot aisles on the exhaust side. The guide says rack return air is typically 85°F or higher, and densely loaded racks can exceed 100°F. It also says the air temperature rise across a server runs from about 10°F to over 40°F, which is why the airflow you need depends on the delta-T you can hold.

Bypass and recirculation

Two leaks cause most airflow trouble. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (LBNL) training slides define bypass air as cold air that never cools equipment, and recirculation as hot exhaust that returns to equipment intakes. LBNL ties net bypass to supply airflow exceeding what the IT gear pulls, and net recirculation to supply falling short of it. Its listed causes of bypass include excess supply airflow, misplaced perforated tiles and leaky cable cutouts. Its listed causes of recirculation include too little supply air, missing blanking panels and gaps between racks. LBNL says recirculation often causes hot spots, and that both problems can exist in the same room at once.

LBNL also describes a quick indicator: compare the cooling unit's supply and return temperatures with the IT equipment's. A low temperature difference across the unit points to bypass; a high one points to recirculation.

Containment

Containment puts a physical barrier between the cold and hot sides. The DOE guide mentions flexible strip curtains or solid partitions that seal the gap between rack tops and the return path, and baffle panels above the racks and at the ends of hot aisles. LBNL notes that enclosed aisles allow higher supply and return temperatures, that full containment is not always necessary because baffles over racks can cut bypass, and that barrier placement has to account for fire codes. Cold aisle containment and hot aisle containment are two ways to build the same wall. Which one a site chose is a design decision, so find out before you open a door or panel.

CRAC and CRAH

A CRAC is a computer room air conditioner: a packaged direct expansion (DX) unit with its own compressor. A CRAH is a computer room air handler: a fan and a coil fed with chilled water from a central plant. STULZ, a maker of both, says a CRAC rejects heat through an air-cooled or water-cooled condenser, suits small or stand-alone sites without a chilled-water plant, and needs regular compressor and refrigerant checks. It says a CRAH uses variable-speed fans, suits large sites with a chiller plant, depends on that plant staying up, and needs coil cleaning, water treatment and fan inspections. The DOE guide calls packaged DX units the most common choice for smaller data centers, and notes that CRAH units on chilled water of 55°F or higher with variable speed fans can keep cooling energy low. Chilled-water basics are in Chilled Water Pumps and Delta-T.

Supply can come through a raised floor, where the DOE guide asks for at least 24 inches of clear height and warns that cable congestion cuts airflow through the perforated tiles, or from overhead ducts and diffusers. The guide also warns that adding or removing tiles to chase one hot spot can cause problems across the whole system.

What temperature to aim for

The number that counts is the air at the server inlet. ENERGY STAR says ASHRAE widened its recommended range from 68 to 77°F to 64.4 to 80.6°F, measured at the front of the server. The DOE guide's table lists that same recommended range, 64.4 to 80.6°F (18 to 27°C), for equipment classes A1 through A4. It lists wider allowable ranges, for example 59 to 89.6°F for class A1 and 50 to 95°F for A2, and says the allowable envelope is tested for function, not for long-term reliability. ASHRAE's technical committee that writes these limits is TC 9.9. We took the numbers from the DOE and ENERGY STAR pages, not from the standard itself. ENERGY STAR adds that higher inlet temperatures can save energy, but cites a maker study warning that very high inlets can speed up server fans and raise total power.

For humidity, the DOE guide gives a recommended floor of a 15.8°F dew point, and LBNL's slides give a practice ceiling of 55% relative humidity.

Economizers and PUE

The DOE guide describes air-side economizers (outside air, with MERV 13 filters and a dew point lockout) and water-side economizers, which put a heat exchanger upstream of the chillers and suit climates with many cool wet-bulb hours. Higher return temperatures extend the free-cooling hours. The general idea is in Economizers Basics.

PUE, power usage effectiveness, is a ratio. Vertiv's explainer gives it as total facility energy divided by IT equipment energy, and The Green Grid, which developed the metric, describes it the same way: total data center energy over ICT equipment energy. The DOE guide notes PUE runs from 1.0 upward and cites an average near 1.6. Vertiv cautions that comparing PUE between sites is hard, so track your own site over time.

Worked example: 100 kW of IT load

Our own arithmetic, from stated numbers: 100 kW of IT load, a 20°F air delta-T, and 160 kW total facility power.

  1. Heat equals power. The APC (Schneider Electric) paper says the thermal output is the same as the power input. So the room must remove 100 kW.
  2. Btu/h. Our own: 100 kW × 3,412 Btu/h per kW = 341,200 Btu/h.
  3. Tons. Our own: 341,200 ÷ 12,000 Btu/h per ton = 28.4 tons.
  4. Airflow. Our own: cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × delta-T) = 341,200 ÷ (1.08 × 20) = 341,200 ÷ 21.6 = about 15,800 cfm, roughly 158 cfm per kW.
  5. PUE. Our own: 160 kW ÷ 100 kW = 1.6. The other 60 kW goes to cooling, power conversion losses and lighting. This treats the power as steady; true PUE uses energy over time.

Our own check: if the servers only warm the air 10°F, the same load needs twice the airflow, about 31,600 cfm. That is why a low delta-T at the cooling unit signals bypass.

Field card: Data Center Cooling

Cold air in the front, hot air out the back, and keep them apart.

  • Check inlet temperature at the server front, not the room average.
  • Missing blanking panels and open gaps cause recirculation and hot spots.
  • Perforated tiles go in the cold aisle only; do not move them without a plan.
  • CRAC = DX with its own compressor. CRAH = fan and chilled-water coil.
  • kW × 3,412 = Btu/h; Btu/h ÷ 12,000 = tons; cfm = Btu/h ÷ (1.08 × delta-T).
  • PUE = total facility energy ÷ IT energy; 1.0 is the floor.
  • Check drains and humidity readings on coil units, and ask which units are the N+1 spare.

What techs check

Start with inlet temperatures at the rack fronts. Then look for open rack slots (blanking panels), gaps between racks and cable cutouts, and confirm perforated tiles sit only in cold aisles. Check humidity readings against the site's limits. STULZ notes a CRAC coil cools and dehumidifies the air; a coil that dehumidifies makes condensate, so check drain pans and drain paths per the maker's manual. Redundancy is usually written N+1, meaning one unit more than the load needs. The DOE guide uses N+1 when it discusses UPS, so ask the site what its design says for cooling units before you shut one down. Emergency stopgaps are in Portable Spot Coolers and Evaporative Coolers. Data center work often needs site rules, lockout and an escort; follow them.
