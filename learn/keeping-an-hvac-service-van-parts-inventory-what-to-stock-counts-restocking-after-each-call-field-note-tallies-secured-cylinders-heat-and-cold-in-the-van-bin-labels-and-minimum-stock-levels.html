Keeping an HVAC Service Van Parts Inventory: Counts, Restocking and Minimum Levels

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A van full of parts is not the same as a van with the right parts. A good inventory means you can finish most calls on the first trip, you never open a bin and find it empty, and you can tell at a glance what to reorder. This lesson covers what to stock, how to count, how to restock, and how to use your own notes to set minimum levels.

Start with what fails most

The parts that go bad most often are cheap, small and electrical, so stock those deepest. A reasonable starting list for a residential and light commercial tech:

  • Capacitors, by rating. Keep run capacitors in the ratings you actually meet, sorted by microfarad value and voltage rating. Match the microfarad value to the nameplate, and never go lower on voltage rating than the original. See Capacitors and Contactors for how they fail.
  • Contactors. A few in the common pole counts and coil voltages for your area.
  • Fuses. Small control board fuses in the amp ratings on the boards you see.
  • Igniters and flame sensors. Gas furnaces eat these. See Flame Sensor Cleaning and Microamps; cleaning a sensor is often enough, but a spare turns a second trip into a first-trip fix.
  • Filter driers. Keep common line sizes. Leave each one sealed until you braze it in. See Filter Driers and Sight Glasses.
  • Fittings and brazing supplies. Couplings, elbows, caps, flux if you use it, brazing alloy sticks and cleaning cloth. Buy the alloy types your work calls for.

Our own rule of thumb: if a part is small, cheap and fails often, carry a few. If it is large, costly and rarely fails, do not carry it; order it for the job instead.

Label the bins

Put a label on every bin: the part, the rating or size, and the minimum count. Label the bin front, not just the lid, because lids get swapped. Sort capacitors by rating on one tray so you read the number, not guess. Keep sealed driers and fittings in their own bins so nothing gets crushed. A label with a minimum count is the whole system in one line: when the bin drops to that number, it goes on the restock list.

Keep counts and restock after every call

The habit that works is simple: use a part, write it down, and fix the shelf before you drive away or at the end of the stop. Do not wait for Friday. Friday restocking depends on memory, and memory misses the one igniter you used on Tuesday.

Count once a week, even if your notes say everything is fine. Open each bin and count by hand. Notes miss parts that were borrowed, dropped or tossed. Write the count next to the minimum, and circle anything at or below it.

Use your field notes to see what you use most

If you already write field notes after each call, add a line for parts used. At the end of the month, tally each part. Two things show up quickly: a short list of parts that make up most of your use, and a long list you hardly touch. Stock the first list deeper and the second list lighter. Your own tally beats any generic list, because your territory, equipment age and season shape what breaks.

Refrigerant cylinders: secured and logged

Cylinders are not inventory bins. They are hazardous material packages and need their own handling. The federal vehicle loading rule in 49 CFR 177.834(a) says that hazardous material packages not permanently attached to the vehicle must be secured against shifting under normal transport conditions, including movement relative to each other, and that packages with valves or fittings must be loaded to minimize the chance of damage. In plain terms: strap them, keep them from rolling or hitting each other, and protect the valves. Our own habit is a cylinder log, kept with the parts counts, with the cylinder label name, size, date in the van and weight on the scale when it goes in and out. That makes a missing or empty cylinder obvious. For securing methods and label reading, see Carrying Refrigerant Cylinders in a Service Van and Driving and Loading an HVAC Service Van Safely. We do not repeat them here.

Heat and cold in the van

A parked van gets much hotter than the air outside in summer and can fall to outdoor temperature overnight in winter. We did not find a federal figure for parts storage, so use this as general good practice and defer to the label or the maker's literature for each item:

  • Chemicals and sealed products. Read the storage statement on each can, bottle or tube. Anything with a stated temperature limit that the van can exceed should come out of the van overnight or in heat waves.
  • Capacitors, boards and sensors. Extreme heat shortens electronic part life, so keep spare boards and capacitors in closed bins out of direct sun, and do not stack them against the van wall.
  • Packaging. Moisture from temperature swings can corrode bare fittings and rod. Keep brazing alloy and fittings in sealed or lidded bins.
  • You. A van cab is not a safe place to cool off in extreme heat; see Heat Stress in Attics and on Roofs.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example, not a real shop's numbers. Assumptions: one tech runs 44 calls in a four-week month; the parts below are the ones used; the van can be restocked from a supplier within about one week; we want a small safety margin of one extra piece.

Rule we use (our own): weekly use = monthly use divided by 4. Minimum stock = weekly use rounded up, plus 1 spare. The bin goes on the restock list when it hits that minimum.

Step by step for one row: 45/5 microfarad capacitor, 6 used. 6 / 4 = 1.5 per week. Round up to 2. Add 1 spare to get a minimum of 3. For the flame sensor: 7 / 4 = 1.75, round up to 2, add 1, minimum 3. For brazing alloy: 20 sticks / 4 = 5 per week, plus 1 gives 6.

Limits of this method: it uses one month, so a hot July and a mild October give different numbers. Recalculate each season. A part that stops the whole system and is hard to find in an emergency may deserve a higher minimum than the math gives.

Field card: Keeping an HVAC Service Van Parts Inventory

Use it, write it, count it, restock it.

  • Stock the small, cheap, frequent failures deepest.
  • Label every bin with part, rating and minimum.
  • Restock after each call, count by hand weekly.
  • Tally field notes monthly to find what you use most.
  • Our own minimum: weekly use rounded up, plus 1.
  • Cylinders are secured, valves protected and logged.
  • Read storage limits on chemicals; heat and cold can affect parts.
