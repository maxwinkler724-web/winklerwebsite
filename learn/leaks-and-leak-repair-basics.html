Leaks and Leak Repair Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the EPA 608 Guide

Also in the Refrigeration Service Guide

A refrigeration system is supposed to be a closed loop. When refrigerant goes missing, it went somewhere. This lesson explains why leaks matter and what good habits look like.

Why leaks matter

A system that loses refrigerant cools poorly, runs longer and wears out parts. It also costs money to refill. Beyond that, many refrigerants harm the environment when released, and some create safety risks in enclosed spaces. For these reasons, federal rules limit deliberate release of refrigerants and set requirements for leak repair on certain larger equipment.

Low refrigerant is a symptom

Refrigerant is not used up like fuel. If a system is low, a leak is the usual cause. Topping off without finding the cause treats the symptom only. The problem comes back, and the repeated releases add up. Good practice is to look for the cause and document what was found and done.

Where leaks tend to happen

Leaks can show up anywhere, but some places deserve attention because of vibration, heat cycles, corrosion or frequent opening. These include connections and fittings, valves and their caps, joints, coils, seals and service ports. Age and rough environments raise the odds. Knowing common weak spots helps you start in sensible places.

General ideas for finding leaks

Technicians use several approaches, and the best choice depends on the system, the refrigerant and the site. The common categories are:

  • Visual checks. Look for oil stains, damaged lines, rubbing and corrosion. Oil often travels with leaking refrigerant.
  • Electronic detectors. Handheld devices that sense refrigerant in the air.
  • Bubble solutions. Applied on suspect joints to show escaping gas.
  • Fixed monitoring. Some sites have sensors that alarm when refrigerant is detected.

Use methods approved by the manufacturer and your employer. Do not use open flame to hunt for leaks, and use only equipment suited for the refrigerant in the system. The right testing approach also depends on whether the system is pressurized, and those details belong to manufacturer instructions and site procedures, not to a general lesson.

Fixing it right

A repair is not finished when the bubbles stop. Parts may need replacement, joints may need to be redone properly, and the system should be verified afterward. Refrigerant should be recovered, not vented, before opening a system. Follow the manufacturer's instructions and the applicable rules for how repairs and checks are verified.

Record-keeping, in general

Written records are how owners, technicians and regulators know what happened to a system. For larger equipment, the EPA's rules place specific record-keeping and leak-repair duties on owners and operators, including how quickly repairs or plans must be made after a leak exceeds certain limits.

Two rules cover leak repair, and which one applies depends on the refrigerant. For appliances holding 50 pounds or more of an ozone-depleting refrigerant (CFC or HCFC), the Section 608 leak repair rule in 40 CFR 82.157 applies. Since a 2020 rule took effect, that section no longer reaches systems that use only HFCs. HFC systems with 15 pounds or more of a refrigerant whose GWP is above 53 fall under the AIM Act Emissions Reduction and Reclamation rule, 40 CFR Part 84, with leak repair duties starting January 1, 2026. EPA's January 2026 leak repair fact sheet (40 CFR 84.106) gives trigger rates of 20 percent a year for commercial refrigeration, 30 percent for industrial process refrigeration and 10 percent for comfort cooling, refrigerated transport and other appliances, with repair due within 30 days of adding refrigerant (120 days if an industrial process shutdown is needed). Each repair needs an initial verification test and a follow-up test within 10 days of the successful initial test. If the owner decides to retrofit or retire the appliance instead of repairing it, or it keeps leaking after repairs and verification tests, a plan is due within 30 days with a schedule to finish within one year. Owners keep the records for three years, on paper or electronically. Residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pumps are exempt from these leak repair requirements; the fact sheet says chillers and certain VRF systems are covered under their own subsectors and not under that residential and light commercial subsector, so they are not part of the exemption. The fact sheet itself says not to rely on it for compliance, so confirm the details in 40 CFR 84.106 and Part 84 Subpart C. Rules are updated, so read EPA's leak repair pages and the regulation text for the current requirements.

Even where no rule applies, good records help. Useful items include the date, the equipment, the refrigerant type, the amount added or recovered, where the leak was found, what was done, and who did it.

Habits to build

  • Treat repeated low refrigerant as a leak until proven otherwise.
  • Document your work in a clear, simple way.
  • Tell the equipment owner when records or repairs are required by rule.
  • Ask your employer how leaks are reported and tracked at your site.

Field card: Leaks and Leak Repair Basics

Missing refrigerant went somewhere, so find it and write it down.

  • Low charge usually means a leak, not normal use.
  • Check likely weak spots: joints, valves, coils, seals, ports.
  • Use approved methods. Never use open flame to find leaks.
  • Recover refrigerant instead of venting it.
  • Check current EPA rules for leak repair and records.
  • Know which rule applies: 40 CFR 82.157 for CFC/HCFC at 50+ lb, the AIM Act rule (40 CFR Part 84) for HFC at 15+ lb. Confirm on EPA/eCFR.
