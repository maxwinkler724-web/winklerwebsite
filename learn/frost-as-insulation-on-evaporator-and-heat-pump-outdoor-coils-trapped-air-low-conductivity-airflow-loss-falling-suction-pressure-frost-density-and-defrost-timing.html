Frost as Insulation on Evaporator and Heat Pump Coils

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A frosted coil looks like a coil doing its job, because it is cold. But the white layer on the fins is mostly trapped air, and trapped air is a poor conductor. As frost grows it slows the heat moving into the refrigerant and narrows the gaps the air has to squeeze through. This lesson explains both effects, what they do to capacity and suction pressure, and why the timing of defrost matters.

What frost is made of

A university thesis on frost growth on a plain fin describes frost as a matrix of ice built from branching ice crystals, with the spaces between them filled with moist air. That is why frost is not like a solid sheet of ice. Water vapor in the air turns directly to ice crystals on any surface that is below freezing and below the air's dew point, and the crystals pile up loosely. Because so much of the layer is air, its thermal conductivity is far below that of the aluminum fin or copper tube underneath it. A model in a second university thesis lets frost conductivity rise as frost gets denser, which fits the picture: more ice and less air means better conduction.

Why frost insulates

Think of heat moving from the air to the refrigerant as a series of resistances: the air film at the coil surface, any frost layer, the fin and tube metal, and the refrigerant side. Series resistances add. Metal adds very little. Frost sits between the air and the cold metal and adds a layer of low-conductivity material that was not there on a clean coil. A national laboratory literature review of defrost technology says the frost layer increases thermal resistance between the air and the refrigerant. For a feel of the size of that resistance, see the worked example below. For the general series-resistance idea on a clean coil, see the related lesson on air-side and refrigerant-side heat transfer.

The second problem: choked airflow

Frost does not only add resistance. It also grows into the gaps between fins. The same laboratory review says frost hinders the airflow and raises the pressure drop through the coil, and that with the reduced flow the system's capacity drops rapidly. It cites a study concluding that the reduction in airflow was the dominant factor in the capacity drop of an evaporator. It also cites another study in which the frost layer's convective resistance, the air film at the frost surface, made up about 90 percent of the total thermal resistance, while conduction from the tube wall through to the frost surface was only 2 to 5 percent. Do not read that as proof that frost is harmless as insulation. It means that in that case the airflow and film effects mattered more than conduction alone, and that is why the worked example below says "before airflow loss is even counted."

A university thesis on display case coils gives a feel for the blockage. In its simulated baseline case, a fan-driven coil lost enough airflow that the case's air curtain broke down at about 530 cfm, and the simulation could not continue after about 8 hours when frost had blocked 77 percent of the initial free flow area. Those are modeled values for one case, not a rule for every coil.

Why capacity and suction pressure fall

Less airflow and more resistance both reduce how much heat the air can hand to the refrigerant. That is the capacity loss. The suction pressure drop is our own explanation of the same cause: the refrigerant absorbs less heat, so less of it boils, and the evaporating temperature has to fall to keep pulling heat across the larger resistance. Lower evaporating temperature means lower saturation pressure, and the suction gauge falls. Frost then forms faster, because the coil surface is colder. This is why a low suction pressure with a frosted coil and weak airflow is a common field picture, and why it is easy to mistake for a charge problem. Check the coil and the airflow before touching the charge. For how this looks on gauges during a defrost, see the related lessons on gauges and defrost loss.

Frost density changes with conditions

Not all frost is the same. The plain-fin thesis reports measured frost density of about 400 kg/m3 at the leading edge of a fin, much higher than the roughly 150 kg/m3 further downstream. It also reports a correlation in which density rises with frost surface temperature, so warmer and wetter conditions make denser frost, and it states that raising absolute humidity has an effect similar to raising fin temperature. It cites measurements showing frost thickness rising 86 percent when relative humidity went from 30 to 75 percent, and density rising with air velocity. The display case thesis cites measured densities of about 100 to 145 kg/m3 for a very cold plate near -35 C. In short, very cold, dry conditions tend to grow light, fluffy frost, while conditions near freezing with humid air tend to grow heavier frost. Dense frost conducts better but weighs more, takes more heat to melt, and still blocks the fin gaps.

Worked example (our own): how much resistance frost can add

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measurement and not from a published test. We compare resistance per square foot of coil surface.

Assumptions. Frost conductivity about 0.1 Btu per hour per foot per F (an assumed value for light frost, chosen for round numbers). Frost thickness 1/8 inch. Coil air-film resistance on a clean coil about 0.1 to 0.2 hour-square foot-F per Btu per square foot (an assumed value; it corresponds to an air-side film coefficient of about 5 to 10 Btu per hour per square foot per F). We ignore the metal and refrigerant side to keep the comparison simple.

  1. Convert the thickness: 1/8 inch divided by 12 inches per foot = 0.0104 ft.
  2. Conduction resistance of the frost: R = thickness divided by conductivity = 0.0104 ft divided by 0.1 Btu per hour per foot per F = about 0.10 hour-square foot-F per Btu.
  3. Clean coil, air film only: 0.1 to 0.2.
  4. Frosted coil: 0.1 to 0.2 for the film plus 0.10 for frost = 0.2 to 0.3.
  5. Increase: 0.10 divided by 0.2 = 50 percent at the high-film end, and 0.10 divided by 0.1 = 100 percent at the low-film end.

So with these assumptions, a frost layer only 1/8 inch thick can add about half or more to the heat-flow resistance, before any loss of airflow is counted. Denser frost would conduct better and add less resistance for the same thickness; thicker frost adds more. The airflow loss from the previous sections then comes on top of this.

Why defrost timing matters

Defrosting too late lets the coil run with low capacity, low suction pressure and high fan load for longer. Defrosting too often wastes heat and, on a heat pump, interrupts heating. The laboratory review notes that defrosts are typically scheduled every six or eight hours with a duration of 20 to 30 minutes, and cites one demand-defrost study that started defrosts as often as three times a day in hot, humid conditions and another study whose algorithm ran about every 38.8 hours compared with a manufacturer's eight-hour schedule. It also reports that humidity-based control under very light use could delay defrost for several weeks. The display case thesis reports blockage after about 5 hours at 50 percent relative humidity, 6.5 hours at 40 percent and 9 hours at 30 percent in one earlier study, which shows how much the right interval depends on moisture. A fixed timer cannot know the weather, so the best interval changes from day to day. The controls and sensors that decide this are covered in the related lessons, which we do not repeat here.

A federal energy department consumer page on air-source heat pumps recommends a unit with demand-defrost control, which it says reduces the number of defrost cycles and the energy they use, and says outdoor units should be shielded from high winds, which can cause defrosting problems.

What to check in the field

  • Look at frost pattern: even frost across the coil is different from frost only on part of it. Uneven patterns point to feed or airflow problems, not just weather.
  • Check coil cleanliness and fan operation before blaming the charge.
  • Compare suction pressure to the coil condition: a frosted coil with low suction is a different problem than a clean coil with low suction.
  • Do not chip ice off fins. Bent fins lose capacity for good.

Field card: Frost as Insulation on Evaporator and Heat Pump Coils

Frost is mostly trapped air: it insulates and it blocks airflow.

  • Frost is a porous ice-and-air matrix with low conductivity next to the metal fin.
  • It adds series resistance between air and refrigerant and narrows the fin gaps.
  • Capacity and suction pressure fall as frost builds; airflow loss is often the larger factor.
  • Our own example: 1/8 inch of frost at an assumed 0.1 conductivity gives R about 0.10, versus an assumed air film of 0.1 to 0.2.
  • Density rises with warmer, wetter, faster air; cold dry air grows light frost.
  • Defrost too late costs capacity; too often wastes heat. Check airflow before charge.
