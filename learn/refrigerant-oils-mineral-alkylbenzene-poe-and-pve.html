Refrigerant Oils: Mineral, Alkylbenzene, POE and PVE

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The oil in a compressor does not stay in the compressor. It leaves with the hot gas, rides around the whole circuit and has to come home. That only works if the oil and the refrigerant get along. This lesson covers which oils go with which refrigerants, why POE needs careful handling, and how an oil change works in a retrofit.

Why the oil has to travel

A compressor needs oil on its bearings, but some of that oil is carried out as a mist with the discharge gas. Our oil return lesson covers pipe sizing and gas velocity. The chemistry matters just as much. BITZER, a compressor maker, explains that when refrigerant dissolves in the oil, the oil gets thinner and flows better, and it interferes less with heat transfer in the coils. Oil that does not mix with the refrigerant can pile up in heat exchangers, and BITZER says it can even stop a system from running. The word for mixing is miscibility: a miscible oil stays blended with the liquid refrigerant and gets carried along with it.

Which oil goes with which refrigerant

BITZER sorts the families this way. Mineral oil and alkylbenzene suit CFC and HCFC refrigerants such as R-22, but they mix poorly with HFC and HFO refrigerants. Polyol ester (POE) was developed for HFC and HFO systems and is the usual choice across most of the refrigeration industry. Polyvinyl ether (PVE) is increasingly used in factory-built air conditioners and chillers. Polyalkylene glycol (PAG) shows up mainly in vehicle air conditioning and some CO2 equipment, so it is outside this lesson.

Copeland's (Emerson) approved-lubricant table follows the same pattern. It lists mineral oil or alkylbenzene for R-12 and R-22, and POE for HFCs such as R-134a, R-404A, R-407C and R-410A. For R-22 it shows POE as the preferred alternative, and it names the specific mineral and alkylbenzene products Copeland recognizes. For A2L equipment, use the oil the equipment and compressor maker name; our R-454B and R-32 lesson covers what changes at the gauges. BITZER adds that HFOs react more readily with oil, which is one reason to use only the oil the compressor maker specifies.

Why POE gets special handling

POE is hygroscopic, which means it pulls water out of the air. A trade-press article on POEs says they hold moisture much tighter than the mineral oils they replaced, and that uptake depends on temperature, humidity, exposure time and surface area. A Copeland oil filter-drier sheet puts a manufacturer number on it: POE absorbs up to 20 times more moisture than ordinary mineral oils. Treat that as a maker claim, not a lab result.

Moisture matters for two reasons. First, water and heat can split a POE molecule back into acid and alcohol, a reaction called hydrolysis. The same article says this needs high temperature and quite a lot of water, and is uncommon in practice, but it is the reason for the moisture limits. Second, acid can plate copper onto steel parts. Danfoss says excess air and moisture raise condensing pressure and discharge temperature and form acids that cause copper plating. BITZER notes that PVE also takes up water, even more than POE, but resists hydrolysis well. Copeland's comparison above puts mineral oil well below POE for water uptake.

The habits that follow are simple:

  • Keep POE in its original sealed container and close it right after pouring. The trade-press handling article says to store it in the original container because many plastics let moisture through.
  • Keep the compressor and system closed except while you are working. Danfoss's guide for its VZH scroll compressors says an opened compressor must not be exposed to air for more than 20 minutes.
  • Use a liquid-line drier sized for the system, and replace it when you open the circuit.
  • Evacuate to the maker's level before charging. See Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics.

For scale, BITZER says experience with POE is positive when water content stays at or below about 100 ppm. Danfoss says its VZH compressors ship with less than 100 ppm moisture. Those are example figures from two makers, so check the number for your equipment.

After a burnout: acid test

When a hermetic motor burns, acid and sludge spread through the system. Sporlan's burnout cleanup guidance says its TA-1 and AK-3 acid test kits can test mineral, alkylbenzene or POE oil. The TA-1 gives a one-time pass or fail and the AK-3 can be reused and reads the acid content. Sporlan says to take a second oil sample after 24 hours of running and test it again, and to consider severe cases as needing a suction-line filter-drier as well. It says the cores may need to be changed several times. Follow the kit's own instructions for reading the result. The acceptable limits are not published in the document we read.

Retrofits and oil changes

If R-22 equipment is converted to an HFC blend, the old mineral oil has to be mostly replaced. Maker guidance varies, so use the compressor maker's retrofit bulletin first. Emerson's approved-lubricant table points to Copeland changeover documents. The Copeland changeover guidance we read says the maximum residual mineral oil should be 5 percent, and 1 percent for systems with enhanced-surface heat exchangers. For semi-hermetic compressors it says to repeat the oil change with ester oil, with the old refrigerant still in the system, until under 5 percent mineral oil remains, then run at least 30 minutes under steady conditions so oil returns, and re-test. The refrigerant is changed only after the oil work is done. The trade-press handling article gives the same 5 percent by volume figure.

Other makers differ. Honeywell's R-407C and R-422D retrofit guide gives no residual percentage. It says to drain the lubricant, measure how much came out, replace it with the same volume of POE, and states that R-407C needs at least 15 percent POE. It also says to change the filter-drier and consider a suction filter. That is why the document for your compressor and your refrigerant is the one to follow. Chemours points technicians with questions about its Opteon products to its Tech2Tech support line.

Worked example: how many oil changes?

This is a simplified model, built to show the arithmetic with an assumed system, not a procedure. Suppose a system holds 4.0 L of mineral oil. Each change drains 3.0 L of the mixed oil and refills 3.0 L of POE. Assume the oil blends fully before each drain (which is why the run time matters). The fraction drained is 3.0 / 4.0 = 0.75, so 0.25 of the oil survives each change.

  • Change 1: 4.0 L × 0.25 = 1.0 L mineral, which is 1.0 / 4.0 = 25 percent.
  • Change 2: 1.0 L × 0.25 = 0.25 L, which is 0.25 / 4.0 = 6.25 percent. Still above 5.
  • Change 3: 0.25 L × 0.25 = 0.0625 L, which is 0.0625 / 4.0 = about 1.6 percent. Under 5 percent, so this meets the 5 percent limit.
  • Change 4: 0.0625 L × 0.25 = about 0.016 L, or about 0.4 percent. A system with a 1 percent limit would need this fourth change.

Real systems hold oil in places a drain does not reach, so Copeland says to re-test the oil after restart and repeat the change if it is still above the limit.

Always use the maker's oil

Oil grade is set by the compressor manufacturer. Viscosity and brand matter. The trade-press handling article advises confirming the POE grade with the compressor or equipment maker before adding or changing oil. Do not mix oil types or add anything to the oil unless the compressor maker allows it. Related reading: Compressor Basics and Failure Modes.

Field card: Refrigerant Oils

Oil must mix, stay dry and match the maker.

  • Oil travels with the refrigerant, so it must be miscible with it.
  • Mineral and alkylbenzene go with CFC and HCFC such as R-22. POE (and PVE in some equipment) goes with HFC, HFO and A2L.
  • POE soaks up moisture. Keep it sealed, keep systems closed, evacuate, change the drier.
  • After a burnout, test the oil for acid with the kit and follow the kit instructions.
  • Retrofit example from one maker: under 5 percent mineral oil left. Use your maker's number.
  • Use the compressor maker's oil. Do not mix oils.
