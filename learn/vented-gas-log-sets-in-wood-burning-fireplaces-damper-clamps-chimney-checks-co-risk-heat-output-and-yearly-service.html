Vented Gas Log Sets in Wood-Burning Fireplaces

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A vented gas log set turns an old wood fireplace into a gas fire without changing the chimney. That makes it the cheapest gas conversion, and also the one that depends most on the chimney being clear and the damper staying open. This lesson covers how the set works, the damper rule, carbon monoxide, how little heat you really get, and what to check every year.

Check the manual and the local code

Your documents rule. This page is general education, not an inspection. Follow the installation manual for your exact log set and burner, and ask your local code office what it requires. We read a model code inspection procedure, several city handouts and one log maker's manual, listed under Sources. We did not read the full text of the National Fuel Gas Code (NFPA 54) or the International Fuel Gas Code, so we say which page described each rule.

How a vented log set differs from the other gas fireplaces

A vented set sits in the fire chamber of a fireplace built for wood. The flame is mainly for looks, and the combustion gases are meant to rise up the existing chimney. The Department of Energy (DOE) describes the listing standards this way: ANSI Z21.60 covers decorative appliances for solid-fuel fireplaces, including gas logs, which it defines as an open-flame appliance with a frame or base holding simulated logs. ANSI Z21.84 covers the manually lighted version. ANSI Z21.50 covers vented decorative appliances in general, such as gas fireplace inserts and direct-vent units.

Two other kinds are easy to mix up with it. A direct-vent unit is sealed and has its own pipe; a vent-free log set has no flue at all and puts its exhaust into the room. Our gas fireplaces lesson compares those. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) says decorative gas logs are not designed to be a significant source of heat and that it does not support log models without outside venting because of indoor air quality. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) tells people never to burn anything in a fireplace that is not vented. For this lesson, the key idea is simple: with a vented set, the chimney is part of the appliance.

Why the damper must be fixed open

A wood fireplace damper is a plate that can close the flue. If it closes while gas logs burn, the exhaust, including carbon monoxide (CO), comes into the room. The log maker's manual we read says the damper must be fully open when the set burns, and it describes a damper clamp that keeps a minimum permanent opening. The minimum depends on chimney height and the set's Btu rating, from a table in the manual. If the clamp does not fit or does not give the opening, the manual tells you to have a permanent stop installed or the damper blade removed or cut.

The model fuel gas code backs this up. Appendix D of the International Fuel Gas Code is a recommended procedure for inspecting an existing appliance installation. As reproduced by one city, it tells the inspector, for gas logs in a wood-burning fireplace with the appliance off, to verify that the damper is in a fixed open position. City handouts put it in plain words: one says the damper must be removed or mechanically locked open, another says removed or welded open, and a third says only that it must be blocked part way open. Those differ, so the lesson for a tech is to follow the log maker's table and the local inspector. Never rely on someone remembering to open the damper.

Chimney condition and sweeping before conversion

The log maker's manual requires the chimney to be free of obstructions or restrictions. The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) says to have chimneys and flues checked for blockages, corrosion and disconnections, and the CDC says to have chimneys checked or cleaned every year because debris can let CO build up indoors. NFPA 211 asks for a yearly chimney inspection, as covered in our chimney liner lesson. Before a conversion, have the flue swept and inspected, and look for cracked tile, a missing cap, nests and a stuck or loose damper. If the flue is damaged, a liner may be the fix, and a permit may be needed. A Minnesota city handout requires a mechanical permit with a gas line pressure test and inspections for the conversion.

Carbon monoxide: the main risk

The CDC says more than 400 Americans die each year from unintentional CO poisoning not linked to fires, more than 100,000 visit an emergency department and more than 14,000 are hospitalized. It lists fireplaces among the sources. The log maker's manual says that if fumes or soot show up in the room with the damper fully open, the draft is defective, and you should not run the logs until it is fixed.

The model code inspection procedure measures CO after five minutes of burner operation. For logs in a wood-burning fireplace, its limit is 400 ppm air free in the firebox. Use the limit in your own code edition and the maker's manual. CPSC recommends CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, and not directly above fuel-burning appliances. The CDC says to replace a detector by the maker's instructions or every 5 years. An alarm is a backup. See our gas smell lesson for the gas side of an emergency.

Btu input and where the heat goes

Log sets are rated by input in Btu per hour. DOE modeling assumed 35,000 Btu/h for a decorative fireplace burner and 1,000 Btu/h for a standing pilot, and says ANSI Z21.60 covers inputs up to 400,000 Btu/h and ANSI Z21.84 up to 90,000. An open flame in an open fireplace sends most of its heat up the flue with the exhaust. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory survey of hearth products says its authors are not aware of reliable efficiency estimates for fireplaces, so we do not quote one as fact. In the example below the efficiency is our assumption.

Worked example: a 60,000 Btu/h log set versus a furnace

Our own example, with assumptions. We assume the set burns natural gas at 60,000 Btu/h input, that 20 percent of the heat reaches the room (the rest goes up the chimney), that gas costs $1.60 per therm, and that a furnace is 95 percent efficient. A therm is 100,000 Btu. None of these three inputs is a measured value; use your own bills and ratings.

  1. Gas burned: 60,000 ÷ 100,000 = 0.6 therm per hour.
  2. Cost per hour: 0.6 × $1.60 = $0.96.
  3. Heat to the room from the logs: 60,000 × 0.20 = 12,000 Btu/h.
  4. A furnace needs input of 12,000 ÷ 0.95 = 12,632 Btu/h to deliver the same heat. That is 0.126 therm, or about $0.20 per hour.
  5. Per million Btu delivered: $1.60 × 10 therms ÷ 0.20 = $80.00 for the logs, against $16.00 ÷ 0.95 = $16.84 for the furnace.

With these assumptions the log set costs about 4.75 times as much per unit of heat delivered (0.95 ÷ 0.20). That ratio shrinks only if the real efficiency is higher than our guess. The pilot adds to it: DOE's survey figures say 71 percent of hearth products use a standing pilot. For fuel price math across fuels, see comparing heating fuel costs.

Gas line, shutoff valve, pilot and doors

  • Shutoff valve. Two city handouts we read want a valve outside the firebox, one within 3 feet of it and another within 6 feet, in the same room. Local rules differ, so ask your inspector. The gas line is pressure tested; one city requires 25 PSIG for 24 hours.
  • Pilot and safety. The model code language reproduced by one city requires a flame safeguard that automatically shuts off fuel to the main burner when the pilot or ignition fails. The log maker's manual describes a pilot flame that must touch the flame sensor, thermopile or thermocouple. See pilot light lessons for how these work.
  • Glass doors and screens. The log maker's manual says a screen must be in place while burning and that glass doors must be open while the set burns. Another city's handout for sealed gas fireplaces (a different product) requires the glass to be sealed. Do not mix the two.

Yearly checks

The CPSC and CDC both advise a yearly professional inspection of heating equipment and chimneys. For a log set, a tech can check:

  • Soot. The maker's manual says logs soot when burned, but improper placement increases it. Follow the placement diagram in the box. If soot is heavy, rub it off gently and check log placement and the flame before blaming the gas.
  • Damper stop. Confirm the clamp or stop is still in place and the opening matches the maker's table.
  • Pilot assembly. The manual says to examine it periodically: flame, sensor contact and clear air.
  • Burner ports. The manual we read did not discuss them. Look for clogged or damaged ports, and use the factory orifice only.
  • Flue and CO. Sweep, inspect, test draft and CO, and check alarms.

Field card: Vented Gas Log Sets

The chimney is part of the appliance.

  • Vented set: exhaust goes up the existing chimney. Vent-free: no flue.
  • Damper fixed open with a clamp or stop; the model fuel gas code inspection procedure checks for this.
  • Sweep and inspect the chimney before conversion and each year.
  • Fumes in the room with the damper open: stop, fix the draft.
  • CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas.
  • Most of the heat goes up the flue. Our 60,000 Btu/h example assumes 20 percent reaches the room.
  • Shutoff valve outside the firebox; check local distance rules.
