Protecting Low-Slope Roofs During Rooftop HVAC Service

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A roof membrane is the only thing between the rain and the building, and a service call can damage it in seconds. A dropped screw, a sharp panel edge or a ladder foot can open a hole you never notice, and the leak shows up weeks later in a ceiling below. This lesson covers how to protect a low-slope roof while you work on rooftop equipment, and the fall hazards you pass on the way.

How a membrane gets damaged

Low-slope roofs are covered by a thin waterproof layer, either a single-ply sheet or a built-up or modified bitumen system made of layered felts or sheets. They are tougher than they look under steady walking, but weak against small, hard, concentrated loads. Our own reasoning, from how pressure works: a load spread over a boot sole is mild, and the same load on a screw tip or a ladder foot is not.

  • Dropped screws and fasteners. A screw left on the roof gets stepped on, rolled over by a cart wheel, or pressed in by a panel set on top of it.
  • Sharp sheet metal. Cut edges on access panels, duct pieces and curb flashing scrape and slice a membrane when dragged or set down.
  • Ladders and stands. A ladder foot or a stand leg carries a point load on a small patch of membrane.
  • Heavy parts. A motor, compressor or coil set down on bare membrane can crush the roof insulation below and leave a depression that holds water. A federal military research report on modified bitumen roofs lists equipment set directly on the roof without a protective pad as an improper support, and rates membrane damage from it as high severity.

The same report (see the Sources) is about modified bitumen, not every roof type, so treat it as a clear example rather than a rule for all membranes.

Oil and solvents on the roof

Compressor oil, refrigerant oil drips and some cleaning solvents are hard on bitumen roofing. The report above rates grease, solvent or oil drippings on a membrane as medium severity when the membrane is not yet degraded, and high severity once it is. We could not confirm how a given single-ply sheet reacts to a given oil, so we do not give a chemical chart here. The safe habit is simple: set a drip pan or absorbent mat under work that can drip, wipe up spills at once, and carry used oil and rags off the roof.

Walkway pads, plywood and padding

A university facilities design standard for single-ply roofs asks for walkway pads that are at least 30 by 30 inches, matched to the membrane, around roof-mounted mechanical equipment and along the path from each roof access point to that equipment. It also calls for a cover board or a thick rubber pad under the pads to protect the insulation, and a thicker membrane (0.090 inch rather than 0.060 inch) in traffic areas. Those are one owner's requirements, not a universal code, but they show what a roofing owner expects.

When no pad exists where you must work, use plywood set on a layer of soft padding, and think about the load. Plywood spreads your weight and a tool bag or ladder foot, but it can also slide on a wet membrane, so keep the edges clear of seams and flashing. Never drag a heavy part across the roof; lift it, or slide it on a protective sheet. The same military report tells inspectors not to step on blisters or edge flashing, and that applies to us too.

Screw trays, filter boxes and debris

  • Keep a magnet tray. Put a magnetic tray or a bowl at your work spot and put every screw in it as it comes out. Sweep the area with a magnet before you leave. See the lesson on dropped screws and parts for the same habit inside equipment.
  • Bag the old filters. Old filters, filter boxes, packing and cut insulation blow across a roof and wash into drains. Bag them and take them down.
  • Check the nearest drain. The same report lists a clogged drain as high severity, notes that clogged drains and obstructions cause ponding, and counts water still standing more than 48 hours after rain as ponding. Loose debris left on the membrane is rated medium. If a drain near your unit is blocked, tell the building owner or roof contact rather than clearing a drain you have not been told to clear.

Report damage, do not hide it

If you drop a tool, tear a membrane or spill oil, report it the same day to your lead and the building owner, with the location and a photo. This is our own advice: a small fresh puncture is a cheap repair, and a hidden one lets water travel through insulation and show up far from the hole. A report also protects you, because it separates a puncture you made today from one that was already there. Hiding damage costs the owner more and puts your name on the next leak.

Skylights and roof edges along the path

Look at the whole walk from the access point to the unit before you carry anything. A federal workplace safety research publication on falls through skylights and roof openings says falls through skylights are a recurring cause of deaths, that skylights can be hidden under snow, and that workers should never sit, lean on or step on a skylight or a cover over a hole. It cites at least 200 pounds as the load a skylight screen must withstand, with screen openings no more than 4 inches long or 2 inches wide, and says a person falling against a skylight can send an estimated 400 to 500 pounds of force into it.

The federal construction fall protection rule sets the protection trigger for unprotected sides and edges at 6 feet or more above a lower level. For holes, which include skylights, it requires fall arrest, covers or guardrails when the hole is more than 6 feet above a lower level. For roofing work on low-slope roofs it allows guardrails, nets, fall arrest, or a warning line combined with another system, and a safety monitor alone only on roofs 50 feet or less in width. Your site may set stricter rules, so follow them. Cold and wet conditions make all of this worse; see the lesson on winter roofs.

Worked example (our own): a half-blocked drain

This is our own example. Every input below is an assumption chosen to show the arithmetic, not a measurement and not a design check.

Assumptions: one drain serves 2,500 square feet of roof; the drain can pass 100 gallons per minute when clear; debris blocks 60 percent of the opening; flow capacity falls in proportion to the open area (a simplification); the rain falls at 3 inches per hour for 30 minutes; the ponded water spreads over 1,000 square feet; water weighs 62.4 pounds per cubic foot; and 1 cubic foot is 7.48 gallons.

  1. Drain capacity left: 100 × (1 − 0.60) = 40 gallons per minute.
  2. Rain arriving: 2,500 ft² × (3 ÷ 12) ft per hour = 625 cubic feet per hour. At 7.48 gallons per cubic foot that is 4,675 gallons per hour, or 77.9 gallons per minute.
  3. Water that cannot leave: 77.9 − 40 = 37.9 gallons per minute. Over 30 minutes that is 37.9 × 30 = 1,137 gallons, or 1,137 ÷ 7.48 = 152 cubic feet.
  4. Depth over 1,000 ft²: 152 ÷ 1,000 = 0.152 foot, about 1.8 inches.
  5. Weight per square foot: 62.4 × 0.152 = 9.5 pounds per square foot. Check: 1 inch of water is 62.4 ÷ 12 = 5.2 pounds per square foot, and 5.2 × 1.82 inches = 9.5.
  6. Total ponded weight: 152 ft³ × 62.4 = about 9,500 pounds.

So a half-blocked drain in a modest storm puts roughly 9,500 pounds of water on a small area, and the depth keeps growing while the rain continues. We have not checked this against any roof's load rating; the roof designer and owner decide that. The point for a technician is that a bag of filter debris can matter, and that standing water at a drain should be reported.

Field card: Protecting Low-Slope Roofs During Rooftop HVAC Service

Walk it, pad it, catch it, clear it, report it.

  • Walk the path first: note skylights, edges and wet or snowy spots.
  • Pad or board the route and the work spot; never set parts on bare membrane.
  • Use a magnet tray; sweep with a magnet before you leave.
  • Keep oil and solvents off the membrane; use a drip pan and wipe spills.
  • Bag filters and debris; report a blocked drain.
  • One inch of ponded water weighs about 5.2 lb per square foot.
  • Report any damage the same day with a photo.
