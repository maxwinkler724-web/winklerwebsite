Condenser Whip Conduit: Liquidtight Flex, Fittings and Grounding

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The short run of flexible conduit between the outdoor disconnect and the condenser or heat pump is called the whip. It sits in rain, sun and mower range, and it is often the first part of the circuit to fail. This lesson covers what the whip is made of, how it is supported and terminated, and how to check an old one.

Check the local rules. The sources below describe the National Electrical Code as inspectors apply it. Your area may adopt a different edition or add amendments, so confirm with the local inspection office before you rely on anything here. This is general education, not legal advice.

What a whip is for

The disconnect is mounted on the wall or a stand within sight of the equipment. A St. Paul, Minnesota inspection checklist quotes the rule in section 440.14 that the disconnecting means be installed within sight of the air conditioning equipment. The whip is the flexible link from that box to the unit's field-wiring compartment, so the unit can be pulled for service without rebuilding rigid pipe. For sizing the disconnect itself, see AC Condenser Disconnect Switches, Fuses and Breakers, and for the clear space in front of it see Working Space and Service Clearance. We do not repeat either one here.

Which flexible conduit is allowed outdoors

Ordinary flexible metal conduit, the spiral type, is the wrong choice outdoors. The St. Paul checklist quotes section 348.12 as saying it shall not be used in a wet location, and a California city inspection guide says the same, adding that liquidtight flexible metal conduit is the approved option for wet locations. The St. Paul checklist also groups liquidtight flexible nonmetallic conduit with the metal type for wet-location use and gives section 356.30 for its fastening.

  • Liquidtight flexible metal conduit has a metal core with a plastic jacket. It stands up better to a stray foot or trimmer swing and is common on condenser whips.
  • Liquidtight flexible nonmetallic conduit is all plastic. It is light and does not rust, but a trimmer line cuts it more easily, and its support spacing is closer (see below).

Pick one type, then use fittings made for that exact type. A metal-core connector will not seal on a nonmetallic jacket, and the reverse is also true. The fitting must also be rated for wet locations, because the St. Paul checklist states that the interior of a raceway in a wet location counts as a wet location. Water that gets past a bad fitting wets the conductors inside.

Support and length

A Maine licensing board violations sheet quotes section 350.30 for liquidtight metal conduit: secure it within 12 inches of each box, cabinet or fitting, and support it every 4.5 feet. An Oregon state building-code study guide lists the same 12 inches and 54 inches for liquidtight flexible metal conduit. For liquidtight flexible nonmetallic conduit the Oregon table shows 12 inches at the termination and 3 feet between supports. The St. Paul checklist asks for the strap within 12 inches of the air conditioner disconnect.

Most whips are only a few feet long, so in practice that means one strap near the disconnect and a well-seated fitting at the unit. We did not find a sourced overall maximum length for the whip in the pages we read, so this lesson gives no number. Ask the local inspector how they read length for your type of conduit.

Straight or 90 degree connectors

This is our own field practice, not a code rule. Use a straight connector where the conduit leaves the bottom of the disconnect and runs straight down, and a 90 degree connector where the whip must turn to enter a unit panel from the side. A 90 degree fitting lets the conduit approach without a tight bend at the jacket, and it keeps the weight of the conduit from pulling on the fitting. Whichever type you pick, tighten the locknut and the sealing gland so the jacket is gripped evenly.

Sealing the ends

Both ends need a wet-location fitting, a bushing or insulating throat where the conductors pass through, and every unused opening closed. The Maine sheet notes that unused knockouts in boxes and enclosures must be closed with approved seals or plugs. The same sheet explains why raceways that cross between different temperatures are sealed: moist air moving through the pipe condenses and corrodes the inside. A whip that runs from a warm wall to a cool outdoor box can do the same thing, so a sealed fitting matters.

Grounding: put a wire in the whip

We run a green or bare equipment grounding conductor inside every whip, from the disconnect's ground bar to the unit's ground terminal, and we do not rely on the metal jacket as the ground path. The St. Paul checklist states that the equipment grounding conductor is sized from Table 250.122. Federal workplace rules, 29 CFR 1910.305(a)(1)(i), say metal raceways and enclosures shall be effectively bonded where necessary to ensure electrical continuity. The conduit still has to be bonded through its fittings, but a conductor is the dependable path. See Equipment Grounding and GFCI for HVAC Equipment for the wire sizing and the neutral-versus-ground rules.

Conductor type in a wet location

A university agricultural engineering tech note explains that a W in an insulation code marks wire permitted in damp or wet locations, and that each H adds 15 degrees C to a 60 degree C base. Common dual-marked THHN/THWN wire fits that description. The St. Paul checklist also says nonmetallic-sheathed cable is not permitted in a raceway outside, so house-wiring cable is the wrong thing to push through an outdoor whip. We pull individual conductors marked for wet locations, sized from the unit nameplate.

Slack, grade and mower damage

Leave enough slack that the unit can be moved a few inches for service, but let the extra length hang in a gentle curve, not a coil resting on the ground. A loop on the soil collects water and debris, gets stepped on, and sits right where a string trimmer works. Our own habits for protecting a whip: keep it close to the wall or equipment pad, route it behind the unit or a guard where the lawn crew does not cut, and tell the homeowner where the conduit runs. Do not bury it or hide it in ground cover.

Our own worked example: conduit fill check

These are our own assumptions, not from a particular job. We run a whip with three conductors: two 8 AWG THHN/THWN conductors for the line and one 10 AWG THHN/THWN equipment grounding conductor, all in 3/4 trade size conduit.

  1. Rule: a state electrical examination guide works an example using Chapter 9, Table 1, and says 40 percent of the conduit cross-sectional area may be used for conductor fill when the conduit holds over 2 conductors. Our whip has three, so 40 percent applies.
  2. Conductor areas: a university engineering page lists, for THHN/THWN in square inches, 0.0366 for 8 AWG and 0.0211 for 10 AWG. That page reproduces an older code edition, so check the current Chapter 9, Table 5 for your wire.
  3. Total conductor area: 2 × 0.0366 = 0.0732; add 0.0211 to get 0.0943 square inches.
  4. Conduit area: the same page lists 0.533 square inches for a 3/4 trade size tube. Liquidtight conduit has its own row in Chapter 9, Table 4, so use the row for your actual conduit type on a job.
  5. Fill percent: 0.0943 ÷ 0.533 = 0.1769, or 17.7 percent.
  6. Compare: 40 percent of 0.533 is 0.2132 square inches. At 17.7 percent the fill is well under the limit, so the wires pull with room to spare.

Fill is only one check. Voltage drop on a long run is separate; see Voltage Drop on HVAC Circuits.

Inspecting an old whip

De-energize first. The line side of the disconnect stays live with the handle off, so shut off the breaker at the panel and confirm with a meter. Then look and feel:

  • Cracked or chalky jacket. Sunlight breaks plastic down. Cracks, a chalky surface or exposed metal mean replace the whip.
  • Pulled fittings. Pull gently on the conduit at each end. A fitting that moves, or a jacket that slid out of the gland, is a fail.
  • Rust and green corrosion at the fitting or terminals, and wet or discolored wire inside.
  • Loose terminals. A California city guide says terminals are torqued to the listed values during inspection. Burned or loose lugs are covered in Loose Electrical Connections in HVAC Equipment.
  • Cuts and nicks from a trimmer, and a flattened section from a foot or a ladder leg.

Replace the whole whip rather than patching the jacket with tape. Tape does not restore a wet-location rating.

Field card: Condenser whip conduit

Wet-location conduit, wet-location fittings, a ground wire in the whip.

  • Standard flexible metal conduit is not for wet locations; use a liquidtight type.
  • Strap within 12 in. of the box, then every 4.5 ft (metal) or 3 ft (nonmetallic) per the sources above.
  • Match fittings to the conduit type and close every unused opening.
  • Run a grounding conductor in the whip; do not rely on the jacket.
  • Use conductors marked W for wet locations.
  • Fill: over 2 conductors means 40 percent of the conduit area at most.
  • Keep slack in a gentle curve above grade, away from the mower line.
