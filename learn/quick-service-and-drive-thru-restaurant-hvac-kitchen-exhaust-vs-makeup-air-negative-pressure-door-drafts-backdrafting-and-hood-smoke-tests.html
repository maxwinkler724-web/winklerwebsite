Quick-Service and Drive-Thru Restaurant HVAC: Exhaust, Makeup Air and Negative Pressure

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A fast-food building is small, tight and busy, and it has one very large fan pulling air out over the fryers. If the air coming back in is short by even a few hundred cfm, the building pays the difference through the doors, the drive-thru window and the water heater flue. This lesson shows where the air goes and how to prove it with a gauge and a smoke test.

Three air streams that have to balance

Think of the building as a box. Air leaves through the hood exhaust fan. Air enters through the makeup air unit, through the outdoor air section of the rooftop unit (RTU), and through leaks. Whatever the first two do not cover, the leaks cover. The hood basics are in Commercial Kitchen Ventilation: Hoods and Make-Up Air Basics, and the makeup unit itself is in Make-Up Air Units. Here we focus on the whole-building result.

What a negative building feels like

  • Hard doors. The front door pulls shut or has to be pushed open against suction, and it may whistle at the edge.
  • Cold or hot streams at the drive-thru window and front door. Outdoor air enters at the easiest openings, and a service window that opens many times an hour is one of them.
  • Backdrafting flues. A natural-draft water heater or heater flue can spill exhaust into the room when the room is lower in pressure than the flue outlet.
  • Fryer odors in the dining room. A starved hood captures less, so smoke and grease vapor escape the hood edge and drift out of the kitchen.
  • Weak cooling and heating. Unconditioned leakage air lands on a space that the RTU is trying to hold at setpoint.

A federal lab literature review of combustion appliance venting notes that tighter buildings depressurize more readily when exhaust equipment runs, which makes naturally vented appliances more likely to spill. The same review warns that the common stress tests are not reliable predictors on their own. A Department of Energy training sheet lists worst-case depressurization as a standard combustion-safety test. Both are written for houses, but the physics is the same in a restaurant: a big exhaust fan against a tight shell. See Worst-Case Depressurization and Spillage Testing for the method.

Why the drive-thru window is the weak spot

A national lab study of entrance infiltration assumed that half of the customers at a quick-service restaurant use the drive-through, which it turned into a peak of 90 door openings an hour. Its model put peak entrance infiltration for a quick-service building at 1,913 cfm without a vestibule and 1,237 cfm with one, for a 21 square foot door. Those are modeled values for automatic doors, and the authors say they may overstate buildings with manual doors, so treat them as a sense of scale, not a prediction. The point is that an entrance that opens constantly is already a big hole. A building pulled negative by an exhaust shortfall makes it worse, because the pressure difference drives air in whenever the window slides open.

Two devices fight the draft. A window air curtain blows a thin sheet of air across the opening, which works only while the building is not strongly negative (see Air Curtains Over Commercial Doors). Many window units also include a small electric or hot-water heater, or a heated sill, to keep staff warm. Neither fixes an air balance problem; they only soften the symptom. If the draft returns when the exhaust fan is switched on and disappears when it is off, the cause is balance, not the heater.

Our own worked example: a 400 cfm shortfall

All numbers here are our own assumptions for a small quick-service building, not measurements or code values.

  1. Air out: hood exhaust = 4,000 cfm.
  2. Designed air in: makeup air 3,000 cfm + RTU outdoor air 600 cfm = 3,600 cfm.
  3. Net shortfall: 4,000 − 3,600 = 400 cfm, which is 10 percent of the exhaust. That air enters as leakage.
  4. Bad day: if the RTU outdoor air damper is closed (an economizer in a cooling lockout, say) and a loaded makeup air filter drops that unit to 2,500 cfm, the shortfall becomes 4,000 − 2,500 = 1,500 cfm.

What 400 cfm does to the pressure. Leakage follows a power-law relation, so the pressure depends on how tight the shell is. We assume 3,000 ft² of exterior envelope, a flow exponent of 0.65 and a leakage rating at 75 Pa (the lab study above used 1.8 cfm per ft² at 75 Pa as a baseline for a quick-service model).

  • Leakage required: 400 ÷ 3,000 = 0.133 cfm per ft².
  • Leaky shell rated 1.8 at 75 Pa: pressure = 75 × (0.133 ÷ 1.8)1/0.65 = about 1.4 Pa (0.006 in. w.c.). Barely noticeable.
  • Tight shell rated 0.25 at 75 Pa (our assumption): pressure = 75 × (0.133 ÷ 0.25)1/0.65 = about 28 Pa (0.11 in. w.c.). That is a strong suction.
  • Door force: 28 Pa is about 0.6 pounds per ft². On a 21 ft² door that is roughly 12.5 lbf of pressure force, and because it acts about mid-door, the extra pull at the handle is roughly 6 lbf. At 1.4 Pa the same math gives under 1 lbf, so nobody notices.
  • Energy: 400 cfm × 1.08 × 40°F outdoor-to-room difference = 17,280 Btu/h of untreated load, where 1.08 is 0.075 lb/ft³ × 0.24 Btu/lb·°F × 60 min/h.

Takeaway: the same 400 cfm gap is invisible in a leaky building and a door fight in a tight new one. That is why two stores with identical equipment can complain differently.

Rooftop units over the kitchen

An RTU sited near the kitchen exhaust or its upblast fan can pull greasy air into its outdoor air path. Grease on filters and the coil face cuts airflow and heat transfer, and the unit then supplies less air to a dining room that was already short of makeup air. Keep intakes clear of exhaust discharge, check the filters and coil face at every visit, and clean coils with a method the maker allows. Exhaust fan cleaning and hinge kits are covered in Restaurant Kitchen Exhaust Fans, and general unit care in Rooftop Unit Basics.

Service checks

  1. Look first. Is the makeup air unit running whenever the hood fan runs? Check its interlock, filters, dampers and belts. Is the RTU outdoor air damper actually opening?
  2. Pressure across the door. With a digital manometer, put the reference hose outdoors out of the wind and the other hose inside, then read with the hood fan on high and the RTU running. Record inches of water or pascals, and note the weather. Compare it with the building's design drawings; the drawings or the adopted code set the target, not us.
  3. Hood capture smoke test. Hold a smoke source at the cooking surface with the equipment as it normally runs and watch whether smoke rises into the hood. Ask the manager first, keep smoke away from detectors and the fire suppression system, and never use open flame near grease. Repeat with the makeup air unit switched off, and note the difference.
  4. Flue check. With every exhaust fan running, check draft at each natural-draft appliance using its maker's instructions. If any spills, stop and tell the owner; this is a safety item, not a comfort item.
  5. Fix the cause, not the number. Do not slow the exhaust fan or force dampers without the hood listing and the engineer's paperwork. Lock out and tag equipment before opening it, and follow roof access rules.

Field card: Quick-Service and Drive-Thru Restaurant HVAC: Exhaust, Makeup Air and Negative Pressure

Air out must equal air in, and the leaks fill any gap.

  • Balance: exhaust out, makeup air plus RTU outdoor air in, leakage for the rest.
  • Our example: 4,000 − (3,000 + 600) = 400 cfm shortfall.
  • Same gap: about 1.4 Pa in a leaky shell, about 28 Pa in a tight one (our assumptions).
  • Symptoms: hard doors, drive-thru drafts, backdrafting flues, odors in the dining room.
  • Checks: makeup unit and interlock, RTU damper, pressure across the door, hood smoke test, flue draft.
  • Stop and report any flue spillage.
