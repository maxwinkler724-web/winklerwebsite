Valve Stem Packing Leaks on Plant Valves: Snug, Repack or Replace

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A damp stem on a plant valve is one of the most common leaks in a mechanical room. It is also one of the easiest to make worse. This lesson covers what packing does, how to snug it correctly, when repacking is the right fix, and when the valve itself should go.

What packing does

A valve stem has to pass from the pressurized valve body to the outside so a handwheel or actuator can turn it. Packing is the soft sealing material wrapped around the stem inside a chamber at the top of the bonnet, often called the stuffing box. A gland follower presses down on the packing, and packing nuts (or bolts) on studs set how hard. Squeezed from above, the packing swells sideways and seals against both the stem and the chamber wall.

A federal leak-detection guide for process plants says valve leaks usually occur at the stem or gland area, typically from packing failure, and names seal wear or improper maintenance as the major cause. A federal illustrated leak guide gives the same cause for shut-off valves: packing that has compacted with age and use. A federal facility O&M guide for boilers puts it on the inspection list: check packing glands for compression leakage. A university construction standard for steam piping asks for packing free of asbestos fibers and selected for the pressure and temperature of the valve, and says that if packing shows hardening or stem corrosion at a one-year check, the valves are to be repacked.

Snug the nuts evenly, a little at a time

The first fix for a stem weep is a small adjustment, and a federal leak guide lists tightening the packing gland nuts as a first repair attempt. A federal facilities document on a related packing repair says to check that the gland sits square and not cocked, and to raise tightness in steps. Our practical reading of that guidance: turn each nut a small fraction of a turn in alternating order so the follower goes down level, then wait and watch before turning again. A cocked follower presses one side of the packing and the stem, and it can score the stem.

Do not chase a leak with a big pull on one nut. Stop when the weep stops. Do not go further for luck.

Why overtightening backfires

A federal facilities document warns that overtightening gland bolts compresses packing to a point of ineffectiveness, and that overtightened gland bolts could lead to bolt failure and gland rupture. A federal nuclear regulator document on packing adjustment says adjustment must stay within the maker's specified tolerance and must not deform the stem, and notes it can affect how the closure member meets its seat. In our own words: a squeezed packing grips the stem, so the valve gets harder to turn and the stem wears faster where it rubs. That friction wear is what starts the next leak, so a valve that was once snugged hard often leaks again sooner, not later.

Repacking: line isolated and depressurized

When the nuts have no travel left, or the packing is hard, repacking is the repair. Our order of work, built on the lockout rules and the repacking steps in the federal documents:

  1. Isolate and release stored energy. The federal workplace-safety lockout standard (29 CFR 1910.147) calls for written procedures, operating the isolating devices, releasing or securing stored or residual energy, and verifying isolation before work begins. See Lockout, Tagout and Verify Dead for HVAC. A valve you are repacking can be the very valve that holds back the system, so isolate upstream and downstream of it, with the line drained and depressurized.
  2. Open the box. The federal facilities repacking steps say to remove the gland, old packing and debris from the packing box.
  3. Fit new rings. The same document says to cut each ring to size and tamp each ring in individually, and to stagger the ring seams at 90 degrees. Use the packing type the valve maker or the plant specification calls for.
  4. Snug and test. Seat the follower square, bring the nuts up evenly, then return the valve to service and check for a weep.

Live loading, where a valve has it, is a spring-assisted way to keep gland load up as packing settles. Follow the valve's own documentation for it, and do not add it on your own.

Hot-water and steam burn hazards

Stem leaks are hot. A weeping steam or hot-water stem can spray at the point where your hands are. Never adjust packing on a live hot line unless your site procedure and the valve instructions allow it. Treat any stem leak you cannot stop with a light snug as a reason to isolate, not to push harder. Hot valves and flanges also cause contact burns. A federal steam tip sheet calls surfaces above 120 F a general-rule threshold for insulating to protect personnel, and advises removable insulation on components needing periodic repair. If you take old insulation off, it also says to check for asbestos first, as federal workplace safety rules require. See bare pipe, valves and flanges.

When to replace the valve

These are our own rules of thumb, not figures from a source:

  • The stem is scored or pitted where the packing rides, so new packing will not seal.
  • The gland has no travel left and the box cannot be refilled, or the follower or studs are corroded or damaged.
  • The valve will not shut off tight, so the repacked valve still cannot isolate the line. See exercising isolation valves.
  • A repack cannot be done safely, because the line cannot be isolated.

The federal facilities document also advises considering full replacement when the packing box bearing surfaces are beyond repair. For which valve to buy, see HVAC isolation and check valves.

Worked example (our own): what a drip costs in a year

This is our own example with assumed inputs, not measured data. Assumptions: a hot-water plant valve drips at 1 drop per second; a drop is 0.05 mL (about 20 drops per mL); one US gallon is 3,785 mL; a snug brings the drip to 1 drop every 10 minutes; make-up water is 50 F and loop water is 180 F; water weighs 8.34 lb per gallon.

  1. Before: 1 drop per second x 86,400 seconds = 86,400 drops per day.
  2. 86,400 x 0.05 mL = 4,320 mL per day. Per year: 4,320 x 365 = 1,576,800 mL, or 1,576,800 / 3,785 = about 417 gallons.
  3. Heat to bring that make-up water to loop temperature: 417 gal x 8.34 lb/gal x (180 - 50) F = about 452,000 Btu, roughly 0.45 MMBtu per year.
  4. After: 1 drop per 10 minutes = 144 drops per day. 144 x 0.05 = 7.2 mL per day, x 365 = 2,628 mL, or about 0.7 gallon per year.
  5. Change: about 416 gallons and about 0.45 MMBtu avoided per year.

The loss is small for a drip. A steam stem leak that has become a jet is a different order of loss; see Steam Leaks and Failed-Open Traps.

Field card: Valve Stem Packing Leaks

Small, even, and stop when the weep stops.

  • Packing in the stuffing box seals the stem; the follower and nuts set the squeeze.
  • Tighten nuts a small fraction of a turn each, in alternating order, so the follower stays square.
  • Overtightening crushes packing, grips the stem and wears it.
  • Repack only with the line isolated, drained and depressurized, with isolation verified.
  • Cut rings to size, tamp one at a time, stagger seams 90 degrees.
  • Hot lines burn: do not adjust a live hot-water or steam stem leak.
  • Replace the valve when the stem is damaged, the box is spent or it will not shut off.
