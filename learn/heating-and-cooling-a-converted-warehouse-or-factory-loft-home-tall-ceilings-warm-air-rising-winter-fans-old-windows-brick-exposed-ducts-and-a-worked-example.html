Heating and Cooling a Converted Loft Home: Tall Ceilings, Old Windows and Brick

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A converted warehouse or factory loft is a great place to live and a hard place to heat and cool. The ceilings are tall, the windows are big and old, and the walls are brick or concrete. This lesson explains why those features change comfort, what to try first, and what to ask before anyone touches the equipment.

Tall ceilings and warm air rising

Warm air is lighter than cool air, so it floats toward the ceiling. In an 8 ft room the difference is small. In a 14 ft or 20 ft loft it is easy to feel: the air at head height can be several degrees cooler than the air near the ceiling. This layering is called stratification. The thermostat usually sits on a wall at about chest height, so it reads the cooler layer and keeps the heat running while the top of the room gets warmer still.

Our own rule of thumb: a tall room wastes heat in two ways. Heat collects where nobody lives, and the bigger volume of air leaks out and gets replaced with cold outdoor air. The worked example below puts a number on the second one.

Ceiling fans, run slowly upward in winter

A ceiling fan can mix the layers. In winter, set it to its slowest speed in the direction that pulls air up through the middle of the blades, so the warm layer is gently stirred down along the walls without a breeze at your skin. In summer, the opposite direction makes a breeze for people. The related lesson on ceiling fans covers direction, speeds and real savings. On a very tall ceiling a fan hung on a long downrod may need to be lowered so it can actually move the air you live in.

Big old windows

Original steel or single-pane windows lose heat, feel cold when you sit near them, and let outdoor air in through the joints. Glass that cold also pulls warm air downward, which creates the cold downdraft in the sketch. Three things help without replacing the windows: weatherstripping and caulk on the leaks, heavy curtains on cold nights, and interior storm panels that sit inside the frame and add a still layer of air. Interior panels often suit buildings where the exterior cannot be changed, but ask first, as noted below.

Single-pane glass is also where you see winter condensation. A federal environmental agency advises keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and drying window condensation promptly because it can signal too much moisture. Lofts often have bathrooms and kitchens with little exhaust, so see the related lesson on window condensation.

Exposed brick and concrete

Masonry and concrete hold a lot of heat, so they are slow to warm in the fall and slow to cool in the spring. On a cold morning the walls can stay cold while the air is already warm, so the room feels cooler than the thermostat says. Do not chase that feeling by turning the setpoint far up. Give the building a steady schedule and use rugs and furniture away from the coldest wall. Our own note: do not drill into brick or concrete for equipment until you know what is behind it and who owns it.

Exposed ducts, ductless heads and open plans

With few walls and no ceiling to hide behind, equipment is on show. Exposed round ductwork is a common loft answer: it is easy to service and looks industrial. The trade-offs are that it is visible, it can be noisy if sized small, and long runs lose heat in winter and gain it in summer unless insulated. Ductless heads need only a small pair of refrigerant lines and a condensate drain, but the heads, line sets and outdoor unit also have to fit the look and the building rules. Ceiling cassettes and ducted mini-splits are worth comparing with wall heads (see the related lessons). Plan where the drain will run before choosing a head location.

Sleeping lofts that overheat

A sleeping platform near the ceiling sits in the warmest layer, so it is hot in summer and often uncomfortable in winter too. Try a fan that moves air across the bed, shades on the windows facing the sun, and a small cooling head aimed at the loft. Do not block a smoke alarm or CO alarm with bedding or curtains.

Shared building systems: ask first

In a converted building, the roof, walls, windows, risers, vents and sometimes the heating plant belong to the whole building. Before any change, ask the building manager. That includes drilling a wall for line sets, putting an outdoor unit on a roof or balcony, changing windows or panels, adding a vent, or working on shared piping. Ask also who can approve it and whether a licensed contractor is required. Rules vary by building, so we do not give legal advice here.

CO alarms with fuel-burning equipment

If your loft has any gas or oil appliance, a fireplace or a wood stove, you need working carbon monoxide alarms. A federal consumer product safety agency advises an alarm on each floor and near sleeping areas, battery-powered or with battery backup, and says interconnected alarms are preferred so that when one sounds they all sound. Its general advice for suspected poisoning is to move to fresh air at once and call 911. Our own addition: in a loft with a sleeping platform, place the alarm so the sleeping area is covered, and read the maker's mounting height instructions. See the related CO alarm lesson.

Worked example (our own, assumed round numbers)

Compare two homes with the same 1,200 ft2 floor, one with a 14 ft loft ceiling and one with an 8 ft ceiling. Assumptions, all our own: the air leaks out and is replaced 0.5 times per hour (0.5 air changes per hour), the indoor-outdoor difference is 40 F, and we use the common sensible-heat relation Btu/h = 1.08 x cfm x temperature difference.

  1. Volume: 1,200 x 14 = 16,800 ft3 for the loft; 1,200 x 8 = 9,600 ft3 for the standard room.
  2. Leakage airflow: cfm = volume x 0.5 / 60. Loft: 16,800 x 0.5 / 60 = 140 cfm. Standard: 9,600 x 0.5 / 60 = 80 cfm.
  3. Heat to warm that air: 1.08 x cfm x 40. Loft: 1.08 x 140 x 40 = 6,048 Btu/h. Standard: 1.08 x 80 x 40 = 3,456 Btu/h.
  4. Difference: 6,048 - 3,456 = 2,592 Btu/h, and 2,592 / 3,456 = 0.75, so the tall loft needs 75 percent more heat, just for leakage air.

This counts only air leakage. Walls, windows and the roof add more, and real leakage rates differ. Use it to see the pattern, not to size equipment.

Humidity

Winter air is dry, but a loft with cold glass, little exhaust and indoor laundry drying can still fog windows. Summer is the other side: a cooling system that is too big cools fast and shuts off before it removes much moisture, so the loft feels clammy. Aim for the 30 to 50 percent range mentioned above and run bath and kitchen exhaust that vents outdoors.

Field card: Heating and Cooling a Converted Loft Home

Tall rooms stratify, so mix the air, seal the leaks and ask the building first.

  • Warm air collects at the ceiling; the thermostat reads the cooler air below.
  • Winter fan: slowest speed, air pulled up through the middle.
  • Old glass: seal leaks, add interior storm panels, watch condensation.
  • Brick and concrete warm slowly; keep a steady schedule.
  • Our own example: 14 ft vs 8 ft ceiling, 6,048 vs 3,456 Btu/h for leakage air, 75 percent more.
  • Ask the building manager before drilling, venting or placing equipment.
  • CO alarms on each floor and near sleeping areas with any fuel-burning equipment.
