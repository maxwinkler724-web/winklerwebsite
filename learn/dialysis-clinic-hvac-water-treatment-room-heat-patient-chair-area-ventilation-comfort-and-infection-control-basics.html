Dialysis Clinic HVAC: Chair-Area Air, Patient Comfort, Water Room Heat and Infection Control

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An outpatient dialysis clinic is a big open room full of people who sit still for hours, a back room full of water equipment, and a rule book that cares about both comfort and infection control. This lesson covers what a facility tech can check on the air side, and where the answer belongs to the designer or the clinic.

Check the rules that apply to your building

Check the rules that apply to your building. The edition of the ventilation standard and the health-facility guidelines in force is set by the state and the authority having jurisdiction. This page is general education from public pages read in 2026. Do not change airflow, pressure or filters in a treated space without the clinic's infection-control staff and the original design documents.

What the federal rules say about the environment

Medicare's conditions for coverage for dialysis facilities are in 42 CFR Part 494. Section 494.60 says the facility must provide a "safe, functional, and comfortable treatment environment," must maintain a comfortable temperature, and must make reasonable accommodations for patients who are not comfortable at that temperature. It also says equipment, including the water treatment system, must be maintained as the manufacturer recommends, and that patients must stay in view of staff during treatment. Section 494.40 covers water and dialysate quality and names the AAMI dialysate standard it relies on. Those rules set no airflow number, but they make a cold-patient complaint a compliance matter.

What the ventilation standard lists

ASHRAE Standard 170 covers ventilation of health care facilities. A published addendum to the 2021 edition (addendum h) lists a "dialysis treatment area" row in its outpatient table. As read, that row shows no pressure requirement, 2 air changes per hour (ACH) of outdoor air, 6 ACH total, no requirement that all air be exhausted outdoors, unoccupied turndown permitted, MERV-8 minimum filtration, and a design temperature of 72 to 78 F. The same table lists the dialyzer reprocessing room as negative to its neighbors with 10 ACH total, all exhausted outdoors. We read an addendum, not the adopted edition, so confirm the numbers your state enforces and the humidity line against the design documents.

New York's state health department publishes its own dialysis design requirements. As read, they require the water treatment equipment area to be a dedicated and secure area with a drain and with access to all components, and they point to AAMI water standards for the equipment (ANSI/AAMI/ISO 26722 and 13959; names only here).

Why patients are cold

Patients sit still for several hours, often under a thin blanket, and dialysis itself cools some people as blood circulates outside the body. We offer that as shop-floor experience, not a sourced fact. The air side adds to it: supply air that falls straight onto chairs. A 6 ACH system moves a lot of air, so the supply temperature and the diffuser throw matter. Rules of thumb for the tech:

  • Ask the clinic which chairs draw complaints and whether they sit under a diffuser.
  • Check that diffusers are not blocked or pointed at the chair, and that the room setpoint is inside the design band.
  • Do not warm the whole room to fix two chairs. Blankets, chair placement and diffuser patterns are cheaper levers. Ask what the clinic is allowed to change.

The isolation station for hepatitis B

CDC's 2001 recommendations for chronic hemodialysis say units should designate a separate room for patients who test positive for hepatitis B surface antigen, with dedicated machines and supplies, and that newly opened units should have an isolation room. The same document says such patients should be treated in a less busy area if a separate room does not exist, and that staff caring for them should not care for susceptible patients at the same time. It does not state any air pressure for that room, and the ASHRAE row above lists none for the treatment area. The danger there is blood-borne, so do not apply the negative-pressure rules from airborne isolation rooms by habit. Ask the designer what is required and leave the pressure as designed.

The water treatment room

The reverse osmosis (RO) skids, pumps, tanks and drain make heat and sometimes wet floors, and water quality depends on a working system. Section 494.40 requires chlorine and chloramine removal with a backup, and ties testing to the AAMI standard. What the tech should do:

  • Keep the room at the temperature the water-system maker allows. We cite no number here, so get the limit from the equipment manual.
  • Do not drain a coil pan, or run a new condensate line, into the dialysis drain without the designer's approval.
  • Report leaks and sweating pipes. CDC's environmental guideline says wet absorbent materials support fungal growth and calls for repair and drying of wet porous materials within 72 hours.
  • Never open or work on the water system itself. That belongs to the clinic's water-treatment staff.

Filters, drain pans and after-hours schedules

CDC's environmental guideline says filtration is the primary means of cleaning the air and that poor maintenance of ventilation systems can contribute to health-care associated airborne infections. In practice: replace filters on schedule at the rated size and grade, never install a lower grade than the design lists, and keep the coil drain pans draining. Our lessons on filters and on coil drain pans cover the habits.

The addendum lists unoccupied turndown as permitted for the treatment area. Many clinics run early shifts and close at night, so ask before setting back: some owners hold the schedule, and the room must be back to the design temperature before the first patient sits down. Warm-up time is the usual early-morning complaint.

Worked example: supply cfm for a 20-chair area

Assumptions (Our own, not from a standard): a 2,400 sq ft area, 10 ft ceiling, 20 patient chairs, 6 staff on the floor, and the 6 ACH total from the addendum row above.

  1. Volume = 2,400 x 10 = 24,000 cu ft.
  2. cfm = ACH x volume / 60 = 6 x 24,000 / 60 = 2,400 cfm total air. Outdoor air at 2 ACH = 2 x 24,000 / 60 = 800 cfm.
  3. Internal sensible heat (Our own assumptions): 20 patients x 250 Btu/h = 5,000; 6 staff x 300 = 1,800; 20 machines at 300 W each = 6,000 W x 3.412 = 20,472; lights at 1 W per sq ft = 2,400 W x 3.412 = 8,189. Total = 35,461 Btu/h. We left out the building envelope, so a real load is higher.
  4. Supply cfm needed for that load at a 20 F difference (room 75 F, supply 55 F): cfm = 35,461 / (1.08 x 20) = 35,461 / 21.6 = about 1,642 cfm.
  5. Compare: the 2,400 cfm air-change figure is larger, so air changes set the airflow. At 2,400 cfm the difference needed is 35,461 / (1.08 x 2,400) = 35,461 / 2,592 = about 13.7 F, so supply air near 61 F in a 75 F room.

The result: the air-change rule moves more air than the heat load needs, so the supply is mild, which is good for cold patients, as long as the system holds that supply temperature and not a colder one.

Field card: Dialysis Clinic HVAC

Comfortable patients, clean air, dry water room, and no changes without the designer.

  • 42 CFR 494.60 requires a comfortable temperature and accommodation for patients who are not comfortable.
  • ASHRAE 170 addendum h row, as read: 2 ACH outdoor, 6 ACH total, MERV-8, 72 to 78 F. Confirm your adopted edition.
  • cfm = ACH x volume / 60. Sensible supply cfm = Btu/h / (1.08 x delta T).
  • Hepatitis B isolation is a separate room with dedicated machines, per CDC. No pressure is listed, so ask.
  • Water room: dedicated, secure, with a drain. Do not touch the water system.
  • Replace filters at the design grade, keep drain pans draining, report wet materials.
