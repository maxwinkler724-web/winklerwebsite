AC Short Cycling: Causes, Oversizing, Charge, Thermostat and Safeties

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The AC starts, runs a few minutes, quits, and starts again. This lesson explains what counts as short cycling, why it costs you comfort and equipment life, the usual causes from the thermostat to the refrigerant safeties, and which checks belong to the owner and which belong to a technician.

What short cycling is

Short cycling means the cooling runs for a short time, shuts off, and restarts again and again, instead of running a good long stretch and resting. There is no single official number. One building-science writer at Energy Vanguard offers a field test: time a run on a hot afternoon. About 10 minutes or less suggests the unit is oversized, about 20 minutes is fine, and 30 minutes or more means humidity should be fairly well controlled. Treat that as a rule of thumb, not a code limit. The heating-season version of this problem is in Why Is My Furnace Short Cycling?

Why it hurts

Humidity. The U.S. Department of Energy's Building America guide on right-sizing points out that in most homes the central AC is the only dehumidifier. Moisture needs time to condense on the cold coil and drain away. An oversized unit reaches the thermostat setting quickly and quits before much water comes out, and water left on the coil can evaporate back into the air. ENERGY STAR says the same: dehumidifying takes enough run time for moisture to condense and drain, and poor humidity control promotes mold and mildew. If your house feels sticky with the AC running, see House Feels Humid With the AC Running.

Equipment and bills. A 2002 DOE fact sheet written by the Florida Solar Energy Center says short cycling reduces efficiency and wears equipment out faster. ENERGY STAR's right-sizing sheet adds that efficiency is low at startup and peaks at around 10 minutes of running; its example shows a unit going from 5 to 9 minutes of run time gaining 17 percent in efficiency. The DOE Building America guide names avoiding compressor short cycling as a way to extend equipment life.

Cause 1: oversized equipment

A unit that is too big pulls the house down to the setpoint fast and shuts off. ENERGY STAR notes that contractors often choose bigger units because they cool quickly and lower the risk of not keeping up, and that an oversized unit can hide other problems such as dirty filters, leaky ducts and a wrong refrigerant charge. The Building America guide recommends Manual J and S calculations rather than guessing. Manual J is the load calculation method developed by ACCA (Air Conditioning Contractors of America), according to Energy Vanguard, and is explained in Heat Load Calculation Intro: Manual J. The guide also cautions that right-sizing works only if the house matches the design, so a leaky house can behave differently than the calculation.

Cause 2: thermostat settings and location

Many thermostats cap how often the compressor may start, a setting called cycle rate or cycles per hour (CPH). Honeywell defines the cycle rate as the maximum number of times a system will run in an hour while holding the setpoint at 50 percent of its capacity. In the setup table for its PRO TH1000/TH2000 series thermostats, Honeywell lists a compressor cycle rate of 3 CPH as the default and says 3 is recommended for most compressors, with 1, 2, 4, 5 or 6 as other choices. Honeywell's page for another thermostat recommends 3 CPH for high-efficiency (90 percent plus) gas or oil forced-air heating, so the right value depends on the equipment and its instructions. The same PRO table has a compressor protection setting that defaults to a five-minute compressor off time. ecobee takes an off-time approach: an ecobee4 installation guide hosted by a utility cooperative has the installer set the compressor minimum cycle off time to 600 seconds (10 minutes) in one procedure. Check your own thermostat's manual before changing anything.

Location matters too. Carrier, writing about furnaces, says a thermostat near a heat source can misread the temperature and shut the equipment off before the rest of the home reaches the setpoint, and recommends an interior wall away from sun, doors, windows, vents, drafts and heat-producing appliances. See Where to Put a Thermostat.

Cause 3: safeties opening

Outdoor units often carry pressure switches that stop the compressor when pressure goes out of range. Trane's support page describes its controller's behavior: if a low-pressure control stays open for one continuous second, the compressor shuts off at once and cannot restart for at least three minutes, and four consecutive opens during a cooling call lock the compressor out until a manual reset. The high-pressure control follows the same lockout rule. A Rheem kit sheet lists a high-pressure switch that opens above 425 psig, closes below 350 psig, and starts a five-minute compressor delay. Those numbers are for that kit; other equipment differs.

What opens the switch? Emerson's compressor troubleshooting chart for a short-cycling compressor asks which control is cycling it: protector, thermostat, low-pressure control or high-pressure control. It tells the tech to check for loss of charge in one step, which fits a low-pressure trip, and to check for dirty condensers, a bad condenser fan or motor, and refrigerant overcharge in another, which fits a high-pressure trip. A dirty outdoor coil is covered in Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure. A frozen indoor coil is its own problem with its own causes: AC Evaporator Coil Frozen.

Cause 4: compressor overload

Compressors have a built-in protector that opens when the motor gets too hot or draws too much current. The Emerson chart separates a compressor that starts and immediately trips on its protector from one that short cycles, and says to allow time for the protector to reset. An ACHR News teardown article lists high head pressure, low suction pressure and high return gas temperature among the possible causes of compressor overheating. A tripping protector is a symptom, so a tech looks for the reason before replacing parts.

Cause 5: built-in anti-short-cycle timers

Timers are not a fault. Honeywell's PRO thermostat default is a five-minute compressor off time. Emerson's Goodman controls spec sheet lists a three-minute compressor short cycle delay for its 49S25-707 outdoor control, and Emerson's digital compressor controller document describes a two-minute anti-short-cycle delay. If your AC waits a few minutes before starting, that is a timer protecting the compressor, not a failure.

Worked example: reading a run log

You note when the AC starts and stops for one hour on a warm afternoon and see six starts, with the compressor on for 30 minutes in total.

  • Cycles per hour: 6 starts in 60 minutes is 6 CPH.
  • Average run: 30 min on ÷ 6 cycles = 5 minutes per run.
  • Average cycle length: 60 min ÷ 6 cycles = 10 minutes.
  • Average off time: 10 min − 5 min = 5 minutes.
  • Run share: 30 ÷ 60 = 50 percent of the hour.

Now compare to Honeywell's recommended 3 CPH. At 3 CPH the cycle length is 60 ÷ 3 = 20 minutes. Honeywell's definition assumes a 50 percent load, which matches this hour's 50 percent run share, so a unit held to 3 CPH would run 10 minutes on and 10 off. This one ran half that long, with 6 ÷ 3 = 2 times the starts. Five-minute runs also sit below Energy Vanguard's 10-minute rule of thumb, and the five-minute off time equals Honeywell's default compressor protection delay, which fits a unit that restarts the moment the timer lets it. The log alone cannot tell you why. A thermostat set to 6 CPH, an oversized unit, or a safety opening could each produce it, which is why the checks below come in order.

Owner checks and tech checks

Owner: Look at the thermostat's settings screen or manual for cycle rate or compressor delay, and do not change it without the maker's instructions. Check the air filter and make sure supply and return vents are open. If you see ice on the indoor coil or lines, turn the cooling off and call a pro. Keep plants and debris away from the outdoor unit. Keep a log like the one above. Do not open electrical panels or handle refrigerant.

Tech: Confirm the thermostat setup and location. Find which device is ending the call by watching the thermostat call, the contactor and the safety circuit. Measure pressures and temperatures for charge and condenser airflow, and test the compressor's amp draw and protector. Compare the equipment to a real Manual J before blaming the size. Handling refrigerant requires EPA 608 certification.

Field card: AC Short Cycling

Count starts per hour first, then find which device ends the run.

  • Runs of about 10 minutes or less per cycle hint at oversizing.
  • Cycles per hour = starts in 60 minutes. Cycle length = 60 ÷ CPH.
  • Honeywell PRO default: 3 CPH compressor, 5-minute compressor off time.
  • Oversizing hurts humidity removal and efficiency; size with Manual J.
  • Low-pressure or high-pressure switches can stop the compressor; find out why.
  • Check thermostat setup and location, filter and coils, then call a tech.
