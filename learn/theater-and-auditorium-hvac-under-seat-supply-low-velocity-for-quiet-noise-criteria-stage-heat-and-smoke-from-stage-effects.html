Theater and Auditorium HVAC: Quiet Under-Seat Supply, Stage Heat and Stage Haze

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A performance hall has a strange job. It must hold hundreds of warm people, absorb a wall of stage lights, stay nearly silent while an actor whispers, and not mistake a haze machine for a fire. This lesson walks through how designers and technicians handle each of those, using an invented 800-seat hall for the numbers.

Why the air comes in under the seat, and slowly

Many halls deliver air through small outlets at or under each seat. That puts the supply in the occupied zone, so it can be cool-but-not-cold and gentle, and the heat from people rises away toward a high return. For stratification basics, see Underfloor Air Distribution and Displacement Ventilation.

The speed matters because the outlet is a few inches from a person. A maker design guide for theatre displacement diffusers says about 0.20 m/s near the seats at a 1.5 K temperature gradient is normally acceptable, describes typical supply of 10 to 14 litres per second with roughly one diffuser per seated person, and notes that near-zone velocity depends on airflow per metre of row and on how the diffuser aims the air. Converted, 0.20 m/s is about 39 feet per minute and 10 to 14 L/s is about 21 to 30 cfm (our own conversion, 1 L/s = 2.119 cfm). Fast air at an ankle is a draft complaint, and through a small outlet it is also a hiss.

A field study of ventilation in live theatres found that only one of the five venues it tabulated used under-seat supply (terminals running along the full length of the rows as one continuous opening, with extraction at ceiling level). The same study reported that most auditoria were not well mixed, so CO2 differed by nearly 400 ppm between front and back rows in one venue and upper tiers often ran higher than lower ones.

NC, RC and what a "quiet" hall means

Noise criteria are numbered curves. A Cornell facilities standard describes NC (Noise Criteria) as classifying continuous background noise in a space across eight octave bands from 63 to 8000 Hz, covering HVAC, equipment and outside noise. It describes RC Mark II (Room Criteria) as a similar single-number rating over 16 to 8000 Hz that also looks at how balanced the sound is, and it asks for RC to be measured and reported at the end of a project. The same document leaves the target for assembly spaces "to be determined" with an acoustical consultant. The federal Whole Building Design Guide, in its page on performance and presentation spaces, gives a recommended NC range of NC-20 to NC-30. A lower number is quieter, and each curve limits every octave band, so a fan that is loud only in the low bands can still fail.

For the field tech, the practical reading is this: the NC target is a ceiling on the sound the system adds to a silent room. Fixes and sound math live in HVAC Noise in Commercial Buildings. For outlet selection and why bigger, slower grilles are quieter, see Supply Registers and Return Grilles.

Duct velocity

Air noise rises with velocity, so quiet halls use slow ducts and large outlets. We did not find a public table we could cite for hall duct speeds, so the example below uses our own assumed speeds. The acoustic consultant decides the real values.

Stage lighting heat

A DOE solid-state lighting field brief says halogen theatrical instruments draw 575 to 2,000 watts each, and that LED theatrical lighting saved 50 to 90 percent over halogen in dance performances. It also says the LED switch greatly reduced radiated heat. That brief does not give cooling load numbers, so every watt below is our own arithmetic. Nearly all electric input to a light ends up as heat in the building, and 1 watt is 3.412 Btu/h. Ask where that heat lands: over the stage, in the fly space or on the audience. A separate stage-house supply and exhaust, or a high return above the stage, keeps stage heat from being carried over the seats.

Haze, fog and smoke detectors

Haze machines exist to make the air visible, and smoke detectors exist to notice visible air. Public pages from a university fire marshal's office say smoke and haze can trip detectors and activate the building fire alarm, require review and approval at least two weeks ahead, weigh proximity to detectors and egress, and may require fire alarm staff to monitor the system. A second university procedure says effects may require partially disabling the system, with a fire watch by a fire alarm technician plus a second person walking the building. A township order says fog may be used only when affected systems are placed on test with a fire watch, requires the applicant to say whether HVAC could carry fog to other areas and set off duct detectors or smoke alarms there, and calls for testing in front of a code official. The same order says fog must not obscure the means of egress.

NFPA's page for NFPA 72 (National Fire Alarm and Signaling Code) lists fire alarm, emergency communications, mass notification, inspection, testing and maintenance among its subjects. We have not read the standard itself, and your fire marshal sets the local rules. See also Duct Smoke Detectors.

For HVAC staff: never bypass a detector on your own to suit a show. Tell facilities and the fire alarm contractor which air handler serves the house and stage, because the fan carries haze wherever that air goes.

CO2 swings and pre-cooling

An auditorium goes from empty to full to empty in minutes. In the same live-theatre study, an interval of about 100 minutes dropped one venue's CO2 close to outdoor levels, and another venue's demand-controlled ventilation ramped up only after average CO2 passed 1,000 ppm, leading the authors to conclude demand-driven systems did not always work as intended. One university building narrative describes an auditorium unit that normally uses CO2 as an occupancy signal to cut ventilation when the room is empty. Details on sensors are in Demand-Controlled Ventilation.

Pre-cooling follows from that. A Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory field study of two commercial buildings found pre-cooling during unoccupied hours can reduce cooling load in peak hours, but that releasing the stored cool too fast can cause high demand, and the abstract gives no savings figure. For a hall, the practical version is to start the air handler before the doors open, bring the seats and house to setpoint while the room is empty, and have outdoor air ready before people arrive rather than reacting to CO2 after the room is full.

Worked example: 800 seats, under-seat outlets

Our own example. All inputs here are assumptions, not measurements.

Assume 800 seats, one outlet per seat, 25 cfm per seat (inside the 21 to 30 cfm maker range above), and an assumed outlet free area of 0.15 square foot (about 22 square inches).

Step 1, total supply: 800 seats x 25 cfm = 20,000 cfm.

Step 2, outlet velocity: 25 cfm ÷ 0.15 sq ft = 167 fpm, which is about 0.85 m/s.

Step 3, compare to the maker's near-seat target: 0.20 m/s is about 39 fpm. The outlet face is about 4 times faster than that, so the diffuser must spread and slow the air before it reaches a person. That is a check against the maker's data for the exact outlet, not something arithmetic can settle. If the free area were half as large (0.075 sq ft), velocity doubles to 25 ÷ 0.075 = 333 fpm, and noise and draft risk rise.

Step 4, main duct, with two assumed speeds: at 1,200 fpm, 20,000 ÷ 1,200 = 16.7 sq ft; at an assumed 800 fpm, 20,000 ÷ 800 = 25 sq ft. The slower choice costs space but removes a noise source. One airstream would be split among several mains in practice.

Step 5, heat sanity check. People: the EnergyPlus documentation lists 108 W for seated, quiet activity. 800 x 108 W = 86,400 W, and 86,400 x 3.412 = 294,800 Btu/h (this total includes moisture heat). Lights (assumed): 60 fixtures at 750 W all on, 45,000 W x 3.412 = 153,500 Btu/h. If the supply air carried only a 10 F difference (assumed), 1.08 x 20,000 cfm x 10 F = 216,000 Btu/h. That is less than the 294,800 total for people alone (part of that total is moisture heat, but the margin is still thin), and the gentle under-seat supply cannot absorb stage light heat on top of it. So stage heat must be removed by the stage-side system, or the supply temperature difference and airflow must be revisited by the designer.

Field card: Theater and Auditorium HVAC

Slow air in low, haze out of the detectors' way, heat handled where it starts.

  • Maker guide: about 0.20 m/s near the seats (about 39 fpm), 10 to 14 L/s per person (21 to 30 cfm).
  • NC target for performance spaces: NC-20 to NC-30 in the federal WBDG page. Confirm with the acoustical consultant.
  • Outlet check: cfm per outlet ÷ free area = fpm. Compare to the maker table.
  • Light heat: watts x 3.412 = Btu/h.
  • Haze or fog: permit, fire watch, never bypass detectors yourself.
  • Start the air handler before doors open.
