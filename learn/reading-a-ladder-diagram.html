Reading a Ladder Diagram

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A ladder diagram is a map of an electrical control circuit drawn like a ladder. Once you know how to read one, a wiring label inside the equipment stops being a puzzle and starts being a story.

The layout

The two vertical lines are the rails. In a control diagram, one rail is the power side and the other is the return or common. Each horizontal line between them is a rung, which describes one job. Work through the diagram one rung at a time, starting at the top and moving from the left side across to the right. The conditions sit on the left, and the thing they control, usually a coil or a load, sits on the right end of the rung.

The symbols, in general

Symbols vary a little by manufacturer and standard, so look for the legend on the diagram. The common ones:

  • Normally open contact: open at rest, closes when its controlling coil or device is energized. Think of a start button or a relay contact.
  • Normally closed contact: closed at rest, opens when energized. Think of a stop button or a safety switch.
  • Coil: an output, drawn as a circle or box. When power reaches it, the relay or contactor it belongs to operates, and its contacts change state.
  • Loads: motors, heaters, igniters and lights, often drawn with a circle and a letter.
  • Safety switches and sensors: limits, pressure switches and thermostats appear as contacts with labels.

The rule for reading a rung

A rung can energize its coil only when there is a complete path of closed contacts from one rail to the other. Contacts in series on one line all have to be closed, which works like AND. Contacts on parallel branches give the circuit a choice, which works like OR. Ask on every rung: what has to be true for the right-hand device to get power, and which of those conditions am I looking at?

The seal-in pattern

A common pattern uses a coil's own normally open contact in parallel with a momentary start switch. Press start, the coil energizes, its contact closes and keeps the coil on after you let go. A normally closed stop contact in series ends it. Many control circuits use the same idea.

A simple way to trace a problem

  • Find the device that is not working and find its rung.
  • Work backward through every series contact to the left rail. Each one is a condition that must be satisfied.
  • Match each contact to a real part, using the labels and the wiring diagram. Many manufacturers pair a ladder diagram with a physical wiring diagram.
  • Use the meter with the lockout and test-before-touch habits from Electrical Basics for HVAC to find which condition is not being met. An open contact is where the path stops.

If the open contact is a safety, the diagram has told you why. Find and fix the cause. Never jumper it.

Reading the real thing

Unit diagrams often combine line voltage and low voltage on one sheet, use color codes and wire numbers, and show boards with terminals and connectors. Programmable controllers use a similar ladder style in software. The method of left to right, rung by rung, conditions then output, still applies. Always use the diagram that came with the specific unit.

Field card: Reading a Ladder Diagram

Left to right, rung by rung: what must be true for this coil to get power?

  • Rails = power and return. Rungs = one job each.
  • NO contacts close when energized. NC contacts open when energized.
  • Series = all must be true. Parallel = any one will do.
  • Trace backward from the dead device to the open contact.
  • An open safety is a clue. Never jumper it.
  • Use the unit's own diagram and legend.
