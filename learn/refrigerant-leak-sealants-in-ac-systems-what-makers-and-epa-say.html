Refrigerant Leak Sealants in AC Systems: What Makers and EPA Say

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A customer asks whether a can of "leak sealer" will fix a slow refrigerant loss. It is a fair question, and the honest answer has three parts: what the product is, what the people who build compressors and recovery machines say about it, and what the federal leak repair rules expect you to do. This lesson walks through all three and ends with a leak rate calculation you can repeat on any job.

What a sealant is, in plain terms

A leak sealant is a chemical added to a running refrigeration or air conditioning circuit. It travels with the refrigerant and oil, and the idea is that it reacts or collects at a small hole and plugs it. Trade press describes several families. In a 2016 ACHR News roundup, one maker's representative said polymer-based products need a system that is "free of air and moisture," which can be hard to achieve in the field. Other products in the same article were described as polymer-free, as moisture-eliminating with a UV dye, or as traveling with the oil.

Sellers describe these products as suited to very small leaks, with one article citing holes of about 300 microns and smaller. Those claims come from the people selling them, so treat them as a starting point, not proof.

What equipment makers say

In the 2016 ACHR News article, a Danfoss representative said the company would not endorse these additives and that, if one is detected during a warranty claim, "it would void our warranty terms." An Emerson Climate Technologies representative said the company does not approve additives in the presence of refrigerants, and raised the concern that added material could block orifices the system was designed to keep open. A representative of Appion, a recovery equipment maker, said the only thing that should be in a system is virgin refrigerant and oil.

Sealant sellers in the same article say their products are harmless when the system is prepared properly. That is a seller's account relayed by trade press, not a published approval from the maker of the unit you are standing in front of. For that, read the warranty and service manual for the exact model. The Trane commercial R-22 conversion bulletin we read does not mention sealants at all. If the paperwork is silent, call the maker before you add anything.

The contamination risk to your tools

A refrigeration instructor quoted in the 2016 article said dyes and leak-stop additives "can degrade the performance of manifold gauges" and vacuum equipment, that the contamination can carry over to the next job, and that he doubted reclaim facilities could easily remove the additives. Robinair's operating manual for a recovery machine built for contaminated refrigerant says any other use could contaminate the recovered refrigerant with hydrocarbons and leak sealants, and says everything recovered with it must be reclaimed or destroyed.

Sellers argue the opposite. The 2009 ACHR News article, written by a sealant inventor, says properly applied vacuum-packed products do not clog gauges or recovery machines. Even so, it says to tag a system that has sealant in it, because the refrigerant may not be reusable. The practical lesson is the tag: if you do not know whether a system has sealant, you do not know what you are about to pull through your machine. See Refrigerant Recovery, Recycle and Reclaim for what happens to recovered refrigerant afterward.

What EPA says about leaks

No EPA page we read mentions sealants. What the rules do cover is how fast a leak has to be dealt with, and that is where sealant talk runs into the law. Two rules apply, depending on the refrigerant:

  • Section 608, 40 CFR 82.157. It applies to appliances with a full charge of 50 pounds or more of an ozone-depleting refrigerant. The text says it does not apply to appliances that contain only substitute refrigerants.
  • AIM Act rule, 40 CFR 84.106. It applies to appliances with a full charge of 15 pounds or more of a refrigerant that contains a regulated substance or a substitute with a global warming potential above 53. It excludes appliances with only ozone-depleting substances and the residential and light commercial air conditioning and heat pump subsector. The requirements apply as of January 1, 2026.

Both rules use the same trigger leak rates for the large appliances they cover: 20 percent a year for commercial refrigeration, 30 percent for industrial process refrigeration, and 10 percent for comfort cooling and other appliances. The owner must calculate the leak rate every time refrigerant is added. If the rate is above the trigger, a certified technician must repair the leaks, and the repair and an initial verification test are due within 30 days of adding the refrigerant, or 120 days if an industrial process shutdown is required. A follow-up verification test comes within 10 days of the successful initial test. The owner can instead choose a retrofit or retirement plan, due within 30 days and finished within a year.

Notice the words: repair, verify, retrofit or retire. Adding refrigerant over and over is not on the list. A sealant is also not a verification test. We found no EPA statement on whether a sealant job counts as a repair, so ask EPA or your compliance contact before relying on one. A typical home split system is below these thresholds and falls outside the leak repair rules, but the same find-it-and-fix-it habit still makes sense. See EPA 608 Certification Basics and Leaks and Leak Repair Basics.

Worked example: does this top-off trip the trigger?

EPA's older compliance guidance for industrial process refrigeration gives the annualizing method this way: pounds added, divided by the normal full charge, divided by the days since refrigerant was last added over 365, times 100. Here is an invented unit.

The job. A commercial refrigeration appliance has a 60 lb full charge. You top it off with 6 lb, and the last addition was 90 days ago.

  1. Pounds added divided by full charge: 6 ÷ 60 = 0.10.
  2. Days since last addition divided by 365: 90 ÷ 365 = 0.2466.
  3. Divide step 1 by step 2: 0.10 ÷ 0.2466 = 0.4056.
  4. Multiply by 100: 0.4056 × 100 = about 40.6 percent per year.

Check: 6 × 365 ÷ 90 = 24.3 lb a year, and 24.3 ÷ 60 = 40.6 percent.

Compare to the threshold. Commercial refrigeration is 20 percent, in 82.157(c)(2) for an ozone-depleting refrigerant and in 84.106(c)(2) for a covered substitute. 40.6 is above 20, so the leak must be repaired and verified within 30 days of the addition, or a retrofit or retirement plan made. For scale, 20 percent of 60 lb is 12 lb a year, about 3.0 lb in 90 days. The 6 lb top-off is double that.

Caution. The guidance document is older and its own example uses a 35 percent trigger that no longer applies. We used it only for the arithmetic; the current triggers come from the CFR sections. Rule 84.106 also allows a rolling average method, so check the rule text for your appliance.

When a real repair is the right choice

The 2016 article's sources lean one way. One said "If you can hear or see a leak, you need to fix it the traditional way." Another said these products should not be allowed as permanent repairs, and noted that Manitoba treats them as temporary and requires leaks to be repaired immediately. A seller quoted there said its products are for leaks a technician cannot find. With the rules above:

  • If the appliance is above a trigger rate, the rule asks for repair, verification and records, not additives.
  • If you can find the leak, fix it: recover, repair or replace the part, pressure test, evacuate, and recharge. See Electronic Refrigerant Leak Detectors and Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods.
  • If the unit is under warranty, check the warranty and the maker's service literature first. Danfoss said detection of an additive during a claim would void its warranty terms.
  • If a sealant has already been added, tag the system, tell the next technician.

Field card: Refrigerant Leak Sealants in AC Systems

Low charge means a leak. Find it, fix it, verify it.

  • Annualized leak rate = (lb added ÷ full charge) ÷ (days since last addition ÷ 365) × 100.
  • Trigger rates for large appliances: 20% commercial refrigeration, 30% industrial process, 10% comfort cooling and other.
  • Above the trigger: repair and verify in 30 days, or a retrofit or retirement plan.
  • Example: 6 lb on a 60 lb charge after 90 days is 40.6%, double the 20% line.
  • Danfoss said additives found in a claim void its warranty terms; Emerson does not approve additives.
  • Tag any system with sealant; contamination can reach gauges and recovery machines.
