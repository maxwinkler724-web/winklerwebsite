V-Belts vs Cogged and Synchronous Belts on HVAC Fans

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Most belt-driven fans use a standard wrapped V-belt, and it works. Two upgrades exist: the cogged (notched) V-belt and the synchronous (toothed) belt. This lesson compares efficiency, shows when a swap is a bad idea, and lists what a worn belt looks like. For the basics of sheaves and tension, start with Belt Drives and Sheave Basics.

Three belts, three ways to grip

A wrapped V-belt is a rubber belt with a fabric cover that wedges into a sheave groove. Friction carries the load, so a small amount of slip is part of how it works. A cogged belt has the same outer shape and runs in the same groove, but notches in the inner face make it easier to bend around the sheave. A synchronous belt has teeth that mesh with a toothed sprocket, like a chain.

Efficiency numbers

DOE says a V-belt drive can reach a peak efficiency of 95 percent or more when new, that notched belts run about 2 percent better than standard V-belts, and that synchronous belts hold about 98 percent. A trade-press article puts new V-belts at 95 to 98 percent and says efficiency drops to a nominal 93 percent if the belt is not periodically retensioned. DOE adds that V-belt efficiency can slide by as much as 5 percent over time from slippage, and that V-belts lose efficiency at high torque because slip rises.

A federal field test on building fans reported: 95 percent for the standard V-belt, 97 percent for cogged and 99 percent for synchronous. Measured savings on a variable-speed fan were larger at low speed. Synchronous belts saved 2.3 percent at 60 Hz and 20.1 percent at 15 Hz, and cogged belts saved 1.2 and 9.3 percent at those speeds. Those are results on those fans, not a promise for yours. A well-tensioned new V-belt is already efficient; most of the gain comes from avoiding the slow slide as belts stretch.

Worked example: what the gap costs

This example is our own. The inputs are assumptions, except the two efficiencies, which come from the sources above (93 percent for a V-belt that has drifted, 98 percent for synchronous).

  • Assumed: a 15 hp motor loaded to 12 bhp at the fan shaft, 92 percent motor efficiency, 6,000 hours per year, $0.15 per kWh.
  • V-belt at 93 percent: motor shaft power = 12 / 0.93 = 12.90 hp.
  • Synchronous at 98 percent: 12 / 0.98 = 12.24 hp.
  • Difference at the motor shaft: 12.90 − 12.24 = 0.66 hp.
  • Convert to input power: 0.66 hp × 0.746 kW/hp = 0.49 kW at the shaft, then 0.49 / 0.92 = 0.53 kW from the line (0.658 × 0.746 / 0.92 = 0.534 kW unrounded).
  • Energy: 0.534 kW × 6,000 h = about 3,200 kWh per year.
  • Cost: 3,203 kWh × $0.15 = about $480 per year.

If the V-belt were kept tight near 95 percent, the gap would shrink by more than half. For scale, DOE's own example (a 100 hp supply fan at an assumed 75 percent load, 95 to 98 percent, $0.08 per kWh) saves about 16,130 kWh and $1,290 per year. DOE says the upgrade is most cost-effective at the end of V-belt life.

When a synchronous retrofit is the wrong call

  • It needs new hardware. Cogged belts fit the same sheaves. Synchronous belts need matching toothed sprockets on both shafts, and the federal test notes sheave replacement and a higher first cost.
  • Speed can creep up. A V-belt slips a little, so a fan turns a little under the nominal ratio. With no slip, the fan can turn faster. DOE warns that on centrifugal fans and pumps, flow, pressure and operating cost could rise if slip is removed, so sprockets must be sized for zero slip. Because power climbs with the cube of speed, even a small increase matters; see Fan Laws. The federal test did not recommend them for constant-volume fans with high-torque starts.
  • Shock loads. DOE calls synchronous belts less suited to shock-loaded applications, and says notched belts may be preferable where vibration damping is needed or where sudden torque could shear teeth.
  • Noise and vibration. DOE says synchronous belts are noisier and transfer more vibration because they are stiffer. The federal test saw no significant sound change, so check the specific job.
  • Alignment is less forgiving. Trade press lists misalignment as a cause of extra fatigue and premature failure on synchronous drives, and notes low tension can cause tooth ratcheting. Use the methods in shaft alignment.
  • You lose the slip. Our own reasoning, not a quoted finding: a V-belt that slips under a jam or stall can act as a weak safety valve for the motor and fan, while a belt that cannot slip passes every bit of torque to the shafts and bearings.

When the drive is not a good match, a cogged belt is the low-risk upgrade: same sheaves, same alignment habits, and about 2 percent better by DOE's figure.

Belt wear signs

  • Glazing. Shiny, hard sidewalls mean the belt has been slipping and heating. A belt distributor's troubleshooting guide lists low tension, worn grooves or an overloaded drive as the usual causes.
  • Cracking. ACHR News says belts that run hot harden and crack from the bottom up. The same distributor guide says cracks across the width point to bending stress, such as sheaves that are too small, and cracks along the length to overtension.
  • Squeal under load. ACHR News says squeal with load often means too little tension. A soapy-water spray on the running belts that quiets the noise points at the belts.
  • Dust. Look for belt dust in the bottom of the guard, which ACHR News ties to slipping.
  • Stretch. New V-belts stretch. ACHR News says to retension after about 24 hours and check every six to 12 months. DOE says check every 3 to 6 months after break-in.

Matched sets and multi-groove drives

On a drive with two, three or more grooves, belts share the load. ACHR News says to always replace worn belts as a set and to use belts from the same maker, since belts from different makers cannot be matched. One new belt beside old stretched ones takes more than its share. The same article says sheaves worn more than 1/16 in. along one side of a groove should be replaced, because a dished groove loses grip and uneven wear makes belts act mismatched.

Guards: OSHA 1910.219

The federal rule for mechanical power transmission requires guarding. Pulleys with any part 7 feet or less from the floor or working platform must be guarded. Where both runs of a horizontal belt are 7 feet or less from the floor, the guard extends at least 15 inches above the belt, and where both runs are 42 inches or less from the floor the belt is fully enclosed. Vertical and inclined belts must be enclosed. Put guards back every time, and lock out the unit first (see Lockout, Tagout and Verify Dead). See rooftop unit PM for where belts fit in the schedule. A university preventive-maintenance page lists inspection and replacement of drive belts on fans as a routine task.

Field card: V-Belts vs Cogged and Synchronous Belts on HVAC Fans

Same sheave is easy; new sprockets need a reason.

  • DOE: V-belt 95 percent or more new, notched about 2 points better, synchronous about 98 percent.
  • Cogged belts drop into the same sheaves. Synchronous needs both sprockets.
  • Check fan speed after any swap. No slip can mean more rpm and more power.
  • Poor fit for shock loads and noise-sensitive spaces.
  • Replace multi-groove belts as a set. Guard back on before restart.
