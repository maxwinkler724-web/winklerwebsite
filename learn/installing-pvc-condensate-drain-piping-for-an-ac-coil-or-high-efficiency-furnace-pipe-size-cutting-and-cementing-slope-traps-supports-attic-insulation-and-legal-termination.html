Installing PVC Condensate Drain Piping for an AC Coil or High-Efficiency Furnace

Last updated: 2026-11-04

The drain line looks like the easy part of a new install, and it is where many callbacks start. A cooling coil can shed gallons a day, and a condensing furnace adds its own acidic water. This lesson walks through running PVC from the unit to a legal place for the water to end: sizing, cutting, cementing, falling, trapping, supporting and insulating it.

Which rule wins

The unit's installation manual and your local code both apply, and the stricter one wins. We read city and state code pages and several maker manuals. Codes differ by place and edition, so confirm with your inspector before you rough in. This is general education, not legal advice. Why the trap exists and how deep it must be is covered in AC Coil Condensate Traps, so we only place it here.

Start at the unit connection

Read the drain outlet on the coil pan or furnace and match it. One air handler manual we read says its drain connections are 3/4 inch female pipe thread and that the line should be at least 3/4 inch PVC or equivalent. A Florida city checklist for final mechanical inspections also says the condensate drain must be a minimum of 3/4 inch. Two furnace manuals we read call for 3/4 inch PVC between the furnace and the drain location. Do not shrink the line below the connection, and size up only where the maker or code allows it. Use a threaded adapter that matches the outlet, and do not overtighten plastic threads.

Cut square, deburr and chamfer

A pipe maker's solvent cement bulletin says to cut the pipe at a right angle with a fine-tooth saw and a miter box or guide. A rotary cutter, such as a ratchet cutter, is fine only if its blade is made for plastic, and any ridge it leaves has to be removed. The bulletin then says to smooth the pipe end, because a rough edge can scrape cement out of the socket and cause a leak. In shop terms: deburr inside and out, then put a light chamfer on the outside edge so the pipe slides in without plowing the cement ahead of it. Wipe off dust and moisture before you go on.

Dry fit before you glue

Push the pipe into the dry fitting. The same bulletin describes a good fit as the pipe touching the socket wall roughly one third to two thirds of the way in, snug and not loose. Dry fit the whole run on the floor or in place, mark the pipe and fitting with a pencil line so you can see the quarter turn later, and confirm the slope and the trap position before any cement comes out.

Primer, cement, cure and fumes

  1. Primer. Scrub primer inside the socket, then on the pipe end to the socket depth, until the surface looks softened. The bulletin says primer and cleaner are not interchangeable.
  2. Cement. Apply cement lightly and evenly in the socket and on the pipe while the primer is still wet.
  3. Assemble. Push the pipe in one steady motion with a quarter turn. The bulletin says to finish within about 20 seconds of the last cement application, then hold the joint for about a minute because the pipe can push back out.
  4. Wipe and leave alone. Wipe the excess from the socket mouth. A good joint shows a bead of cement all the way around.
  5. Cure. The bulletin does not give a single number, only that cure time runs from minutes to days depending on temperature, humidity, pipe size and cement type. It tells the reader to use the cement maker's cure times. Read the label on your own can, and do not water-test before that time has passed.

Fumes: the cement and primer labels and their safety data sheets set the ventilation and fire precautions. In an attic or closet, open the space up, bring in fresh air, keep ignition sources away, and cap the cans when you are not using them.

Slope: how much fall

The model mechanical code (IMC), as adopted by two government code pages we read, requires condensate piping to slope at not less than one eighth unit vertical in 12 units horizontal, which is a 1 percent slope, in the direction of discharge. A Florida city checklist also asks for a uniform 1 percent slope. Makers often ask for more. The air handler manual we read says at least 1 inch of drop per 10 feet of run, and two furnace manuals say 1/4 inch per foot. Use whichever number is largest for your unit and jurisdiction, and keep the pipe uniform. A sag holds water and grows slime.

Support the pipe

A Florida city installation-tips sheet lists "strap drain if required" to avoid breakage, which is the point of hangers: a sagging line fails the slope rule between supports. We found no spacing number we could source, so follow the maker, your inspector and the hanger spacing in your local plumbing or mechanical code. Strap loosely enough that the pipe can move a little, and never use a strap to pull a pipe into slope.

Traps, open tees and cleanouts

  • Where a trap is needed. The air handler manual we read calls for a trap as close to the coil as possible, 5 inches deep, with the top of the trap below the bottom of the drain pan, primed with water, and only one trap in the line. A Florida city checklist says condensate drains must be trapped per the maker's requirement. A furnace manual says not to trap the line anywhere except at the trap supplied with the furnace. Never add a second trap.
  • Open tee. One furnace manual requires an open tee near the cooling coil when a coil shares the drain, to relieve plenum pressure so it does not interfere with the furnace trap. Another maker's external trap guide says the drain must end at an open or vented point, and that an air conditioning drain needs its own trap before joining the furnace drain. Install a vent or open tee only where your maker's manual calls for it, and in the place it shows.
  • Cleanout. This one is our own shop practice, not a cited code rule: put a tee with a removable cap or plug at the trap or just after it, so a brush or wet-vac can clear slime without cutting glue joints. Check that it does not conflict with the maker's drawing.

Insulate to stop sweating and freezing

The air handler manual we read says to insulate the drain where it passes through an unconditioned space, then leak-test it. A Florida city sheet lists insulation on horizontal drains in unconditioned space, and a furnace manual says to insulate the trap and all tubing there. The reason is simple: a cold drain line in a hot attic collects dew and drips on the ceiling below. Furnace condensate lines can also freeze, and two furnace manuals say not to route them outside where they may freeze.

Where the water may go

Code pages differ, so these are examples, not a universal rule:

  • One state mechanical code page requires condensate drains not to connect directly to any plumbing drain, waste or vent, and says a sanitary sewer must be reached through an indirect connection under the plumbing code.
  • A state plumbing code page requires condensate to discharge through an indirect waste pipe by an air break or air gap into a waste receptor, which must connect to the building drain, and says residential lavatories cannot receive indirect waste.
  • One city code page says the drain runs to an approved place of disposal and may not discharge into a street or alley so as to cause a nuisance.
  • One state fuel-gas program form allows a pit or French drain at least 24 by 24 by 24 inches, filled with 1 inch washed rock, at least 30 inches from the foundation.

So a pipe taped into a sewer line, or dumped onto a walkway, is not a legal ending. If the nearest legal place is far, consider a pump; see Condensate Pump Not Working. Also plan the backup: Secondary Drain Pans and Float Switches.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not from a code or maker. Assume the drain outlet on the unit sits 6 inches above the point where the pipe must end, and the pipe runs 30 feet horizontally. We ignore the trap height and fitting lengths to keep it simple.

  1. Code minimum: 1/8 inch per foot. Fall = 30 ft x 1/8 in/ft = 3.75 in.
  2. Available drop is 6 in, so it fits with 6 - 3.75 = 2.25 in to spare.
  3. If the unit's maker asks for 1/4 inch per foot, fall = 30 x 1/4 = 7.5 in. That is more than 6 in by 1.5 in, so the run does not fit as drawn.
  4. If the maker asks for 1 inch per 10 feet, fall = 3 x 1 in = 3 in, which fits.

The answer: the stricter of the sources decides. Here we would shorten the run by at least 6 feet (7.5 in at 1/4 inch per foot needs the run to be no more than 6 / 0.25 = 24 ft), move the termination lower, or look at a pump.

Field card: PVC condensate drain

Size to the outlet, fall every foot, trap once, end it legally.

  • Pipe at least 3/4 inch PVC, matched to the unit outlet.
  • Square cut, deburr, chamfer, dry fit, primer, cement, quarter turn, hold about a minute.
  • Cure time and fume precautions come from the cement label.
  • Fall: code is 1/8 inch per 12 inches (1 percent); use the larger maker number if there is one.
  • One trap, as the maker shows; open tee only where called for.
  • Insulate in unconditioned space; keep furnace lines out of freezing air.
  • End at an approved place; no direct sewer tie, use an air gap or air break where required.
