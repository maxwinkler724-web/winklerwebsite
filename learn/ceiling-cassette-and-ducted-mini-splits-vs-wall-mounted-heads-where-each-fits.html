Ceiling Cassette and Ducted Mini-Splits vs Wall-Mounted Heads: Where Each Fits

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The outdoor unit of a mini-split is only half the choice. The indoor unit decides how the room looks, how the air moves, how the water leaves and who can reach the filter. This lesson compares the wall head, the ceiling cassette and the slim ducted unit, using numbers from maker spec sheets and manuals.

Check the maker's sheet

Check the maker's sheet. The figures below come from specific models named in the Sources. Other models, and newer revisions of the same models, differ. For any real job, the installation manual for the exact unit governs. For the basics of what a mini-split is, see Mini-Split Basics.

The types in one pass

  • High-wall head. The familiar box high on a wall. It needs a wall, a short line-set run through that wall and a drain that can slope away. It is the easiest to install and the easiest to see. One Fujitsu General (Australia) help page suggests about 1.8 m (roughly 6 ft) above the floor, on a wall with suitable access for drainage.
  • Ceiling cassette. A square box recessed in the ceiling with a flat grille. Mitsubishi's PLA-A12EA7 is 33-1/16 inches square and 10-5/32 inches high, with a 37-13/32 inch grille. Carrier's 40MBCQ36 is 33.07 inches square and 9.65 inches high with a 37.4 inch panel, and the grille is sold separately.
  • Slim ducted (concealed) unit. A low box above a ceiling or in a soffit that pushes air through short ducts to grilles. Mitsubishi's SEZ-KD18NA4 is 7-7/8 inches high, 46-7/8 inches wide and 27-9/16 inches deep, hung from M10 bolts.
  • Floor console. Sits low on an exterior wall, like a baseboard heater. Not covered in the sources we read here, so check a maker sheet for one.

What each one asks of the building

Ceiling space. A cassette is mostly a depth question. The Mitsubishi PLFY-EP manual (a commercial cassette) says the panel-to-ceiling gap must be 10 to 15 mm and the ceiling opening must fall within 860 to 910 mm square (about 33-7/8 to 35-13/16 inches). Since the box is about 33 inches wide, it is wider than a single typical joist bay, so framing is often involved. That is a structural question for a qualified person, not a guess. The same manual says to mount the unit at least 2.4 m (94-1/2 inches) above the floor.

Condensate. Wall heads drain by gravity. Cassettes and ducted units often have a built-in lift pump because they sit high and the drain may have to climb. The table shows what the sheets say. For leak causes and slope, see Mini Split Leaking Water.

Built-in condensate lift, as published
Unit (type)Published lift
Mitsubishi SEZ-KD18NA4 (slim ducted)21-11/16 in
Mitsubishi PEAD-A18AA7 (ducted)27-9/16 in (700 mm)

Compare those numbers: the lift is measured against the unit, so the vertical rise you plan above the drain outlet must stay under the published figure with margin. Some sheets also limit the riser itself. A Daikin FBQ manual says drain raising pipes go less than 250 mm high, and an LG cassette manual says the riser sits no more than 300 mm from the unit and the horizontal drain slopes 1/50 to 1/100. Neither number is a general rule. Use your unit's manual.

Return path and filter. A cassette pulls room air up through its own grille, so the filter lives behind the grille and is reached from below. On a ducted unit Mitsubishi says that if you add an inlet duct you remove the supplied filter and put a field-supplied filter on the suction side, and it lists a filter box (FBL1-3 with MERV 8 filters) as an option. That filter and the grille you add are extra pressure the blower must overcome. Mitsubishi also says to keep service space under the unit and provide an access door at the marked position. A beautiful ceiling with no access panel is a callback waiting to happen. For habits that keep filters clean, see Mini-Split Cleaning and Maintenance Habits.

Static pressure: the ducted unit's real limit

A ducted mini-split blower is small. Its rating is an external static pressure (ESP), the resistance of ducts, grilles and filters outside the box that it can push against. The Mitsubishi SEZ-KD18NA4 submittal lists four settings: 0.02, 0.06, 0.14 and 0.20 inch of water column (about 5, 15, 35 and 50 Pa), with 0.06 as the rated point. A higher setting makes the fan work harder, and the maker publishes fan curves for each. Mitsubishi's PEAD-A18AA7 lists settings up to 0.60 inch, so the same capacity class can differ by model. If you need the idea behind these numbers first, read Airflow and Static Pressure Basics, and for how a duct is sized see Duct Sizing Basics.

Worked example: does a simple duct fit the SEZ-KD18NA4?

An ACHR News article explains that the design friction rate comes from the blower's available static pressure divided by the total effective length, which is straight duct plus fitting equivalent lengths. Energy Vanguard notes that many people simply assume 0.1 inch per 100 ft. We will use that 0.10 as our rate. The fitting and grille losses below are made-up values for teaching, so use the numbers from your own duct calculator or the maker's data.

  1. Straight duct: 20 ft supply + 5 ft return = 25 ft.
  2. Fittings: 4 elbows, each assumed equal to 15 ft of duct = 60 ft.
  3. Total effective length = 25 + 60 = 85 ft.
  4. Duct loss = 85 × 0.10 / 100 = 0.085 in. w.c.
  5. Grilles and filter (assumed): supply register 0.03 + return grille with field filter 0.04 = 0.07 in. w.c.
  6. Total external resistance = 0.085 + 0.07 = 0.155 in. w.c.

Compare with the unit's settings. The rated point of 0.06 is far too low: this duct is about 2.6 times larger (0.155 / 0.06). The 0.14 setting is short by 0.015. The 0.20 setting leaves 0.20 − 0.155 = 0.045 in. w.c. of margin, which is about 22 percent of the setting. So the design only works on the top setting, and you must check the airflow that setting delivers on the fan curve. If you cut the design to two elbows, the effective length becomes 25 + 30 = 55 ft, the duct loss is 0.055, the total is 0.055 + 0.07 = 0.125, and the 0.14 setting then covers it. The lesson: fewer fittings and a shorter run buy back real capacity.

Throw, placement and the comfort tradeoff

Wall heads blow across the room from a high spot, so furniture rarely blocks them, but the head is always visible. A cassette hides in the ceiling and throws air in several directions. Mitsubishi's PLFY manual limits its maximum ceiling height by setting: for the small sizes, 2.5 m on silent, 2.7 m on standard and 3.5 m on the high-ceiling setting. Throw is limited, so a cassette in a tall room may need that setting. A ducted unit can serve two or three rooms from one box, at the price of duct losses, grilles in the ceiling and the access door.

In short: wall heads win on cost and simplicity, cassettes on a clean ceiling look in an open space, and slim ducted units when you want only grilles showing and have a few inches of ceiling space and a short duct.

Multi-zone pairing limits

Not every indoor type goes with every outdoor unit. Mitsubishi lists the SEZ-KD18NA4 for its MXZ-B multi-zone systems only. The MXZ-3C30NA sheet requires at least two indoor units, a minimum of 12,000 Btu/h installed, and no more than 130 percent connected capacity. It allows 82 ft of line per indoor unit and 230 ft in total. It has separate performance tables for ducted and non-ducted combinations. A longer run to a cassette uses up part of that budget, so tie the choice to the line-set plan in Mini-Split Installation Basics. If you are choosing between systems for a house without ducts, see Adding AC to a House Without Ducts.

Field card: Cassette, Ducted or Wall Head

Space, water, air, access.

  • Wall head: needs a wall and a drain that slopes down. Simplest, most visible.
  • Cassette: needs ceiling depth near 10 in. plus panel, and an opening of about 34 to 36 in. square. Check framing.
  • Slim ducted: needs about 8 in. of height for the box (SEZ-KD18) and an access door under it.
  • Pump lift on the sheets above: about 22 to 33 in. Stay under your unit's figure.
  • Ducted ESP: add up duct, fittings, grilles and filter, then match a setting with margin.
  • Multi-zone: check connected capacity, line limits and which indoor models the outdoor unit accepts.
