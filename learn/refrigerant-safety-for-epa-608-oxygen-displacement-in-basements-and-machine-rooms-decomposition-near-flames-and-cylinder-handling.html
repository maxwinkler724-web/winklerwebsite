Refrigerant Safety for EPA 608: Oxygen Displacement in Basements and Machine Rooms, Decomposition Near Flames and Cylinder Handling

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The EPA 608 core exam has a safety section, and its questions come back to a short list of ideas: refrigerant vapor can crowd out the air you breathe, heat can turn refrigerant into poisons, the wrong gas can wreck a pressure test, and cylinders are pressure vessels. This lesson puts those ideas in one study sheet and works one basement example with real arithmetic. For the general safety basics, start with Refrigerant Safety Basics; for the full exposure picture, including heart effects, see Refrigerant Exposure Hazards for Techs.

What EPA says the safety section covers

EPA publishes a list of test topics for Section 608 certification. Under safety, it names the risks of exposure to refrigerant, with oxygen deprivation, cardiac effects, frostbite and long-term hazards as examples. It also lists the decomposition products of refrigerants at high temperatures, the contrast between reusable and disposable cylinders (never refill the disposable kind), the risk of filling a cylinder more than 80 percent full, and the use of nitrogen rather than oxygen or compressed air for leak detection, with a pressure regulator and relief valve. The rest of this page follows that list.

Heavy vapor, thin air

NIOSH lists the relative gas density of the older refrigerant R-12 as 4.2, with air at 1, and lists asphyxia among its symptoms. Newer refrigerants are lighter molecules than R-12 but still heavy compared with air. Using a molar mass of 100 g/mol for an R-134a-type vapor and about 29 g/mol for air (both our own round assumptions), the vapor is about 3.4 times as heavy as air. Heavy vapor does not rise and vent. It runs downhill into basements, pits, stairwells, crawlspaces and the low corners of machine rooms, and it can stay there. A CDC card on another heavy, low-collecting gas, hydrogen fluoride, makes the same point for sewers, basements and tanks: vapors can collect in low, poorly ventilated spaces where dangerous concentrations build up quickly.

OSHA's confined space rule defines an oxygen deficient atmosphere as one with less than 19.5 percent oxygen by volume, and it counts oxygen below 19.5 percent or above 23.5 percent as a hazardous atmosphere. Normal air is about 20.9 percent, so the safety margin is smaller than most people expect: only 1.4 points of oxygen. Do not trust your nose. Refrigerant vapor can displace oxygen with no strong smell, and the CDC card above warns specifically that odor should not be depended on as a warning.

What low oxygen does to a person: a CDC/ATSDR table (a historical 2001 document written about landfill gas, which we use only for the general body effects) lists deteriorated night vision around 17 percent, very poor muscular coordination and rapid fatigue at 14 to 16 percent, nausea and unconsciousness at 6 to 10 percent, and death in minutes below 6 percent. The practical sign is that the victim often does not notice the problem. Poor judgment and clumsiness come before collapse, so a partner who sees a tech acting oddly in a low space should treat it as an emergency, not a joke. Never go in after a collapsed person without air testing and rescue equipment; see Confined Spaces in HVAC Work for the testing order and the storage room lesson for the same oxygen-depletion idea with a different gas.

Our own worked example: 30 lb into a basement

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a measured event. Assumptions: all 30 lb of an R-134a-type refrigerant escapes and becomes vapor; molar mass 100 g/mol (our round figure); room conditions of 70 F (294.3 K) and 1 atmosphere; the vapor behaves as an ideal gas; the basement is 20 ft by 30 ft by 8 ft with no ventilation running.

  1. Mass: 30 lb × 453.6 g/lb = 13,608 g.
  2. Moles: 13,608 ÷ 100 = 136.1 mol.
  3. Volume at room conditions: 136.1 mol × 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K) × 294.3 K = 3,286 L. One cubic foot is 28.32 L, so that is 3,286 ÷ 28.32 = 116 ft³. (Density check: 13,608 g ÷ 3,286 L = 4.14 g/L, about 0.26 lb/ft³.)
  4. Room volume: 20 × 30 × 8 = 4,800 ft³.
  5. Fully mixed fraction: 116 ÷ 4,800 = 0.0242, or 2.4 percent.
  6. Oxygen: 20.9 × (1 − 0.0242) = 20.9 × 0.9758 = 20.4 percent. That is above OSHA's 19.5 percent line, so the well-mixed average passes.
  7. For scale, the fraction that would reach 19.5 percent is 1 − 19.5 ÷ 20.9 = 0.067. In this room that is 0.067 × 4,800 = 322 ft³, about 83 lb. A full mix of 30 lb is far from the line.

Now the same 116 ft³ settling low, which is what heavy vapor does. The floor is 20 × 30 = 600 ft². A pure layer would be only 116 ÷ 600 = 0.19 ft, about 2.3 inches deep, with almost no oxygen left in it. More realistically the vapor mixes with the air near the floor. If it mixes into the bottom 2 ft (600 × 2 = 1,200 ft³), the fraction is 116 ÷ 1,200 = 0.097, and oxygen is 20.9 × 0.903 = 18.9 percent, below 19.5. If it mixes into only the bottom 1 ft (600 ft³), the fraction is 0.193 and oxygen is 20.9 × 0.807 = 16.9 percent, close to the 17 percent level where the CDC/ATSDR table lists deteriorated night vision.

Why the layer matters: an average is a fair picture only if the air is stirred. Heavy vapor released at floor level spreads out along the floor first and mixes upward slowly, so the concentration depends on height. A tech standing at the stairs has their face about 5 ft above the floor and may feel fine, while a tech reaching under a unit, kneeling at a floor drain or climbing into a pit is in the thin layer. The numbers also ignore any partitions or dead-end corners, which would concentrate the vapor further. This is one reason machine rooms with large charges use detectors and ventilation; see Refrigerant Machine Rooms.

Heat and flame: decomposition products

A CDC publication on fluorocarbons and halons says they are only mild irritants as parent compounds but, when heated, may produce more potent irritant gases and vapors such as phosgene, hydrochloric acid and hydrofluoric acid. It adds that this may happen in a fire or if a refrigeration line is cut by a welding torch or electric arc. That matches the EPA exam topic on decomposition products at high temperatures. For the exam, remember the direction: heat plus refrigerant makes products that are more toxic and acidic than the refrigerant you started with.

The CDC emergency card for hydrogen fluoride describes irritation and burns of the nose and throat, fluid in the lungs, and symptoms that may be delayed 12 to 36 hours. The NIOSH pocket guide lists 30 ppm as its immediately dangerous level. The study-sheet rules are simple: recover the refrigerant and purge before brazing, keep open flame and hot work away from a leaking system, and leave at once if you smell something sharp or your eyes and throat burn near a torch. Feeling fine after leaving is not clearance, because of the delay. Read Hot Work Permits and Fire Watch for the work routine.

Pressure testing: nitrogen, not oxygen or air

EPA's exam topics tell you to use nitrogen rather than oxygen or compressed air for leak detection, and to use a pressure regulator and relief valve with it. The rule to memorize is short: never pressurize a refrigeration system with oxygen or compressed air, and never put a cylinder of nitrogen on a system without a regulator. We did not find a government page that explains the reason in detail, so we give the rule as EPA states it. See Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods for the procedure.

Cylinder handling

  • Upright and secured. NIOSH's compressed gas checklist cites the requirement that cylinders be stored upright and immobilized by chains or other means. OSHA's construction rule says cylinders shall be secured in an upright position at all times, with an exception while being hoisted or carried.
  • Valve protection. OSHA's construction rule says valve protection caps stay in place and secured, and are never used to lift cylinders. The NIOSH checklist asks for valve covers on whenever a cylinder is not in use.
  • Heat limit. The NIOSH checklist asks whether cylinders are always kept below 125 F, away from radiators and other heat sources, and says no flame may touch them. For a refrigerant cylinder, the limit stamped or printed on the cylinder and its label is the one that governs, so read it. Heat raises pressure, and a hot, full cylinder can rupture.
  • Fill limit. EPA's exam topics flag the risk of filling a cylinder more than 80 percent full. Liquid expands as it warms, and the empty space is where it goes. See the recovery machine lesson for scale-based filling.
  • Never refill a disposable cylinder. EPA's exam topics say to never refill the disposable type. Refillable and recovery cylinders are the ones built for reuse. Our lesson on disposable cylinders covers the markings and the rules.
  • Ventilated, dry storage. OSHA's rule asks for a protected, well-ventilated, dry location, away from open flame and hot metal. If a cylinder leaks and tightening the valve does not fix it, the NIOSH checklist says to tag it unserviceable, move it outdoors to a ventilated spot and contact the supplier.

PPE for liquid contact

NIOSH lists frostbite from liquid contact for R-12 and gives frostbite treatment as first aid for skin and eye contact. Liquid refrigerant boils far below freezing at room pressure, so it pulls heat out of skin and eyes fast. Wear eye protection and gloves whenever you connect, disconnect or purge hoses. OSHA's general PPE rule requires protective equipment for eyes, face, head and extremities when a hazard assessment says it is needed. Keep your face and hands out of the line of any release. Gloves do not stop a vapor hazard; no glove or goggle protects you from low oxygen. See Work Gloves for HVAC Techs.

Field card: EPA 608 safety points

Heavy vapor pools low. Heat makes poison. Nitrogen only. Cylinders upright, cool, never overfilled.

  • Vapor is heavier than air: basements, pits and machine rooms trap it.
  • OSHA oxygen deficient line: below 19.5 percent. Normal air: about 20.9.
  • Our example: 30 lb in 4,800 cu ft mixes to 20.4 percent, but a 2 ft layer drops to 18.9 and a 1 ft layer to 16.9.
  • Flame or a hot surface can make irritant acid gases from refrigerant. Recover first.
  • Pressure test with nitrogen through a regulator and relief valve, never oxygen or compressed air.
  • Cylinders: upright, secured, caps on, away from heat, no more than 80 percent full, never refill disposable ones.
  • Liquid contact means frostbite: eye protection and gloves.
