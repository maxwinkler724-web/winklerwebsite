Gas Furnace and Stove Flame Color: Blue vs Yellow, What It Means

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you are searching "yellow flame in furnace," you probably looked at your burners and saw something other than a calm blue flame. This page explains what makers and gas utilities say a normal flame looks like, what a yellow, lazy or lifting flame can point to, and when to stop looking and call someone. It ends with a short technician section on why flame color is only a first clue.

What a normal flame looks like

Gas utilities and manufacturers agree on the basic picture. New Jersey Natural Gas says natural gas equipment should have a clear, blue flame and that a yellow or orange flame may indicate a problem. Jackson Energy Authority, a Tennessee utility, says a healthy flame is mostly blue, sometimes with a tiny yellow tip.

Manufacturers are a little more specific. The Goodman installation instructions for its MVC80 and CVC80 furnaces say to look at the flames with the burner compartment door in place. They should be stable, quiet, soft and blue. Dust may cause orange tips, but the flames must not be yellow. They should reach straight out from the burners without curling, floating or lifting off, and they must not touch the sides of the heat exchanger tubes. For a water heater, the Bradford White service manual says the main burner should light smoothly from the pilot and burn with a blue flame with a minimum of yellow tips. A Reznor furnace conversion-kit sheet allows "some light yellow streaks" near the outer part of a blue flame. So wording differs by product. The label and manual for your own appliance are the reference.

Yellow, lazy or lifting: what can be behind it

Jackson Energy Authority says a yellow or orange flame suggests the mix of air and gas is off. It adds that this is often temporary and may come from dust or a dirty burner that needs cleaning, while a flame that stays yellow or orange may be more serious because incomplete burning can produce too much carbon monoxide (CO). That fits the Goodman wording about dust and orange tips.

A gas log heater manual we read lists three yellow-flame causes: not enough air, low inlet gas pressure or a defective gas regulator. It also says blocked burner holes and blocked air holes create soot. The Bradford White chart for a pilot that goes out periodically lists a clogged or incorrect vent and too little combustion air among probable causes. You can see why these are tech jobs. Vent and air problems also spill exhaust into the room. See Combustion and CO Safety Basics and Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained.

One caution on stoves and logs: not every flame is meant to be blue. The vent-free gas log heater manual says its slotted burner is supposed to show a yellow flame. That is a decorative appliance with its own manual. A range top or oven burner is not that, and the utilities above treat yellow or orange on a gas appliance as something to have checked. Read your own manual.

Soot is a stronger warning than color

A flame that looks off is one thing. Black soot on or around an appliance, on the burners, or at the vent is another. The gas log manual ties blocked ports to soot, and the Goodman manual lists soot among the results of improper installation. Treat soot, a persistent yellow flame, or a change in how the flame looks as a reason to shut the appliance off if you can do so safely using the controls the label describes, and call a technician.

Carbon monoxide and alarms

You cannot see or smell CO. The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) says to put battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, and that interconnected alarms are best. If you suspect CO poisoning, CPSC says to get outside to fresh air right away and then call 911. EPA says an alarm is a backup, not a replacement for proper installation, use and maintenance of fuel-burning appliances, and repeats the recommendation for a professional inspection of furnaces, stoves, water heaters and other appliances. Flu-like symptoms that ease when you leave the house are a warning sign that Jackson Energy Authority lists. For what to do when an alarm sounds, read Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

What to do, in order

  1. Smell gas or hear an alarm? Leave and call from outside. See Furnace and AC Smells: What Each Odor Means.
  2. Brief orange tips on a first fire-up of the season? Dust can do that, per Goodman. Watch it. If it does not clear, treat it as a persistent problem.
  3. Yellow, lazy or lifting flame that stays? Call a qualified technician or your gas utility. NJ Natural Gas says to call for service right away if you think there is a problem, and Jackson Energy Authority says to call a professional.
  4. Do not adjust the gas valve or regulator. Goodman says only minor adjustments are made at the regulator, and Lennox says only a licensed professional should service the furnace and that force or attempted repair may cause fire or explosion. Those are not homeowner tasks.
  5. A pilot that keeps going out is a related but separate problem: Pilot Light Won't Stay Lit.

Tech section: flame is a clue, not a measurement

An apprentice should treat flame color as a quick visual screen. The manuals above describe how the flame should look, but the pass or fail for CO comes from instruments. The Goodman installation manual we read gives no numeric CO limit, and the Lennox user manual sends the reader to service literature for combustion values. A combustion analyzer reads O2, CO and stack temperature, and corrects CO to an air-free value so readings compare fairly. An ACHR News article by Jim Bergmann says CO above 400 ppm air-free is not permissible and that the goal is to keep flue CO below 100 ppm air-free. It also says a burner with rising CO is far more dangerous than a stable reading. Because a look at the flame cannot measure CO, the numbers decide. Always use the limits in the unit's own literature and your local code. See Combustion Analyzer Readings: O2, CO2, CO Air-Free and Excess Air, and for the ignition side, Flame Safeguard and Ignition Basics.

Manifold pressure is the other number that shapes the flame. Goodman gives, for these furnaces on natural gas, a low-stage range of 1.6 to 2.2 inches of water column (nominal 1.9) and a high-stage range of 3.2 to 3.8 (nominal 3.5), and for propane 5.7 to 6.3 low and 9.7 to 10.3 high. Bradford White says its water heater manifold pressure is not adjustable and the control is replaced if the reading is out of range. Trane's UH1-H and DH1-H guide gives a natural gas adjustment range of 3.0 to 3.7 inches and a table with 3.5 for most models, with different values on a few. So the target is model-specific; use the data plate and manual.

Worked example: reading the manifold pressure against the spec

A tech checks a Goodman MVC80 furnace on natural gas at high stage and reads 3.0 inches w.c. on the manometer. The manual's high-stage natural gas range is 3.2 to 3.8, nominal 3.5.

  1. Compare to the minimum: 3.2 - 3.0 = 0.2 inch w.c. below the bottom of the range.
  2. Compare to nominal: 3.5 - 3.0 = 0.5 inch w.c. low.
  3. Express as a share of nominal: 0.5 / 3.5 = 0.143, about 14 percent low.
  4. Check against the manual's tolerance: it says final manifold pressure cannot vary by more than 0.3 inch w.c. for natural gas. 3.5 - 0.3 = 3.2, the same bottom of range, and 0.5 is more than 0.3, so the reading fails.

The result is a low-pressure flame problem to correct by the manual's procedure, then confirm with a combustion analysis. The same reading of 3.0 would be fine on a Trane unit with a 3.0 listed setting, which is why you check the model first. Propane units are different again: a nominal 10.0 is almost three times the natural gas figure.

Field card: Gas Furnace and Stove Flame Color: Blue vs Yellow, What It Means

Blue, steady and quiet is the goal. A persistent change is a call, not a DIY.

  • Utilities: natural gas flame should be blue; yellow or orange may be a problem.
  • Goodman: stable, quiet, soft, blue; dust may give orange tips, never yellow; no curling, floating or lifting.
  • Soot, a flame that stays yellow, or an alarm: shut off if safe, get out if the alarm sounds, call.
  • CO alarms on every level and outside sleeping areas (CPSC).
  • Never adjust the gas valve at home.
  • Tech: analyzer, not color. Air-free CO over 400 ppm is not permissible (ACHR); aim under 100.
  • Goodman natural gas high stage: 3.2 to 3.8 in. w.c., nominal 3.5.
