HVAC Pump Types and Where Each Fits: End Suction, Inline, Split Case, Turbine, Circulator

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Walk into a mechanical room and you will see pumps that look nothing alike: a small round body bolted into a pipe, a big motor on a steel base, a long can standing in a sump. They all move water with a spinning impeller, but each shape exists for a reason. This lesson sorts the main types, says where each one fits, and gives you a few quick ways to tell them apart on a job.

The five families you will meet

End-suction pumps. Water enters straight into the eye of the impeller from the end of the pump and leaves out of the top or side of the casing. A state training chapter calls this style the most common centrifugal pump and says it comes close-coupled or frame-mounted. In a close-coupled pump the impeller sits on the motor shaft, so there is no coupling to align. In a frame-mounted (base-mounted) pump a separate bearing frame carries the shaft, a coupling joins it to the motor, and both sit on a common steel base. A university design guideline requires the back pull-out design for base-mounted end-suction pumps: you unbolt the rear assembly and withdraw the impeller, seal and bearings without disturbing the suction and discharge piping.

Inline pumps. Suction and discharge connections sit on one straight line, so the pump hangs in the pipe like a valve. A university specification lists close-coupled and separately coupled versions, horizontal and vertical. They need little floor space, which is why they show up in tight rooms and on risers. That specification requires a vertical inline pump to be suspended independently of the piping, so the pipe does not carry the weight. A different university guideline says flexible connectors are typically not required on inline pumps; follow the maker and the project documents.

Horizontal split-case double-suction pumps. The casing splits along the shaft centerline and water enters both sides of the impeller. Lift the top half of the casing and the rotating assembly comes out with the piping still connected. A university design guideline says this design lets the impeller be serviced without disturbing the piping, and the state training chapter notes split-case pumps are commonly found as fire and circulation pumps. They are the large central plant pumps.

Vertical turbine pumps. A stack of impeller bowls hangs below the floor, with a long shaft inside the discharge column and the motor on top. A university extension publication on deep-well turbines says the bowls must stay submerged while pumping and the shaft may be oil or water lubricated. In HVAC you find this style where the water is below the pump floor: a cooling tower basin or sump, a well, or a tank. Submersible turbines flip the arrangement and put the motor under the bowls, so no long shaft is needed.

Small wet-rotor circulators. These are the compact pumps on residential and small commercial hot-water loops. Our own plain description, not taken from the sources below: the motor rotor runs in the system water, so there is no shaft seal to leak, and the pump is usually replaced as a unit rather than rebuilt. They are made for low flow and modest head.

How they differ at a glance

  • Flow and head range. A federal pump-selection fact sheet says the first step is knowing the required flow and total system head. One university specification caps base-mounted end-suction and inline pumps at 500 gpm and calls for double-suction split-case pumps above that. That is one owner's rule, not a law of nature; makers publish their own ranges.
  • Footprint. Inline pumps and circulators are smallest. Frame-mounted end-suction pumps need a base and room to withdraw the rear assembly. Split-case pumps need the most floor area and headroom to lift the cover. Vertical turbines need clear height above the sump to pull the column.
  • Maintenance access. Back pull-out for frame-mounted end-suction, open the casing for split-case, pull the column for a turbine, replace the unit for a wet-rotor circulator. One specification asks for enough space between pumps, including pipe and valves, for lubrication and for replacing seals and motors.
  • Alignment. Close-coupled and wet-rotor units have no coupling to align. Frame-mounted and split-case pumps do. One guideline wants base-mounted pumps laser aligned, typically at factory start-up, and a specification wants a preliminary alignment before grouting. See the shaft alignment lesson linked below.
  • Seals. One university guideline requires mechanical seals on all pumps except fire pumps, which use stuffing boxes. The state training chapter explains that packing and mechanical seals do the same job of controlling leakage at the stuffing box. Our shaft seals lesson covers both.

Suction piping: the straight run

An end-suction or split-case impeller wants calm, even flow at its eye. An elbow right at the inlet throws the water to one side and starves part of the impeller. One university guideline asks for a straight run of not less than 10 pipe diameters in the direction of flow ahead of the pump and says to use a suction diffuser if space does not allow it. A different specification asks for at least 5 diameters of straight pipe on the suction and discharge, with long-radius elbows within 10 diameters of the suction connection. The numbers differ because they are different owners' rules, so follow the pump maker and the project documents. The state training chapter also prefers gate or ball valves on the suction side because butterfly valves cause high head loss. Our suction diffuser lesson covers the fitting that shortens this run.

Our own arithmetic for a 6 inch suction pipe (an assumed size): 5 diameters is 30 inches and 10 diameters is 60 inches of straight pipe.

Telling them apart on a job

  1. Is the pump body bolted directly to the motor with no coupling? That is close-coupled (end-suction or inline) or a small circulator.
  2. Is there a coupling guard and a common steel base? Frame-mounted end-suction or split-case.
  3. Does the casing have a horizontal joint with a row of bolts along the middle and pipe connections on opposite sides? That is a split-case double-suction pump.
  4. Do the suction and discharge flanges line up straight across the pump? Inline.
  5. Is the motor on a floor plate with a column going down into a sump or well? Vertical turbine.
  6. Is the unit small, with a motor can and no seal housing, on a hot-water loop? A wet-rotor circulator.

Always read the nameplate for flow, head and speed, and lock out before opening any casing.

Worked example (our own): power for 600 gpm at 70 ft

Our own example, with assumptions: clean water at a specific gravity of 1.0, 600 gpm, 70 ft of total head, and a pump efficiency of 75 percent. The efficiency is our assumption; a university extension publication gives 65 to 80 percent as the typical range for pumps, so 75 percent is plausible. The constant 3,960 also appears in a national laboratory report on pump power.

  1. Water horsepower: whp = gpm × ft ÷ 3,960 = 600 × 70 ÷ 3,960 = 42,000 ÷ 3,960 = 10.6 hp.
  2. Brake horsepower: bhp = whp ÷ efficiency = 10.6 ÷ 0.75 = 14.1 hp.
  3. Check: 14.1 × 0.75 = 10.6, and 10.6 × 3,960 ÷ 70 = 600. The numbers agree.
  4. Shaft power in kilowatts: 14.1 × 0.746 = 10.5 kW. The same laboratory report uses 0.746 kW per brake horsepower.

The motor has to be rated above 14.1 hp at the operating point, and motor losses come on top. At 600 gpm this duty is above the 500 gpm cap in one owner's end-suction rule, so a double-suction split-case pump is a natural candidate there; another owner might choose differently. Where the pump actually runs is set by its curve and the system curve; see the pump curves lesson.

Field card: HVAC Pump Types and Where Each Fits: End Suction, Inline, Split Case, Turbine, Circulator

Match the pump to the room, the flow and the way you will service it.

  • End-suction: most common; close-coupled or frame-mounted; back pull-out on frame-mounted.
  • Inline: connections in a straight line; small footprint; support the weight apart from the pipe.
  • Split-case: lift the top casing half; large central plant flows.
  • Vertical turbine: bowls submerged in a sump, well or basin; motor on top.
  • Wet-rotor circulator: small loops; replaced as a unit.
  • Suction: straight run ahead of the eye; 5 to 10 diameters in the guidelines we read.
  • whp = gpm × ft ÷ 3,960; bhp = whp ÷ efficiency.
