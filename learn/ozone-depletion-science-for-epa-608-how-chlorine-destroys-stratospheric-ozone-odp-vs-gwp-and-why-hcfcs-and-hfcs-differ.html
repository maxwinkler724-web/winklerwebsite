Ozone Depletion Science for EPA 608: How Chlorine Destroys Ozone, ODP vs GWP, and Why HCFCs and HFCs Differ

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The EPA 608 core exam asks about ozone in a few short questions, but the answers make more sense once you can picture the chemistry. This lesson covers the science: where the ozone layer sits, how chlorine gets up there, why one atom does so much damage, and why ODP and GWP are two separate scales that you should never mix up.

Where the ozone layer is and what it blocks

EPA places the ozone layer in the stratosphere, about 9 to 18 miles (15 to 30 km) above the surface. NOAA's assessment describes the highest ozone concentrations as lying between about 15 and 35 km, so the exact edges depend on who is counting. The point for the exam is the same: it is far above the air we breathe and the weather we see. EPA says the layer protects the Earth from most of the sun's UV-B, which NOAA defines as wavelengths of 280 to 315 nm. EPA links extra UV-B at the ground to skin cancer, cataracts, and harm to some crops and sea life.

How chlorine gets there and gets freed

CFCs and HCFCs are very stable in the lower atmosphere, which is part of why they worked well as refrigerants. EPA says that once an ozone-depleting substance is emitted at the surface, it can take two to five years to be carried up into the stratosphere. There, intense UV light breaks the molecule apart and releases chlorine. NOAA puts it the same way: CFCs break down in the upper atmosphere and become a major source of chlorine in the stratosphere. This is the reason the venting prohibition exists. A cupful of refrigerant let go in a basement is not gone; it is on a slow trip upward. For the rules side, see the venting prohibition lesson.

The catalytic cycle: one atom, many ozone molecules

Once a free chlorine atom (Cl) is loose, NOAA lists two reactions as the main cycle at middle and tropical latitudes:

  • Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂ (chlorine takes an oxygen atom from ozone)
  • ClO + O → Cl + O₂ (the chlorine monoxide hands that oxygen to a free oxygen atom and the chlorine is back)

NOAA gives the net result as one ozone molecule and one oxygen atom turned into two ordinary oxygen molecules. The chlorine comes out the same as it went in, so it acts as a catalyst: it speeds up the destruction without being consumed. That is why a tiny amount matters. EPA says one chlorine atom can destroy over 100,000 ozone molecules before it is removed from the stratosphere, and NOAA says many thousands. Both agree on the idea; the exact count depends on conditions, so on the exam remember "thousands of ozone molecules per chlorine atom."

Why HCFCs have a lower ODP than CFCs

HCFCs still contain chlorine, so they still destroy stratospheric ozone. What changes is the added hydrogen. NOAA's monitoring lab explains that the hydrogen lets the molecule react with hydroxyl (OH) in the lower atmosphere, giving it a shorter atmospheric lifetime. NOAA's lifetime table shows the effect: HCFC-22 at 12 years, against 52 years for CFC-11 and 102 years for CFC-12. More of an HCFC is destroyed down low, so less chlorine reaches the stratosphere. Less chlorine means a lower ozone depletion potential.

What ODP and GWP numbers mean

EPA defines ODP as the ratio of a chemical's impact on ozone compared with the impact of a similar mass of CFC-11, so CFC-11 is 1 by definition. EPA's table lists CFC-12 at 1 (Montreal Protocol value) or 0.82 (a 2011 international ozone assessment), and HCFC-22 at 0.055 or 0.04. EPA states that HFCs have zero ODP because they contain no chlorine.

GWP is a different scale. EPA defines it as the warming caused by a substance compared with a similar mass of carbon dioxide, and the usual time frame is 100 years. CO2 is 1. EPA's reference values (from the 2007 international climate assessment) include HFC-134a at 1,430, HFC-32 at 675, and HFC-125 at 3,500, while HCFC-22 is 1,810 in EPA's greenhouse gas inventory annex. Blends are calculated: EPA lists R-410A at 2,088 and R-404A at 3,922. Newer assessments give slightly different numbers, so always say which table you used.

So an HFC can score 0 on ODP and still be in the thousands on GWP. Zero ODP means "safe for ozone," not "safe for the climate." The phase-down side of that story is in the AIM Act lesson, and the low-GWP replacements are in the HFO lesson.

Worked example (our own): leaking 5 lb of R-22 versus 5 lb of R-410A

Our own example. Assumptions: a one-time 5 lb release of each gas (an assumed amount, not a real incident), 100-year GWPs from the EPA values above (R-22 at 1,810; R-410A at 2,088), and the exact definition 1 lb = 0.45359237 kg. This is illustration only; releasing either gas on purpose is prohibited.

  1. Convert mass: 5 lb × 0.45359237 = 2.268 kg.
  2. R-22: 2.268 kg × 1,810 = 4,105 kg CO2-equivalent, which is 4.11 metric tons (divide by 1,000).
  3. R-410A: 2.268 kg × 2,088 = 4,736 kg CO2-equivalent, which is 4.74 metric tons.

The R-410A leak has about 0.63 metric tons more CO2-equivalent, roughly 15 percent more. But only the R-22 also harms ozone. With EPA's ODP of 0.055 or 0.04, those 5 lb act on ozone like roughly 0.2 to 0.3 lb of CFC-11 (5 × 0.04 = 0.2; 5 × 0.055 = 0.275). R-410A has zero ODP because it has no chlorine. The lesson: ranking by GWP alone would call R-22 the "better" gas, which misses the ozone damage entirely. Always check both columns.

Field card: ozone science for the core exam

  • Ozone layer: stratosphere, about 15 to 30 km up (EPA). It absorbs most UV-B.
  • CFC and HCFC chlorine is freed by intense UV in the stratosphere.
  • Cl + O3 gives ClO + O2; ClO + O gives Cl + O2. Chlorine is regenerated, so it is a catalyst: thousands of ozone molecules per atom.
  • HCFCs: hydrogen shortens lifetime, so lower ODP, but not zero.
  • HFCs: no chlorine, ODP zero, but GWP can be in the thousands.
  • ODP is measured against CFC-11 (=1). GWP is measured against CO2 (=1) over 100 years.
