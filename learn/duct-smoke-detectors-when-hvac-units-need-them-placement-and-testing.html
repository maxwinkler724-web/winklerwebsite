Duct Smoke Detectors: When HVAC Units Need Them, Placement and Testing

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A small box on the side of a duct, with a thin tube poking into the airstream, is a duct smoke detector. Its job is not to warn the building. Its job is to stop the fan so the HVAC system does not carry smoke to every room. This lesson covers when a rooftop unit needs one, where it goes, what airflow it can handle, and who should test it.

Check the local code first

Check with the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ). This page is built from public summaries and maker documents read in 2026. We did not read the code books themselves, and the code edition your town enforces may differ. Treat the numbers here as study material, and confirm with the local building or fire official before you rely on them.

What the detector actually does

System Sensor, a major maker, describes its duct detector as a device that samples air moving through a duct. When it senses smoke it trips a relay, and that relay is used to shut down fans. Kidde makes the same kind of device, and its data sheet pairs it with a remote test and reset station. Neither maker pitches it as a room alarm. It protects the air system. A smoke detector in a ceiling and a detector in a duct are different jobs, and a building can need both.

The 2,000 cfm line

Public summaries of the International Mechanical Code (IMC), Section 606, say smoke detectors go in return air systems with a design capacity greater than 2,000 cfm. A Florida code staff page quotes that wording, and a North Carolina State Fire Marshal interpretation uses the same 2,000 cfm line. The same section has an exception: no duct detectors are needed where area smoke detectors tied to a fire alarm system protect the whole building served. The NC interpretation adds two more points. If two or more systems share supply or return ducts or plenums and their combined capacity is above 2,000 cfm, the return system needs detectors. And detectors are not required where the distribution system cannot spread smoke beyond the room or space of origin.

The mechanical code is not the only document. NFPA 90A (air-conditioning and ventilating systems) and NFPA 72 (fire alarm and signaling) also speak to duct detectors, and NFPA describes 90A as covering how HVAC systems are planned, connected to other systems and checked. We cite them here by name only; the full texts are paid products.

Return or supply?

This is where sources disagree, so ask the AHJ. One trade article says the ICC code puts the detector in the return duct or plenum upstream of any filters, while NFPA 90A puts supply-side detectors downstream of the filters. A fire protection consultant's forum thread shows an inspector and a designer arguing over exactly this, and some jurisdictions enforce both. System Sensor's rooftop package exception is more practical: for packaged units the detector may be mounted in the supply section, the return section, or both, "as determined by local code." For ducts wider than 18 inches, the maker recommends mounting downstream of a bend, an obstruction, or an air inlet so the air is mixed.

Sampling tubes and the airflow range

The tube is the part that actually meets the smoke. System Sensor's DUCTSD manual says the tube must extend at least two thirds of the way across the duct, with its inlet holes facing into the airflow, and its Table 1 maps duct width to tube length (for example, a 2 to 4 ft wide duct uses the 3 ft tube). A tube that is too short samples only the edge of the airstream.

Detectors are also listed for a velocity range. The System Sensor DUCTSD and DNRW pages both give 100 to 4,000 feet per minute. Kidde's SuperDuct SD-4W data sheet gives the same 100 to 4,000. A Bosch housing we read uses 300 to 4,000, so never assume one number covers every model; read the label and manual for the detector actually installed.

The shutdown interlock

System Sensor's manual says the auxiliary relays provide fan shutdown, and up to 30 DUCTSD units can be linked to shut down several fans. Two details matter to a tech. After an alarm, if smoke is still present, the auxiliary relay opens for 20 seconds so fans can clear the duct. And the auxiliary contacts must not be wired to the fire alarm panel's initiating circuits; the separate alarm initiation contact is for that. How the shutdown contact ties into the unit's control circuit depends on that unit's wiring diagram. If you can read a ladder diagram, find the detector contact the way you would find any other safety in the chain. See Reading a Ladder Diagram and RTU Cooling Sequence of Operation and the Safety Chain.

Remote test and indicator stations

A detector on a rooftop unit is awkward to test from a ladder. System Sensor sells remote test stations (the RTS151 family) and remote annunciators, and limits wire runs to 25 ohms or less per wire for the test station. Kidde says its remote test and reset station lets staff run sensitivity and alarm tests without climbing to the roof. Bosch lists a remote test kit and a remote LED indicator for its housing.

Testing and cleaning

Makers agree on the broad idea. System Sensor says the detector "must be tested and maintained regularly following NFPA 72" and "should be cleaned at least once a year." Its sensor blinks green every 5 seconds when sensitivity is fine, and red when maintenance is needed. Cleaning means removing the sensor, vacuuming the screen, and cleaning the chamber, then restoring power and doing a detector check. Notify the proper parties before and after.

For NFPA 72 itself we read only public summaries. A university fire safety table of the inspection chapter shows duct detectors on a semi-annual visual inspection line, and a Bosch manual says the same. A Texas county testing guide summarizes the functional test: confirm smoke reaches the sensing chamber and causes an alarm, using smoke or a listed product per the maker, plus a sensitivity check and a check that sampling tubes sample the airstream. Kidde's data sheet gives no fixed interval. Your site's required interval comes from the AHJ and the inspection contract.

Who is allowed to test

The public pages we read do not say plainly who may test a duct detector. One trade article notes that the mechanical contractor usually supplies and installs the detectors, while an electrical contractor usually connects power and any link to the fire alarm. New Jersey regulates fire alarm work: the state Division of Consumer Affairs says that anyone who installs, services or maintains fire alarms needs a license, under P.L. 1997, c.305, and its Fire Alarm, Burglar Alarm and Locksmith Advisory Committee sits within the Board of Examiners of Electrical Contractors. That page does not say whether cleaning or testing a duct detector on a rooftop unit counts. So the safe habit is to ask the building owner and the fire alarm company who owns the test, tell them before you touch the device, and never leave a unit with the detector bypassed.

Worked example: three rooftop units

An invented small retail building has three rooftop units on separate duct systems: 1,800 cfm, 2,400 cfm and 6,000 cfm. We apply the "greater than 2,000 cfm" line and assume no shared ducts and no area detectors covering the whole building.

Step 1, which units need one. 1,800 cfm is not greater than 2,000, so no detector under this rule. 2,400 is greater than 2,000, so yes. 6,000 is greater than 2,000, so yes. (If the 1,800 unit shared a duct with another unit, the NC interpretation would compare the combined cfm instead.)

Step 2, check the velocity for the 2,400 cfm unit. Say its return duct is 30 in wide by 16 in high.

Area = 30 in × 16 in = 480 square inches. 480 ÷ 144 = 3.33 square feet.

Velocity = cfm ÷ area = 2,400 ÷ 3.33 = 720 feet per minute.

Step 3, compare to the maker. System Sensor lists 100 to 4,000 ft/min. 720 sits inside it, so the airflow is acceptable for that detector. The Bosch housing needs at least 300, and 720 passes that too.

Step 4, the tube. The duct is 30 in, or 2.5 ft, wide. Table 1 puts that in the 2 to 4 ft row, the 3 ft tube. The tube must reach at least two thirds of the width: 30 × 2/3 = 20 in. Follow the maker's instructions on cutting or fitting the tube to the duct width.

Do the same arithmetic for any unit you meet. Wide, slow duct sections can fall below the low end of the range, and a fast, narrow one can exceed the top.

Field card: Duct Smoke Detectors

It stops the fan; it does not replace the room alarm.

  • Public summaries of the IMC say detectors go in return systems greater than 2,000 cfm. Confirm the edition with the AHJ.
  • Return, supply or both varies. Ask before you mount.
  • Check duct velocity: cfm ÷ area in square feet. System Sensor and Kidde list 100 to 4,000 ft/min.
  • Tube reaches at least two thirds of the duct width, holes facing the flow.
  • Do not wire auxiliary shutdown contacts to panel initiating circuits.
  • Clean at least once a year per System Sensor; follow the maker and the AHJ for test intervals.
  • Tell the owner and the fire alarm company before testing.
