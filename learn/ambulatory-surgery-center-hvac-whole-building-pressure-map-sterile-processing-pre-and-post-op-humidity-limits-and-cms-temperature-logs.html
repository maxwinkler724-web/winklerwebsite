Ambulatory Surgery Center HVAC: Pressure Map, Sterile Processing, Humidity and Logs

Last updated: 2026-10-24

An ambulatory surgery center (ASC) is a small building with a hospital-style air problem. Some rooms must push air out, some must pull it in, and the humidity has to stay in a band. If you service the units, the useful picture is a pressure map of the whole building, plus the daily log that proves it holds.

The facility's rules come first

The facility's rules come first. The numbers here are general education from public sources. The center's infection control lead, the engineer of record and the commissioning papers decide what each room must do, and state or local rules can be stricter. Do not change airflow, setpoints, filters or pressure controls without their sign-off. The operating room itself is covered in Operating Room HVAC, so we do not repeat it. This lesson is about everything around it.

What CMS asks of an ASC

The federal environment rule for ASCs is 42 CFR 416.44 in the eCFR. It calls for a functional and sanitary environment for surgical services, a separate recovery room and waiting area, emergency equipment on hand, and building safety under the 2012 edition of the Health Care Facilities Code. It gives no room-by-room pressure or air change table. For humidity, CMS Survey and Certification letter 13-25 (April 2013) says an ASC must keep operating room humidity in line with "nationally accepted guidelines" under 416.44(a)(1). The hospital waiver in that same memo (20 percent minimum instead of 35, with a recommended ceiling of 60) is written for hospitals and critical access hospitals, not ASCs.

The building as a pressure map

Think of the corridor as zero. Air should drift from the cleanest spaces toward the dirtiest. ASHRAE's public interpretation of Standard 170 (IC 170-2017-12) describes the general rule as moving air "generally from clean to less-clean areas," then says the specific pressure requirements, such as operating rooms positive to adjoining spaces, win when the two seem to conflict. Applied to an ASC:

  • Operating rooms: positive to the corridor. The CDC's environmental infection control guideline says to maintain positive pressure relative to corridors and adjacent areas.
  • Clean workroom (where sterile packs are assembled): positive. A Massachusetts Department of Public Health sterile processing checklist built on the 2022 FGI outpatient guidelines lists a clean workroom at a minimum of 4 total air changes per hour, positive pressure.
  • Decontamination: negative, at least 6 total air changes per hour, exhausted, with no recirculating room units, per the same checklist.
  • Sterile storage: drawn positive on our plan because it is the clean end of the chain. Confirm the sign and the air change rate on your project's pressure schedule.
  • Soiled holding and similar rooms: drawn negative. Same rule: read the schedule.
  • Pre-op and recovery bays: we did not find a public source for a pressure sign, so our drawing leaves it to the design schedule. Do not assume a sign.

Two tricky points. First, a central sterile core does not get to feed the ORs and corridors just because it is clean. ASHRAE's interpretation IC 170-2017-12 answered that question with no: the operating rooms must stay positive to adjoining spaces. Second, the Massachusetts checklist says required pressure relationships have to hold in all HVAC operating modes, which is the trap for setbacks and shutdowns.

Sterile processing: two sides, one wall

Decontamination is where soiled instruments arrive and get cleaned. The clean side is where they are inspected, assembled and packaged. The air rule is simple: the soiled room is exhausted (not returned to a shared recirculating system) and held negative; the clean room is supplied and held positive; instruments and people move one way.

Temperature has a wrinkle. ASHRAE interpretation IC 170-2013-7 reports the 2013 Standard 170 design range of 72 to 78 degrees F for decontamination, clean workroom and sterile storage, and notes that an instrument-reprocessing standard (ANSI/AAMI ST79) quoted 60 to 65 degrees F for decontamination areas. The committee said the 72 to 78 range does not pose unacceptable contamination risk. Ask the infection preventionist which range the center follows before you retune.

Humidity: limits and what to do out of range

The CDC says to engineer humidity controls into the HVAC system and to monitor them so excess moisture is removed. CMS letter 13-25 says that where a facility uses its waiver it must monitor RH in anesthetizing locations and take corrective action to stay at or above 20 percent. For ASC operating rooms the band to know is the guideline band our OR lesson cites, 20 to 60 percent, and the CMS recommended ceiling of 60 percent is the one that matters most in a humid season.

When RH leaves the band, our own suggested order of work, which you should clear with facility engineering:

  1. Tell the person who owns the room (charge nurse or manager) before touching anything.
  2. Check the sensor against a calibrated handheld reading in the same spot.
  3. If it is high: check cooling coil operation, the economizer or outdoor air damper position, reheat and dehumidification mode, and condensate drain. If it is low: check the humidifier, its water supply and control signal.
  4. Do not shut off or reduce airflow to fix humidity, because that can lose pressure.
  5. Log the reading, action and approver.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Assumptions we chose: decontamination room 200 sq ft, 9 ft ceiling, 6 air changes per hour of exhaust (the minimum in the Massachusetts checklist, used here as a labeled example), and a 75 cfm offset to stay negative (our example, not a code value). Sea-level pressure p = 101.325 kPa (standard atmosphere).

Airflow. Volume = 200 x 9 = 1,800 cubic feet. Exhaust = 1,800 x 6 / 60 = 180 cfm. Supply = 180 − 75 = 105 cfm. That is 3.5 air changes per hour of supply. If your pressure schedule calls for 6 total supply air changes instead, supply is 180 cfm and exhaust is 180 + 75 = 255 cfm.

Humidity. Room at 68 F (20 C), humidity ratio W = 0.0075. Saturation pressure at 20 C is 2.339 kPa from the saturated water table in a Penn State Mechanical Engineering handout of property tables (the table title is "Properties of saturated water"). Solve W = 0.622 pw / (p − pw) for pw: pw = p W / (0.622 + W) = 101.325 x 0.0075 / 0.6295 = 1.207 kPa. RH = pw / psat = 1.207 / 2.339 = 0.516, or about 52 percent. That is inside 20 to 60.

If it drifted high. Using the moisture rule 4.5 x cfm x delta W, drying 1,000 cfm of air by 0.0012 lb/lb (our assumed numbers) removes 4.5 x 1,000 x 0.0012 = 5.4 lb of water per hour, which is 4,840 x 1,000 x 0.0012 = 5,808 Btu/h of latent load. For more on humidity ratio see Psychrometrics for Technicians.

One rooftop unit or dedicated units

Our own reasoning, not a code citation: one rooftop unit serving surgery, sterile processing, pre-op and offices ties every room to one fan, one supply temperature and one humidity condition. Decontamination needs its own exhaust, so a single unit needs good zoning and return paths that carry no soiled air. Dedicated units make it easier to hold the map when one system is down. Either way, pressure relationships must hold in all operating modes. For reheat and dehumidification on rooftop units see Hot Gas Reheat in Rooftop Units, and for the equipment itself Rooftop Unit Basics.

Daily logs

CMS does not hand an ASC a standard log form in the sources we read. The CDC says to monitor ventilation systems per engineer and manufacturer recommendations, and to monitor special ventilation areas for air changes, filtration and pressure differentials. From that, a sensible daily log (our suggestion) has: date, time, room, temperature, RH, pressure reading with its sign, who read it, any out-of-range note and the action taken. Never back-fill. Compare to the isolation-room checks in Airborne Infection Isolation and Protective Environment Rooms.

Backup power and shutdowns

The CDC recommends backup emergency power and air-handling and pressurization for operating rooms and other critical areas, a contingency plan for general power failure, and fixed backup ventilation where feasible. It also says not to shut down patient-care HVAC except for maintenance, repair, emergency-backup testing or new construction, to coordinate startups with infection control, and to allow time for the air to clear after startup. Know which units sit on the emergency panel and ask before any planned outage. See Emergency Generator Room Ventilation.

Field card: ASC HVAC

Corridor is zero; clean rooms push out, soiled rooms pull in.

  • ORs positive to the corridor; clean workroom positive; decontamination negative.
  • Decon example: 6 air changes in 1,800 cubic feet is 180 cfm exhaust; minus a 75 cfm offset is 105 cfm supply.
  • RH check: pw = p W / (0.622 + W), then RH = pw / psat.
  • Do not cut airflow to fix humidity; call facility engineering.
  • Log date, time, room, temperature, RH, pressure sign, action and initials.
  • Pressure must hold in every HVAC mode, including setback and startup.
