Window Shades, Blinds, Cellular Shades, Films and Awnings: Cutting Solar Heat Gain and Winter Heat Loss

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The west bedroom that is 85 degrees at supper time is usually a window problem before it is an air conditioner problem. Sun that comes through glass turns into heat inside the house. This lesson covers what shades, blinds, cellular shades, films, storm panels and awnings can do, what the federal and national-lab tests found, and when shading beats buying more cooling capacity.

Why windows matter and why orientation matters

The U.S. Department of Energy's Energy Saver pages treat windows as a place where heat moves both ways: unwanted sun heat in summer and heat loss in winter. For warm weather it points to white shades, drapes or blinds, with exterior shades preferred, and to awnings on south- and west-facing windows. It also lists sun-control or reflective films on south-facing windows and, in hot climates, east- and west-facing ones. That matches what you feel at home. A south window gets high sun that an overhang can block. A west window gets low, late-afternoon sun that comes in nearly straight, right when the house is already warm. North windows get little direct sun, so shading them does the least for cooling.

Inside versus outside

Glass lets sunlight through, and the heat is released where the light lands. A shade inside the window catches the sun after it has already crossed the glass, so much of that heat stays in the room. A shade outside stops the sun before the glass. The Oak Ridge National Laboratory awning report says awnings can reduce solar heat gain more effectively than interior shades. A Department of Energy Better Buildings Residential Network summary reports exterior shades on south- and west-facing windows cutting cooling use 12 to 25 percent in tests, and preliminary Lab Homes results of 9 to 25 percent of cooling load. The same summary notes these come from different studies with different baselines and are not directly comparable.

In the Oak Ridge awning measurements on an Atlanta house, the average sunlight transmitted indoors between 8 a.m. and 7 p.m. was about 12 W/m² with awnings closed versus about 41 W/m² without, roughly three times lower. Modeling that house for April through September showed 15.3 percent less HVAC electricity with the awnings closed and 12.1 percent half closed. The field data came from one house over about four months, and the percentages are simulated, so treat them as a good indication and not a promise.

The winter side: cellular shades and the awning penalty

Cellular (honeycomb) shades trap air in their cells and slow heat loss at night. In a Department of Energy fact sheet on a Knoxville test home, cellular shades saved up to 20 percent of heating energy and up to 15 percent of combined heating and cooling energy compared with no shades. A related Oak Ridge and Lawrence Berkeley study in side-by-side rooms found 9 to 23 percent daily heating savings for five cellular shades from 6 p.m. to 6 a.m. These are test results, not a guaranteed bill cut. Savings depend on whether you close the shades at dusk and open them on sunny winter days.

The Department of Energy's window-attachment report adds a caution. In northern and central climates, awnings can raise heating energy enough to make a net penalty, so it suggests opening or retracting them in winter and closing them in summer. It also found vertical blinds carried a heating penalty in the North. In cooling-dominated climates all attachments save energy, and interior panels and exterior storm panels were the most consistent savers across climates. Attachments help leaky windows most: average modeled savings were about 9.6 GJ a year on single clear aluminum windows, 3.3 GJ on double clear wood, and almost nothing (0.05 GJ) on double low-e vinyl.

The Better Buildings summary also shows how much operation matters. Against vinyl blinds, cellular shades kept down all the time saved 13.3 percent cooling and 9.3 percent heating, but under typical use that dropped to 5.8 and 2.0 percent. A shade only works when it is in the right position.

Films, storm panels and the cautions

Low-e film on the inside of a window and low-e interior or exterior storm windows are both on the Energy Saver list. In Pacific Northwest National Laboratory figures summarized by the Better Buildings network, a single-pane window with an exterior low-e storm went from a solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) of 0.61 to 0.46, and a double-pane from 0.57 to 0.42. Exterior storms lowered SHGC more than interior ones.

Films deserve care. A glass manufacturer's technical bulletin on field-applied plastic films says films can in some circumstances cause or contribute to glass breakage, and that room-side films on insulating glass can break the glass from thermal stress unless it is heat treated. It also says heat-absorbing films can raise sealant temperature enough to risk seal failure, and that the glass maker gives no warranty against breakage after any film is applied. It adds that room-side solar films mainly reduce glare and do little to lower solar heat gain. Before you film a dual-pane window, read your window warranty and ask the film supplier in writing whether the film is approved for your glass. Fog between the panes after filming is a seal problem, not a cleaning problem.

Worked example: one west window

Our own example. Every input below is an assumption unless a source is named. A west bedroom window is 3 ft wide by 5 ft tall, double-pane clear glass, in the late afternoon of a hot day.

  1. Glass area: 3 ft × 5 ft = 15 ft² (assumed).
  2. Sunlight on the west wall in late afternoon: 150 Btu/h per ft² (our own assumption; real values vary with date, latitude and clouds).
  3. SHGC: 0.59, the double-clear value listed in the Department of Energy window-attachment report.
  4. No shade: 15 × 150 × 0.59 = 1,327, or about 1,330 Btu/h of heat entering.
  5. Exterior shade: we assume a 70 percent cut, picked because the Oak Ridge awning measurement (12 versus 41 W/m²) was about 71 percent lower for a closed awning over a full day. A fixed awning blocks low western sun less well, so a drop-down exterior shade is the better match. Remaining: 1,330 × 0.30 = about 400 Btu/h, so about 930 Btu/h saved.
  6. Inside blind: we assume a 40 percent cut (no source; real products vary widely). Remaining: 1,330 × 0.60 = about 800 Btu/h, so about 530 Btu/h saved.

Now compare a small room air conditioner. ENERGY STAR sizing guidance gives 5,000 Btu/h for a roughly 147 sq ft room and says to add 10 percent for a sunny room, here 500 Btu/h. Our example window shed about 930 Btu/h with an exterior shade, close to 19 percent of a 5,000 Btu/h unit's output (930 ÷ 5,000 = 0.186). With two similar west windows the saving is about 1,860 Btu/h, more than the sunny-room allowance. These results are our own and a real house will differ. The point is the size: shading removes a load that a unit would otherwise have to carry.

When shading beats adding AC capacity

  • One room is hot while the rest of the house is fine, especially a west or south room in late afternoon. Shade that window before sizing up equipment.
  • The AC runs nonstop on hot afternoons but the house cools overnight. That is a solar-gain pattern, not a capacity shortfall.
  • You are thinking of a bigger unit to cover a few windows. Oversized equipment can short-cycle and dehumidify poorly on mild days.

Shading will not fix an undersized system, thin attic insulation, leaky ducts or a house that is hot all day. For those, see attic insulation and air sealing. Seal air leaks first with the steps in drafty doors and windows, and in winter watch moisture; see condensation on windows, because a shade pulled down tight can leave the glass colder.

A practical order of operations

  1. Close light-colored shades or blinds on west and south windows before the sun hits them.
  2. Add exterior shading to the worst windows (awning, drop-down exterior shade or storm panel).
  3. Use cellular shades on cold-side windows and close them at dusk in winter.
  4. Choose film only after checking your glass type and warranty.
  5. Add air movement with a ceiling fan, or compare a whole-house fan, before adding capacity.

Field card: Window shading

Stop the sun outside the glass, and keep winter heat in at night.

  • Shade west and south windows first; exterior beats interior.
  • Awnings help in summer but can raise heating use in cold climates, so retract them in winter.
  • Cellular shades: close at night in winter; tests showed up to 20% heating savings.
  • Film on dual-pane glass can cause thermal stress or seal trouble; check the warranty first.
  • Our example: about 930 of 1,330 Btu/h cut on one west window (assumptions).
