Sun and UV Exposure for Rooftop HVAC Techs: Sunscreen Timing, Cover-Up and Skin Checks

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A rooftop unit does not care what the weather is, so a roof tech often works in direct sun for hours with no shade at all. Ultraviolet (UV) light adds up over a career. This lesson covers the habits that keep it manageable: sunscreen on a timer, covering skin, hats, glasses, shade breaks and checking your own skin.

Why roof work means long sun hours

Rooftop equipment sits on open roofs, and service often means a long stretch beside the unit: checking, cleaning coils, changing belts, waiting on a part or a crane. Federal worker-health guidance says UV exposure is generally highest in summer and between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m., and that UV still reaches workers on cloudy days. A federal environmental page adds that nearly half of the daytime UVB arrives in that 10 to 4 window. A roof also has no tree or building shadow to hide in, so a shade plan has to be something you bring or make.

Reflected glare off light roofs

UV does not only come from above. The federal worker-health page notes that snow and light-colored sand reflect UV, so exposure can come from above and from below. Another federal skin-cancer page lists water, cement, sand and snow as reflectors. None of the sources we read tested roofing membranes. Our own reasoning is that a bright, light-colored roof surface is a large pale surface, so treat it with the same caution: glare can reach the underside of a hat brim, the chin, the ears and the lower face. Sunscreen and eyewear matter on those spots even when you wear a hat.

Sunscreen and reapplying

  • Strength. The federal worker-health page says to use SPF 30 or higher. A federal environmental page says broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher. Another federal page sets SPF 15 as the minimum, so SPF 30 is the safer choice for a long shift.
  • Amount and timing. The worker-health page says to apply at least 1 ounce about 20 minutes before going outside, covering ears, scalp, lips, neck, tops of feet and backs of hands.
  • Reapplying. Reapply at least every 2 hours, and after heavy sweating. The same page says wind, humidity, sweat and application technique change how well it works, and that you may need more frequent reapplication with insect repellent. Roof work is sweaty work, so treat 2 hours as the longest gap, not the target.
  • Shelf life. The worker-health page says to discard sunscreen after 1 to 2 years. Another federal page says check the expiration date, and use it sooner if it has been exposed to high heat. A van in summer qualifies, so do not leave the bottle on the dash.

Cover skin with clothing

Sunscreen is the backup layer. The federal skin-cancer page advises long sleeves and long pants when possible, and notes that tightly woven fabric protects best and wet fabric protects less than dry. The worker-health page says dark, tightly woven clothing beats light, loosely woven cloth. A university extension publication gives examples: dense dark fabrics such as denim and polyester can reach a rating of 30 or higher, while light cotton can be as low as 5 to 7. A loose, breathable long-sleeve work shirt is a good match for the heat lesson linked below, because covering skin also means more sweat to manage. Drink water and take your breaks.

Hats under or over a hard hat

The federal skin-cancer page says a wide brim that shades the face, ears and back of the neck is best, in a tightly woven material, and that a baseball cap leaves the ears and neck for clothing, sunscreen or shade to cover. A standard wide-brimmed hat is not head protection, and it will not fit under a hard hat. Our own advice: when your site requires a hard hat, ask the site safety lead what shade add-ons the hard hat maker allows, and follow the maker instructions. Do not modify the shell yourself. If the roof is clear of overhead hazards and no hard hat is required, a wide-brimmed hat is the better sun tool, but that is the site safety lead's call, not ours. A neck cover or sunscreen on the neck fills the gap either way.

UV-blocking safety glasses

The worker-health page recommends sunglasses with almost 100 percent UV protection and side panels. A federal environmental page says 99 to 100 percent of UVA and UVB, and notes that UVB can cause snow blindness, a temporary loss of vision from a sunburned cornea. Wraparound styles block light from the sides. On a roof, glasses also have to meet your site's eye-protection rules, so use safety eyewear that also blocks UV. Impact ratings, lens tints and face shields are covered in the eye protection lesson, so we do not repeat them here.

Shade breaks

The worker-health page tells employers to schedule outdoor work for times with less sun and to provide shaded or indoor break areas. A university extension publication points to heat illness alongside sun exposure. Practical moves we suggest from those points: do the longest roof tasks early when the job allows, carry a pop-up canopy or use the unit's own shadow for breaks, and pair every sunscreen reapplication with a drink of water. A federal page notes that trees, umbrellas and canopies do not give complete protection, so keep sunscreen and clothing on in shade too.

Reading the UV index

The UV index is a forecast scale of 1 to 11 and up. A federal environmental page groups it as low (1 to 2, minimal protection), moderate to high (3 to 7, protection needed, shade from late morning to mid-afternoon), and very high to extreme (8 and up, extra protection, with the shadow rule: if your shadow is shorter than you, seek shade). A federal skin-cancer page says to take protection steps when the index is 3 or higher in your area. Check the forecast the night before and treat a 3 as the trigger to put the whole system on: sunscreen, sleeves, hat, glasses.

Skin checks and when to see a clinician

A federal skin-cancer page says a change in your skin is the most common sign. Watch for a shiny bump, a pink or red scaly patch, a firm red bump, or a sore that does not heal, is crusty or bleeds. For moles, it lists the ABCDE signs: asymmetrical, jagged border, uneven color, larger than a pea, and evolving over weeks or months. It advises talking to your doctor if you notice skin changes. A university extension publication suggests a monthly self-exam with a mirror and bright light, with a coworker or family member checking the back of the neck and tops of the ears. For sunburn, the worker-health page says see a doctor if it covers more than 15 percent of the body, if you are dehydrated, if fever is above 101 degrees F, or if extreme pain lasts more than 48 hours. It also lists some common medicines that can raise sunburn risk, so ask a clinician or pharmacist about yours. This lesson is education, not medical advice.

Worked example: planning sunscreen times for a roof shift (our own example)

Our own example with assumed numbers. These are assumptions, not data from a source.

  • Crew: 4 techs, each on the roof from 7:00 a.m. to 3:30 p.m.
  • Reapply interval: every 2 hours (the federal minimum guidance above; we assume no heavy-sweat extra applications yet).
  • First application 20 minutes before going up, so at 6:40 a.m.
  • Amount: 1 ounce per application per tech (the worker-health page says at least 1 ounce).

Times. 6:40 plus 2 hours is 8:40, then 10:40, 12:40 and 2:40 p.m. The next would be 4:40, after the shift, so we stop. That is 1 first application plus 4 reapplications = 5 applications.

Product. 5 applications x 1 ounce = 5 ounces per tech. 4 techs x 5 ounces = 20 ounces for the crew for this shift.

Heavy-sweat check. Suppose we assume the hot day pushes the interval down to 1.5 hours: 6:40, 8:10, 9:40, 11:10, 12:40, 2:10 p.m. That is 6 applications, so 6 x 4 = 24 ounces. The extra 4 ounces is cheap protection for the crew. Bring more than the plan says, and set phone alarms.

Quick rules

  • SPF 30 or higher, broad spectrum, 1 ounce, 20 minutes early, reapply at least every 2 hours.
  • Long sleeves, tight weave, wide brim or approved shade add-on, UV-blocking safety glasses.
  • Plan shade breaks; UV is strongest from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m.
  • Check skin monthly; see a clinician for any sore that does not heal or a changing mole.

Field card: Sun and UV Exposure for Rooftop HVAC Techs: Sunscreen Timing, Cover-Up and Skin Checks

Sunscreen on a timer, skin covered, eyes protected.

  • SPF 30 or higher, about 1 ounce, 20 minutes before going up.
  • Reapply at least every 2 hours and after heavy sweating.
  • UV strongest 10 a.m. to 4 p.m.; it still reaches you on cloudy days.
  • Light roof surfaces and other reflectors mean glare from below: treat the chin and ears.
  • Wide brim or site-approved shade add-on, long sleeves, UV-blocking safety glasses.
  • UV index 3 or higher: full protection.
  • Check your skin monthly; talk to a doctor about changes.
