Working Alongside Other Trades on a Job Site: A New HVAC Tech's Guide

Last updated: 2026-11-04

On a new house or a gut renovation, you are rarely the only crew in the building. Framers, plumbers, electricians, insulators and drywall hangers all need the same walls, floors and ceiling space, in a set order. A new HVAC tech who learns the order, asks before cutting, and leaves a clean path behind is easy to work next to. This lesson covers that part of the job.

Who is usually on site

The mix changes by job, but a typical house has these crews:

  • The general contractor or site lead. This person owns the schedule and the plans, and decides who goes where each day. When something is unclear, this is who you ask.
  • Framers. They build the floors, walls and roof structure that your ducts, lines and equipment must fit inside.
  • Plumbers. Drain, vent and supply pipes compete with your ducts and condensate drains for the same joist bays and wall cavities.
  • Electricians. They run cable through the same framing, and your equipment needs power and control wiring from them.
  • Insulation and air-sealing crews. They close up the cavities after rough-in work is done.
  • Drywall crews. They hang, tape and sand, which makes dust and closes the walls for good.

Learn first names on day one. A quick "what are you working on today?" often prevents a mistake that would cost both of you an afternoon.

Sequencing: who goes before whom

The general order inside a house is: frame, then rough-in (ducts, pipes, wiring), then inspections where required, then insulation, then drywall, then finish work. Rough-in goes before insulation and drywall because those steps hide the walls and ceilings. Equipment is usually set after the walls are closed and the space is dry and fairly clean, so a furnace or air handler does not sit in dust for weeks. Our rough-in lesson covers what the HVAC rough-in itself includes, so we do not repeat it here. The point for this lesson is that your work is one link in a chain, and a late or incomplete link holds up the crews behind you.

Ask before you cut or notch framing

It can be tempting to notch a stud or drill a joist to get a duct or a line set through. Do not do it on your own. Framing members carry loads, and the people who know which ones matter are the framer, the designer and the site lead. A federal housing-research design guide, in its chapter on wood framing, says notches in bending members such as joists should be avoided, with only small ones allowed. It also says holes in bending members should not be larger than one-third of the member depth and should be located along the center line. We cite that as general background, not as a rule for your job, because the actual limits depend on the member, its span and the local code. On the job, follow the building plans and ask the site lead before you cut anything structural. If a duct will not fit without a cut, that is a problem to report, not one to solve with a saw.

Sharing chases and ceiling space

Chases (vertical shafts) and ceiling spaces fill up fast. Large ducts are the hardest to move, so crews usually agree to place them first and run smaller pipe and cable around them. Settle this by talking before work starts, not after something is already installed in the way. Some habits that help:

  • Walk the space with the other trades before you start and point out where your main trunk will go.
  • Keep your duct and drain runs where the plans or the site lead put them. If you need to shift, say so first.
  • Do not strap, hang or tape your work to another trade's pipe or cable unless you have agreed it with them.
  • Leave room for the insulation crew to reach what they need to seal.

Protect what is installed, and respect other trades' work

Open duct ends collect sawdust, drywall dust and dropped screws. Cap or tape open ends as soon as you stop work, and keep stored equipment covered and off the floor. Those habits come up again in protecting your HVAC during a renovation. The same respect runs the other way. Do not step on, move or cut another trade's pipe, cable or boxes, and do not walk through wet paint or fresh work. If something of theirs is in your way, tell them or the site lead and let the owner of that work move it. If you damage something, say so right away, since a quick report is much easier to fix than a hidden problem found at inspection. For an occupied home during service work, see protecting a customer's home.

Communicate changes in writing

Spoken agreements get forgotten. When a plan changes, such as a moved chase, a different equipment location or a new duct route, send a short written note to the site lead and the affected trades: a text or email with what changed, where, who agreed and the date. A phone photo of the marked-up area makes it clearer. This is general good practice, not legal advice. It gives everyone the same facts and keeps small changes from turning into disputes later. Your employer may have its own change process, so follow it.

Daily cleanup and shared safety

OSHA's construction housekeeping rule says form and scrap lumber with protruding nails and other debris must be kept cleared from work areas, passageways and stairs. It also says combustible scrap and debris must be removed at regular intervals, and that containers must be provided for waste, with covers for containers holding garbage or oily, flammable or hazardous waste. Take your own cut-offs, packaging and tape out at the end of each day.

Safety on a shared site also means watching out for each other. OSHA has published an interpretation letter explaining that its multi-employer policy looks at the roles employers play on a site, such as creating a hazard, being exposed to it, correcting it or controlling the site, and that an employer in some of those roles may be cited even if its own workers are not exposed. You do not need to sort out those roles. The practical lesson is that a hazard you leave behind can hurt someone from another crew, so fix it, guard it or report it. A ladder is a good example. OSHA's ladder rule says a portable ladder used to reach an upper landing must extend at least 3 feet above it, that a ladder should be set at about a 4 to 1 ratio (horizontal distance about one-quarter of the working length), and that a ladder in a passageway, doorway or other spot where traffic could knock it over must be secured or barricaded. Our ladder lesson covers setup in more detail.

Keep a simple daily log

A pocket notebook or phone note is enough. At the end of each day write the date, who was on site, what you finished, what you could not finish and why, any damage or conflicts, and any change that was agreed. Our own habit is to add one line for tomorrow. The log helps you answer "when did that happen?" without guessing, and it makes the next morning faster. For timing your own tasks, see tracking your own job times.

Our own worked example: a small house and one lost day

Our own example. Every time and day below is an assumption for teaching, not a real job or an industry standard. Assume a one-story, 1,600 square foot house, framed and dried in, with a ducted system. We assume the trades visit in this order, one step at a time:

  • Day 1: framers finish the last walls.
  • Day 2: plumber rough-in and HVAC rough-in (assumed 8 hours for two techs).
  • Day 3: electrician rough-in.
  • Day 4: rough inspections and the HVAC pre-drywall tests (assumed 2 hours).
  • Day 5: insulation.
  • Days 6 to 8: drywall hang and tape.

Planned finish: day 8. Now suppose the HVAC rough-in needs 12 hours instead of 8, because a chase was blocked by plumbing pipe. That is 12 − 8 = 4 hours over, and with 8-hour days the work spills into the next day (assumption). So HVAC finishes on day 3, not day 2.

The electrician needs the same ceiling space, so we assume the electrician moves from day 3 to day 4. Tests and inspection move from day 4 to day 5, insulation from day 5 to day 6, and drywall from days 6 to 8 to days 7 to 9. Result: one lost day on the HVAC job becomes a one-day delay for every trade behind it, and the house finishes on day 9, not day 8. If the HVAC tech had told the site lead on day 1 that the chase was blocked, the plumber could have moved the pipe first and the slip might not have happened. That is why early, written notice matters.

Field card: Working with other trades

Ask first, protect your work, write it down.

  • Know the site lead and who is on site each day.
  • Rough-in before insulation and drywall; set equipment after.
  • Never cut or notch framing without asking the site lead.
  • Agree on shared chases and ceiling space before you install.
  • Cap duct ends and cover equipment.
  • Send written notes for any change.
  • Clear scrap and debris from walkways daily.
  • Keep a short daily log.
