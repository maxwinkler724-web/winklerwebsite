Fire Extinguishers for HVAC Techs: Classes and Ratings, OSHA Checks, PASS Method and What to Carry in the Van

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Brazing torches, 240-volt panels, compressor motors and a van full of cylinders all sit in one day's work. A small fire is a real possibility, and the right extinguisher in the right place is the cheapest insurance you carry. This lesson covers what the label means, what OSHA expects, how to use one, and when to skip it and walk away.

Check the rules where you work

Check your site and employer. This page is general education built from federal rules and public safety pages read in 2026. Your employer's fire plan, the customer's site rules and any permit for hot work decide what you actually do. The extinguisher standard NFPA 10 is the industry rule behind most of this. We name it in plain text only and do not reproduce it.

The fire classes

Extinguishers are matched to the fuel. OSHA's evacuation eTool and university safety offices list the same groups: Class A is paper, cloth, wood, rubber and many plastics. Class B is oils, gasoline, grease, solvents and other flammable liquids; university pages also put flammable gases here. Class C is energized electrical equipment such as wiring and fuse boxes. Class D is combustible metals such as magnesium and titanium. Class K is cooking oils and fats. A FEMA training handout adds a useful point: once the power is off, a Class C fire is really a Class A fire, because now it is just burning insulation and plastic.

What the three common agents do

  • Water is for Class A only, according to the university pages we read. It cools the fuel. The FEMA handout warns that water conducts electricity and can spread burning oil or gas.
  • Carbon dioxide (CO2) is a gas that displaces oxygen. The pages list it for Class B and C. It leaves no powder, and one campus page says it has no pressure gauge, so it is checked by weight.
  • ABC dry chemical coats the fuel with powder. Campus pages call it the common all-round type for A, B and C, and one calls it "extremely messy." One campus page says it can be used on every class except combustible metals. Dry chemical packs hard over time, so the monthly check includes turning it over.

Reading the rating: 2-A:10-B:C

The number in front of the A tells how much water the unit equals, with each unit equal to 1.25 gallons, per OSHA's eTool and USFA. The number in front of the B is the square feet of Class B fire that a non-expert user should be able to put out. The C has no number: it only says the agent does not conduct electricity. So 2-A:10-B:C means 2.5 gallons of water equivalent on Class A, about 10 square feet of Class B, and safe on energized equipment. USFA also says to look for a mark showing the unit is listed by a nationally recognized testing laboratory.

What OSHA asks of employers

Two federal rules apply, depending on whether you are in a general workplace or on construction.

  • 29 CFR 1910.157 (general industry). The employer must keep extinguishers fully charged, in place, and accessible (c). Travel distance to a Class A extinguisher is 75 feet or less, and to a Class B hazard 50 feet or less (d). The employer visually inspects extinguishers at least monthly and gives each one an annual maintenance check, with the annual date recorded and kept for one year after the last entry or the life of the shell, whichever is less (e). Where extinguishers are provided for employee use, employees get education on the general principles when first hired and at least annually, and employees designated to fight fires get hands-on training at assignment and at least annually (g). A written plan in which everyone evacuates and no one fights fires can exempt an employer from most of the rule (b).
  • 29 CFR 1926.150 (construction). At least a 2A extinguisher for each 3,000 square feet of protected building area, with the nearest one within 100 feet of any point. Where more than 5 gallons of flammable or combustible liquid, or 5 pounds of flammable gas, is in use, a 10B extinguisher must be within 50 feet. They must be listed or approved by a nationally recognized testing laboratory and maintained and inspected.

The CDC's workplace checklist, which summarizes the OSHA rule, adds hydrostatic testing intervals from 5 years for water and CO2 types to 12 years for some dry chemical types. Your service company handles those tests; you handle the monthly look.

Fight it or leave

Several sources agree on when you may fight a fire. USFA and the university pages say to use an extinguisher only if everyone has been alerted, the fire department has been called, the fire is small and contained, you have a clear way out behind you, you know what is burning, you have the right extinguisher, and you are trained. A FEMA handout adds that if you cannot put it out in about 5 seconds, or you have any doubt, you leave. If any answer is no, get out, close the door behind you if you can, and call 911 from outside.

PASS is the method on those pages: Pull the pin, Aim low at the base of the fire, Squeeze the handle, Sweep side to side. Stand roughly 6 to 8 feet away with your back to the exit, and watch for re-ignition after it looks out.

Fires from the work we do

  • Brazing. Campus hot work programs say to move combustibles at least 35 feet from the work, or cover them, close gaps and cracks that let sparks travel, keep an extinguisher ready, and post a fire watch afterward. One page asks for at least 30 minutes of watch and another at least one hour, so follow your site's permit. Our own habit: set the extinguisher within arm's reach before the torch is lit, and check behind the joint for scorching in framing or insulation. See the brazing lesson.
  • Fuel gas and cylinders. A university fact sheet says not to try to put out a compressed gas fire unless you are trained and it is safe. Acetylene cylinders heated above 125 F can be damaged, and a flashback into the cylinder can leave it unstable. Close the cylinder valve if you can do so safely; otherwise evacuate and call 911 from outside. Tell the fire crew about every cylinder on the van.
  • Electrical panels and motors. A trade magazine article on Class C fires says the key is de-energizing: once power is off, fight the fire by its fuel, and use CO2 or dry chemical on a de-energized cubicle. It says never to use a straight stream of water. Our own rule: do not open a panel that is arcing or smoking to get a better shot. Shut off power only if you can do it from a safe spot, then leave and call 911. See arc flash and shock safety.

Mounting one in the van

The federal rule for commercial trucks, 49 CFR 393.95, shows what good mounting looks like: the extinguisher must be securely mounted to prevent sliding, rolling or vertical movement, readily accessible, and have a gauge or other way to see that it is charged. It asks for at least 5 B:C on an ordinary truck and 10 B:C where hazardous materials are carried in placarded quantities. Whether it covers your vehicle depends on its weight and use, so confirm with your safety lead. USFA's vehicle-fire page says that if a vehicle burns, you stop, get everyone out, stay at least 100 feet away and call 911; it does not tell you to fight it. Ours: mount it by the driver or side door, not behind loaded racks, and not in the cargo area with cylinders. For cylinder rules, see carrying refrigerant cylinders in a service van.

Worked example: our van

Our own example, with assumptions. The van carries an oxyacetylene set, refrigerant cylinders and power tools. Assume the 5-pound ABC extinguisher we pick has a label reading 2-A:10-B:C. This is an assumed label for the example; read your own.

  1. Choose the class. Wood and plastic packaging: A. Fuel gas, oil and solvents: B. Cords, tools, batteries and panel work: C. That points to ABC dry chemical.
  2. Check the numbers. 2 A x 1.25 gallons = 2.5 gallons of water equivalent. The B number is 10, which is not below 10 B:C, the higher federal truck figure, so we would meet either truck tier if the rule applies to us. For a construction site, 2A meets the 1926.150 minimum of 2A.
  3. Mount it. Bracket bolted to the vehicle, within reach of the driver or side door, gauge facing out.
  4. Monthly check (from the university inspection list): it is on its bracket and the bracket is secure; nothing blocks access; no dents, leaks, rust or clogged nozzle; the pin and tamper seal are intact; the gauge needle is in the green; invert it a few times to loosen the powder; check the service tag shows an annual check within the last year; then initial and date the tag.
  5. Replace or service it if the gauge is out of the green, the seal is broken or it has been used even once. Rechargeable units get serviced after every use; disposables are replaced.

Field card: Van fire extinguishers

Right class, in reach, checked monthly, and leave when in doubt.

  • A is solids, B is liquids and gases, C is energized electrical, D is metals, K is cooking oil.
  • Rating 2-A:10-B:C: each A unit is 1.25 gallons of water; the B number is square feet of liquid fire; C has no number.
  • OSHA general industry: monthly visual check, annual maintenance check, training when hired and yearly.
  • Construction: 2A per 3,000 sq ft, within 100 feet; 10B within 50 feet where flammable liquid or gas is in use.
  • PASS: Pull, Aim low, Squeeze, Sweep.
  • Fight only if it is small, you have an exit, the right unit and training, and 911 is called. Otherwise leave.
  • Brazing: clear 35 feet or cover, extinguisher at hand, fire watch.
