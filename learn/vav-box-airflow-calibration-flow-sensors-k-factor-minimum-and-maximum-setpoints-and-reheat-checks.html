VAV Box Airflow Calibration: Flow Sensors, K-Factor, Minimum and Maximum Setpoints and Reheat Checks

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A VAV box controller never "sees" airflow. It sees a small pressure difference from the inlet sensor, and a number called the K-factor turns that pressure into cfm. If you understand that one step, most box airflow problems stop being mysterious.

How the box measures airflow

Price Industries describes its inlet sensor as one that does not measure velocity or flow directly. It measures the average difference between total pressure and static pressure, using several total-pressure ports spread across the inlet, much like a balancer averaging a traverse. The result feeds the damper controller and shows up on the thermostat and the building automation screen. Titus describes the same idea in its AeroCross sensor: total-pressure ports face the air on the front, static ports sit on the back, and the controller reads the difference. See VAV Boxes Basics for the whole box.

Trane's catalog calls its version a flow ring placed just ahead of the air valve, and says pressure-independent control uses that measuring device so delivered airflow does not depend on inlet static pressure.

Amplification and the square root

A plain pitot tube reads true velocity pressure, and Titus notes it takes 4005 fpm to make 1 in. w.c. Many box sensors amplify the signal so the controller has more pressure to work with. Titus gives amplification of 1.7 to 2.9 depending on inlet size, and Taylor Engineering's conference slides recommend specifying an amplification factor of 2.0 or more. Because amplified readings are bigger than true velocity pressure, the "pressure" in the box math below is the sensor signal, not a pitot reading.

Siemens publishes a flow-balancing formula for one of its controllers that puts the pressure under a square root: Kf = Q divided by the square root of (250 times dP). Nailor and Titus define K as the airflow that produces a 1.0 in. w.c. signal. Together those say the same thing: airflow equals K times the square root of the signal. Double the pressure and airflow rises only about 41 percent. (Constants differ by maker, so use the one your controller expects.)

Worked example: K-factor, minimum and hood check

Our own example (an example K-factor, not a maker's value; real values depend on the box and sensor). Titus lists K-factors from 273 for a size 4 to 7176 for a 24 by 16 inlet, so a K of 800 is in a plausible range.

Step 1, airflow from a reading. Our own: K = 800 and signal = 0.25 in. w.c. The square root of 0.25 is 0.5. Airflow = 800 × 0.5 = 400 cfm.

Step 2, pressure needed for a 200 cfm minimum. Our own: 200 ÷ 800 = 0.25. Squared, 0.25 × 0.25 = 0.0625 in. w.c. So half the airflow (400 down to 200) takes the signal from 0.25 to 0.0625, a quarter of the pressure. That is why low minimums are hard to measure.

Step 3, corrected K from a hood. Our own: the controller shows 800 cfm and the hood reads 760 cfm. New K = 800 × 760 ÷ 800 = 760. Check: 760 × 0.5 = 380 cfm at the same 0.25 signal, which is 95 percent of the old 400 cfm, matching 760 ÷ 800. Follow your controller maker's procedure for entering the correction, because some controllers store a K, an area or a multiplier, and the one-point check says nothing about the other points.

Why low minimums are hard to read

Price's engineering page says commercial pressure sensors lose accuracy below about 0.02 in. w.c. of velocity pressure and that some controllers read down to 0.004 in. w.c. with careful calibration. It adds that 400 fpm makes only 0.01 in. w.c. Taylor Engineering's slides say the lowest controllable signal depends on the controller's transducer accuracy and analog-to-digital resolution, quote most controllers at about plus or minus 10 percent at 0.003 in. w.c., and give sample minimums at 0.004 in. w.c. of 58 cfm for an 8 inch box and 91 cfm for a 10 inch box. Taylor also argues catalog minimums are conservative, at roughly 0.03 in. w.c. and about 30 percent of design cfm. Our own comparison: our 0.0625 in. w.c. example sits above Price's 0.02 floor, but a minimum of 80 cfm on the same box (one tenth of K) needs only (80 ÷ 800)² = 0.01 in. w.c., below that floor. Always compute the signal your minimum produces before trusting it.

A Siemens support note shows the other side: below a configurable zero-flow threshold (default 0.008 in. w.c. in that controller) the controller reports no flow at all.

Minimum ventilation, heating maximum and dual maximum

The UC Berkeley Center for the Built Environment paper (Kaam, Raftery, Cheng and Paliaga) explains dual-max logic: separate maximums for cooling and heating, so airflow can rise in heating once discharge air is warm enough. It reports dual-max allows deadband minimums of about 10 to 20 percent of cooling airflow versus 30 to 50 percent for single-max, and that code ventilation was below 11.2 percent of cooling airflow in half the zones studied. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory Modelica library, which implements ASHRAE Guideline 36 terminal unit sequences, carries separate cooling and heating maximum airflow limits and a minimum ventilation calculation. PNNL's building retuning guide flags minimum settings above about 35 percent as candidates for review. Setpoints come from the design, the sequence and the ventilation requirement; this lesson sets none.

Reheat and discharge temperature checks

PNNL suggests discharge air temperature sensors on reheat boxes. A leaking reheat valve shows as a space that overheats while cooling; a small difference between air handler discharge and box discharge temperature while reheat is supposed to be off points to leakage. The Berkeley paper kept measured discharge temperatures under 90°F in heating to avoid stratification, and the LBNL library defaults to a discharge limit of 11 K (about 20°F, our own conversion) above the zone heating setpoint. Electric reheat needs an airflow switch and safety limits; the Florida Tech facilities standard lists them. See PID control loops for valve hunting.

Common faults

  • Tubing. Trane's troubleshooting article says to confirm the high and low tubes are in the right place and attached, then check for clogs, plugs, leaks or a clogged flow ring, and verify against the box's delta-P chart.
  • Dampers that will not seal. Trane lists a damper that does not close tightly among causes of a failed zero-flow calibration.
  • Wrong box size. Titus shows K changes with inlet size, and that using an old K with a new sensor changed a size 8 reading by 2.6 percent. Our own: with Titus values of 904 (size 8) and 448 (size 6), a size 8 box set up as size 6 would report about 50 percent of real flow (448 ÷ 904 = 0.496).
  • Drift. PNNL notes an out-of-calibration flow station can leave actual flow well above measured flow and cause overcooling. See BAS sensor calibration and drift.
  • Supply pressure. A starved box cannot hit setpoint; see duct static pressure reset.

Field card: VAV Box Airflow Calibration

Airflow = K times the square root of the sensor signal.

  • The sensor reads a pressure difference; K turns it into cfm.
  • Half the airflow needs a quarter of the signal.
  • Example: K 800, 0.25 in. w.c. gives 400 cfm; 200 cfm needs 0.0625 in. w.c.
  • Corrected K = K × measured ÷ displayed.
  • Check tubing, flow ring, damper seal and box size before changing K.
  • Use the maker's procedure; do not adjust K to hide a low-flow limit.
