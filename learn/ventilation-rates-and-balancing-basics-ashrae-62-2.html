Ventilation Rates and Balancing Basics (ASHRAE 62.2)

Last updated: 2026-10-16

How much fresh air does a home need, and how do you know a ventilation system is actually delivering it? ASHRAE 62.2 is the residential standard people cite most. This page summarizes public descriptions of it. It is not the standard itself.

Read this first

The standard is copyrighted and is revised on a cycle, and what applies to a job depends on the edition your local code or program adopts. Everything below comes from public summaries of the 2013 and 2016 editions, so treat it as orientation and verify against the adopted edition, the authority having jurisdiction and the equipment instructions. We do not reproduce the standard.

Scope in one line

A state weatherization program summary says the 2013 edition applies to single-family and multifamily dwellings up to three stories above grade, including manufactured and modular homes. An industry summary of the 2016 edition says the standard was retitled to cover ventilation and indoor air quality in residential buildings and now covers multifamily buildings of all heights. The title and scope changed between editions, which is why you check the edition.

The whole-building rate

Public summaries give the continuous whole-building rate as 0.03 cfm per square foot of floor area plus 7.5 cfm per person, with the number of people estimated as bedrooms plus one. One summary works an example of a roughly 1,500 square foot two-bedroom home that comes to about 68 cfm in that summary (the formula as stated gives 67.5: 1,500 x 0.03 = 45, plus 3 people x 7.5 = 22.5), and another example of a 2,000 square foot three-bedroom home near 90 cfm. A table method exists for homes up to a stated size and bedroom count, and you add 7.5 cfm per extra person if the home has more occupants than the table assumes. The standard also allows a credit for natural leakage in some cases, so a leaky home needs less mechanical air than a tight one, and existing homes use a worksheet for this. Whether an authority sets a different number is a local question.

Local exhaust: kitchens and baths

The 2013 summary describes bathroom fans rated for at least 20 cfm continuous or 50 cfm intermittent, tested as installed, and kitchen exhaust of 100 cfm intermittent or a continuous alternative that reaches five air changes per hour. The 2016 summary says kitchen ventilation moved to separate requirements and does not list the values, so check the edition. Intermittent controls must be easy for occupants to find and use, and the summaries list sound limits for fans, in sones, with a lower limit for whole-building fans than for local exhaust.

System types

A training page on whole-house ventilation lists three families. Exhaust-only systems pull air out and let outdoor air leak in, which is simple but uncontrolled. Supply-only systems bring filtered air in and slightly pressurize the house. Balanced systems move equal amounts in and out, usually with a heat or energy recovery ventilator. The standard asks that design documents identify which type is installed. For recovery equipment see ERV vs. HRV.

Measure, do not guess

The 2013 summary says installed airflow should be measured and controls verified and labeled, and that ducting outside the pressure boundary should leak very little. A design article says each ventilation fan should be commissioned by measuring airflow against the target. Low flow often means high duct static pressure or a fan starved of makeup air. That article also says balancing supply registers to design airflow is part of commissioning and is often skipped.

  • Record the design airflow and the measured airflow side by side.
  • Check filters, hoods, dampers and duct kinks before changing settings.
  • For balanced systems, compare supply and exhaust. A large mismatch can pressurize or depressurize the house.
  • Leave the occupant a plain description of how to run and maintain the system.

Ventilation cannot do everything

Ventilation dilutes indoor pollutants. It does not replace source control, filtration or dehumidification. A trade article warns that neither a heat recovery nor an energy recovery unit removes the moisture a house generates. See Indoor Air Quality and Ventilation Basics and Airflow and Static Pressure Basics.

Order of authority

The rule that overrides this page: the manual for the exact unit in front of you, your employer's procedures and the codes where you work always govern. This lesson explains concepts only, and every number below is a labeled example from one source, not a setting to copy.

Field card: Ventilation Rates and Balancing Basics (ASHRAE 62.2)

Know the edition, size the rate, then measure what you built.

  • Public summaries give the whole-building rate as 0.03 cfm per sq ft plus 7.5 cfm per person (bedrooms plus one).
  • Natural leakage can be credited in some cases, so tight homes need more mechanical air.
  • Local exhaust for kitchens and baths has its own requirements that changed between editions.
  • Measure installed airflow and compare to design. Balance supply and exhaust.
  • Always verify against the adopted edition and the local authority.
