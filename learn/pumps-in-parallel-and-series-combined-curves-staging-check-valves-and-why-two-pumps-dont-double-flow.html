Pumps in Parallel and Series: Why Two Pumps Don't Double Flow

Last updated: 2026-10-16

It seems obvious that two pumps move twice the water. They do not, and the reason sits on the pump curve. This lesson shows how parallel and series curves are built, works one example with numbers, and covers the check valves, staging and standby habits that go with a two-pump setup.

How the curves combine

Start from the single-pump curve. Bell & Gossett (Xylem) technical brochure TEH-1109A describes the parallel curve this way: pick several head values on one pump's curve, double the flow at each, and connect the points. For a series pair you do the opposite: double the head at each flow. The Hydraulic Institute's pump data tool says the same thing in words: in series the heads add, and in parallel the flows add.

Either way the operating point is still where the combined pump curve crosses the system curve. If that idea is new, read Reading Pump Curves and System Curves first.

Why parallel does not double the flow

The parallel curve doubles flow at equal head, but the system does not stay at equal head. Friction head climbs with the square of flow, according to the same Xylem brochure, so the system curve steepens and the two pumps meet it at a lower flow than double. The Hydraulic Institute article on parallel pump optimization puts it plainly: parallel pumps add flow at each head, but higher flow means more friction loss, so the operating point shifts left. It adds that the less friction a system has, the greater the flow gain from a second pump. Pump systems dominated by static lift see the biggest gain, while friction-dominated systems see little change in flow. Most closed hydronic loops have no static lift to speak of, so they are on the friction side of that rule.

Series is the mirror image. The Hydraulic Institute data tool says that in a friction-dominated system, added series pumps bring a significant rise in head with a smaller rise in flow.

Worked example (our own arithmetic)

Our own numbers: a system with zero static head has the curve H = 0.000025 × Q² (ft, gpm). One pump has the curve H = 100 − 0.00001 × Q².

One pump. Set pump head equal to system head.

  • 100 − 0.00001 Q² = 0.000025 Q²
  • 100 = 0.000035 Q², so Q² = 2,857,143
  • Q = about 1,690 gpm
  • H = 0.000025 × 2,857,143 = about 71.4 ft

Two identical pumps in parallel. Each pump carries Q/2, so the pump head is 100 − 0.00001 × (Q/2)² = 100 − 0.0000025 Q².

  • 100 − 0.0000025 Q² = 0.000025 Q²
  • 100 = 0.0000275 Q², so Q² = 3,636,364
  • Q = about 1,907 gpm
  • H = 0.000025 × 3,636,364 = about 90.9 ft
  • Each pump carries about 953 gpm, and 100 − 0.00001 × 953² = about 90.9 ft, which checks.

Flow gain. (1,907 − 1,690) ÷ 1,690 = 0.128, so about 12.8 percent more flow from 100 percent more pumps. Head rises about 27 percent (71.4 to 90.9 ft), so the extra pump mostly pushes harder against friction. Our own note: if one of the two pumps drops out, the survivor slides from about 953 gpm back out to about 1,690 gpm on its own curve, nearly double what it was carrying. That is why the sources below warn about checking the single-pump case.

The U.S. Department of Energy's tip sheet on parallel pumping shows a similar pattern in a larger example: a second pump raised flow 25 percent (2,000 to 2,500 gpm) while power rose 62 percent, and each pump's efficiency fell from 90 to 80 percent in that example. DOE says to run the minimum number of pumps the system needs and to compare multiple pumps against one pump with speed control.

Check valves on each discharge

When one of two parallel pumps is off, system pressure would push water backward through it. Xylem's brochure says a check valve at each pump discharge prevents that backflow through the idle pump. Armstrong's typical pumping-package specification calls for a combination valve on the discharge of each pump that includes a spring-closure, non-slam check along with shut-off and throttling. The Xylem brochure adds a related risk: with dissimilar pumps, if the system curve crosses above the smaller pump's shutoff head, its check valve closes and long dead-heading can severely damage it.

Single-pump risks and standby

Xylem lists what happens when one pump runs alone: flow rises and head falls, so piping, strainers and valves must handle the higher flow; brake horsepower usually rises, and in its example a 40 hp motor would be overloaded at about 50 hp; and if the system curve does not intersect the single-pump curve, the pump can run past its end point with cavitation and failure. Check the motor and the curve for the one-pump case, not just the two-pump design point.

For standby, Xylem says a parallel pair can deliver roughly 70 to 90 percent of full flow on one pump in most cases, with its examples at about 75 and 82 percent. Armstrong's specification describes duty/standby operation with alternation between pumps by hours of operation, or manually, and automatic omission of a failed pump. Alternating gives the standby pump run time so it is known to work.

Staging and unequal pumps

The Hydraulic Institute says to start extra pumps only when demand requires them. With variable speed, one pump can run alone at low demand and ramp up, and additional pumps come on after that, as long as the pumps' heads stay well matched. Armstrong's specification says its controller stages pumps up and down to meet demand and changes speed to hold a differential pressure setting. For the drive side, see Variable Frequency Drives on Pumps and Fans.

Match the pumps. The Hydraulic Institute says parallel pumps should have similar shutoff heads and similar best-efficiency flows; otherwise the higher-head pump does most of the work and the other may put zero flow into the system, which can lead to failure. DOE adds that identical pumps with drooping curves should not run in parallel at variable flow when demand can approach zero.

Field card: Pumps in Parallel and Series

Parallel adds flow at equal head. Series adds head at equal flow.

  • Parallel curve: double the flow at each head. Series curve: double the head at each flow.
  • In a friction-dominated system a second parallel pump gives much less than double flow.
  • Our example: 1,690 gpm with one pump, about 1,907 gpm with two, a 12.8 percent gain.
  • Put a check valve on each pump discharge so an idle pump does not backflow.
  • Check motor load and curve end point for the one-pump case.
  • Match pump shutoff heads; alternate duty and standby.
