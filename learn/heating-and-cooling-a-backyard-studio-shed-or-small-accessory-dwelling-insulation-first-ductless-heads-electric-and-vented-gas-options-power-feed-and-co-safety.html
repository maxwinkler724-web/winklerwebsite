Heating and Cooling a Backyard Studio, Shed or Small ADU

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A backyard studio, a converted shed or a small accessory dwelling unit is a tiny building with its own weather problems. Heating and cooling it is less about picking a box and more about the order of the work: shell first, then equipment, then power, then the small details such as drains and moisture.

Insulate and air-seal first

A small building has a lot of surface for its volume, so leaks and thin walls show up fast. The Oak Ridge National Laboratory insulation fact sheet says to stop air leaks before adding insulation, because insulation can hide leaks and make them harder to reach. Typical leak paths are gaps around electrical boxes, hatches, and plumbing or wiring penetrations. In a studio that also means the door sweep, the sill plate, and the gaps around the window. Seal first, then insulate walls, ceiling and floor.

The same sheet warns that wet insulation performs poorly, and that adding insulation changes wall temperatures, which can create new places for moisture to condense. Compressing a batt into a cavity that is too shallow also lowers its R-value. So the shell is a design job, not just a shopping list. Local permits may apply to a conversion or a new unit, so check with the local building department before you start.

Why extending the house ducts rarely works

It sounds cheap: run a duct across the yard. In practice it rarely works. The U.S. Department of Energy says duct losses can exceed 30 percent of space-conditioning energy, especially with ducts in an unconditioned space, and a buried or exposed run across a yard is about as unconditioned as it gets. On top of that, a house system is sized for the house. Feeding a few hundred more square feet of load through a long run can starve the house rooms, and the studio will want heat or cooling when the house system is off or on a different schedule. The studio also needs its own thermostat and its own control. A separate small system is usually the better answer.

The options and their trade-offs

  • Small ductless heat pump. A wall or ceiling head inside, an outdoor unit, and a thin line set. The Department of Energy says the outdoor unit can sit up to 50 feet from the head, the connection needs only about a three-inch wall opening, and it does both heating and cooling. It also says oversized or poorly placed heads can short-cycle. Upfront cost is higher than other choices. A load calculation (the ENERGY STAR page names Manual J) is how a contractor checks the size.
  • Through-wall or window unit. Lower first cost and no refrigerant work, but it needs a wall or window opening, makes more noise, and usually gives less even comfort. Many are cooling only. If it has heat, it is often electric resistance.
  • Electric baseboard or panel heaters. Simple, no combustion, no outdoor unit. But ENERGY STAR says certified mini splits use up to 60 percent less energy than standard electric radiators, so running costs are higher. They give no cooling. See Electric Space Heaters: Safety, Real Cost and When They Make Sense.
  • Vented gas heaters. A sealed-combustion or direct-vent heater sends exhaust outdoors through a vent made for it. It adds a gas line, a vent through the wall, and a gas supply to a building that may not have one. This is a job for a qualified installer who follows the maker's instructions and local rules.

Carbon monoxide: what not to do

The Consumer Product Safety Commission says outdoor heaters, grills and generators should never be used indoors, because they can cause deadly carbon monoxide buildup in minutes. That includes a patio heater or a propane heater from a worksite in a shed. The CPSC also says unvented gas or propane heaters pose the greatest carbon monoxide risk because they need a constant supply of fresh air, and some areas have outlawed them. The EPA lists unvented kerosene and gas space heaters as sources of carbon monoxide, nitrogen dioxide and particles, and describes carbon monoxide as colorless and odorless, with symptoms that are easy to mistake for the flu. A tight, well-insulated small room makes this worse, not better, because there is little natural leakage to carry exhaust away. Use only heating equipment approved for indoor use, and ask the local building department what alarms and venting rules apply. See Outdoor Gas Appliances at Home.

The power feed needs an electrician

A studio is not a long extension cord. It needs a feeder from the house panel, usually to a small subpanel in the studio, sized to the whole studio load: heating, lights, outlets and any other equipment. The house panel may or may not have spare capacity. That depends on a load calculation, the size of the house service, and how the feeder is routed and protected outdoors. Those are electrical code questions and the answer depends on the house. Have a licensed electrician size and install it, and ask the local building department about permits. Our lesson Will My Electrical Panel Handle a Heat Pump? shows how service capacity is checked. Note also that a heat pump head can draw far less than electric resistance heat for the same output, which helps a small feed go further.

Condensate, moisture and freeze risk

Condensate. The Department of Energy says a ductless unit's condensate must drain outdoors, so plan a drain location, and the drain needs a downhill path. ENERGY STAR also notes outdoor units release water when they defrost in winter, which can form icy patches, so keep the outdoor unit and its drain away from walkways and doors.

Ventilation and moisture. A small, sealed room has no accidental airflow to dry it out. Cooking, showering, drying clothes or just several people breathing can raise humidity, and the ORNL sheet notes that moisture can cause mold and structural damage. A bath fan or kitchen exhaust vented outdoors helps; see Bathroom Exhaust Fans for the idea. If the room will be a bedroom or have a bath, ask the local building department what ventilation is required.

Freeze risk when empty. If the studio has a sink, a toilet or any water line, an unoccupied building with the heat off can freeze pipes. Decide in advance: keep a low setpoint, or drain and winterize the plumbing. A heat pump that is switched off, or a feed that trips, leaves the pipes unprotected, and a freeze can crack a pipe without any warning until it thaws. Ask a plumber what is right for your setup.

Worked example (our own): is a small heat pump enough?

This is our own example. All inputs are assumptions we chose for a typical insulated studio, not measurements. Heat loss is UA times the temperature difference, where UA is area divided by R-value, summed over each surface.

  • Studio 12 by 16 ft, 8 ft walls, floor and flat ceiling 192 sq ft each.
  • Walls: gross 56 ft of perimeter times 8 ft is 448 sq ft; minus a 12 sq ft window and a 20 sq ft door leaves 416 sq ft at an assumed whole-wall R-13. UA = 416 / 13 = 32.0.
  • Ceiling: 192 sq ft at R-30. UA = 192 / 30 = 6.4.
  • Floor: 192 sq ft at R-19. UA = 192 / 19 = 10.1.
  • Window: 12 sq ft at an assumed U of 0.35. UA = 4.2.
  • Door: 20 sq ft at R-3. UA = 20 / 3 = 6.7.
  • Air leakage: volume 192 x 8 = 1,536 cu ft at an assumed 0.5 air change per hour is 1,536 x 0.5 / 60 = 12.8 cfm. Using the usual 1.08 factor, UA = 1.08 x 12.8 = 13.8.

Total UA = 32.0 + 6.4 + 10.1 + 4.2 + 6.7 + 13.8 = 73.2 Btu/h per degree F. For an assumed design day of 15 F outside and 70 F inside, the difference is 55 F. Load = 73.2 x 55 = about 4,000 Btu/h, which is about 1.2 kW (4,000 / 3,412) if it were all electric resistance.

Now compare. Assume a small head with a nominal 9,000 Btu/h rating that delivers about 70 percent of that at 15 F: 9,000 x 0.70 = 6,300 Btu/h. That 70 percent is our assumption; read the maker's low-temperature capacity table for a real unit. Even so, 6,300 is well above 4,000, so a small head covers the load with margin, and the margin is a reminder that the head must not be so oversized that it short-cycles at mild temperatures.

Now the cost of skipping the shell. If the walls were only R-4, wall UA = 416 / 4 = 104, up by 72. Total UA = 145.2 and the load is 145.2 x 55 = about 8,000 Btu/h, double. Insulation first is how the equipment gets smaller and the power feed gets smaller. A real design should be done by a contractor with a proper load calculation.

Field card: Heating and Cooling a Backyard Studio

Shell, then equipment, then power.

  • Air-seal first, then insulate walls, ceiling and floor.
  • Do not extend house ducts across the yard; duct losses can exceed 30 percent.
  • A small ductless heat pump gives heat and cooling from one outdoor unit.
  • Never use outdoor heaters indoors or unvented gas or propane heaters; carbon monoxide builds fast.
  • The yard feed and subpanel are electrician work, with local permits as required.
  • Plan the condensate drain, humidity control and freeze protection for an empty building.
  • Our own example: about 4,000 Btu/h loss at 15 F for an insulated 12 by 16 ft studio.
