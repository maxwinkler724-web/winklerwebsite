Boiler Relief Valve Dripping or Discharging: Causes, Testing and Replacement

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A drip from the small brass valve on a hot water boiler is the system telling you something. The valve is doing its job. This lesson explains what it is rated for, why it opens, what the makers say about piping, testing and replacing it, and how to tell a pressure problem from a bad valve.

Safety first. Never cap, plug or adjust a relief valve, and never close a valve in front of it. Discharge water can scald. Your boiler manual and the valve tag rule over this page.

What the valve is

A hot water heating boiler carries an ASME-rated relief valve, usually set at 30 psi in homes. Conbraco (Apollo) explains that the ASME "HV" mark means the valve was designed and tested to the ASME boiler code for heating boilers, and the "NB" mark means the National Board has certified the capacity printed on the nameplate. That capacity is in Btu/h. A residential valve in the Watts 174A line, for example, is offered with settings from 30 to 150 psi, and Watts calls it emergency safety relief that must not be used as an operating control.

Because it is a last line of defense, it is meant to open rarely. Watts adds one detail worth knowing: pressure builds toward the setting during thermal expansion, so a tiny discharge can happen even when nothing is broken. A valve that opens every day means something else is wrong.

Why it opens

1. Expansion tank trouble. This is the most common cause. Water expands as it heats, and the tank's air cushion gives it room. Amtrol's sizing literature assumes a tank precharged to the 12 psi fill pressure. If the air side is gone (a waterlogged tank) or the tank is too small, the extra water has nowhere to go. See Expansion Tanks and Fill Pressure for checking a tank.

2. A fill valve that lets pressure creep. The fill valve (pressure reducing valve) tops up the system. If it passes water when it should be closed, system pressure climbs even with a good tank. Watts also warns that overfilling with the fast-fill purge open, without watching the gauge, can exceed the relief setting and discharge. Watts does not describe creep in its sheet; that is the usual field explanation, so treat it as a lead to test, not a given. The fill valve lesson covers it.

3. An indirect water heater coil leak. An indirect tank has a heating coil inside it. Inspectapedia, a secondary source, says a leaking coil can cause unsafe pressure in the heating boiler and relief valve leaks. Which way water moves depends on the two pressures, so a technician should confirm it. See Indirect Water Heaters on a Boiler.

4. Overfiring or a control failure. If the limit controls fail and the burner keeps running, temperature and pressure climb. Watts notes that in a runaway the valve will discharge steam at or above the rate the boiler makes it. This is a service call, not a do-it-yourself fix.

5. The valve itself. Mineral deposits or debris on the seat can make a good valve weep at normal pressure. Conbraco recommends keeping normal operating pressure at least 10 psi or 25% of the set pressure (whichever is greater) below the setting, because less margin can cause seat leakage. Check the gauge when the weep happens. A weep at 12 psi cold points to the valve. A discharge at 28 psi hot points to the pressure sources above.

Why you never cap or plug it

Conbraco prints it in plain words in its instructions: do not cap, plug or obstruct the discharge pipe outlet. Weil-McLain's boiler manual says the same, and warns that not following its guidelines can keep the valve from working and cause severe injury, death or major property damage. Caleffi says the valve on the boiler supply must never be shut off and that no shut-off may sit between valve and boiler. A plugged valve turns an annoyance into a bursting hazard.

Discharge piping

The makers agree closely. Conbraco and Weil-McLain both call for a line at least as large as the valve outlet along its full length, kept short, sloped down so it drains, with no valve in it, ending in a plain unthreaded end where the discharge is visible and cannot freeze. Weil-McLain says to end the line at least 6 inches above a floor drain and to use material rated for 375°F or more. Conbraco asks that the line be supported on its own so the valve carries no weight. Caleffi limits its manifold to a discharge pipe of 2 m with no more than two elbows. Local plumbing and mechanical codes add rules, so confirm yours with the inspector. We did not read the code text itself for this lesson.

Testing and replacement

Watts says the boiler owner should trip the lever at least once a year so the waterways stay clear. Before lifting it, make sure a discharge line is connected, and expect hot water. Caleffi says to test its valve annually by hand with the system cold. Conbraco says to hold the lever open at least five seconds and release it so the valve snaps shut; if nothing comes out, shut the equipment off and call qualified service. Weil-McLain says that if the valve does not discharge freely, replace it. Watts makes the same point: no water on a lever test means the valve is stuck, so turn off the boiler and call a plumber.

A valve that keeps dripping after the lever test often has debris on the seat. Do not tighten it or hammer it shut. Replace it with a valve of the same set pressure and at least the same capacity, and use a licensed professional. Conbraco says repairs and adjustment belong to an authorized service facility.

On replacement intervals, none of the boiler valve instructions we read gave a fixed number of years. The only interval we found was in Watts instructions for a water heater temperature and pressure valve: inspection by a licensed plumber at least every three years. That is a different valve, so treat it as a reference point, not a boiler rule. Watts also says to inspect water system safety and control valves annually.

Matching capacity to the boiler

Watts tells installers the valve's Btu/h rating must be higher than the boiler's heating output, and the setting must be below the boiler's maximum working pressure. Conbraco says capacity must equal or exceed the equipment. Capacity grows with valve size and with set pressure. Watts lists the 174A at a 30 psi setting at 650,000 Btu/h in the 3/4 inch size, 1,005,000 in 1 inch and 1,682,000 in 1-1/4 inch. A 100,000 Btu/h boiler is well under a 3/4 inch valve. Never swap in a different pressure or a smaller valve to stop a drip.

Worked example: cold fill to hot

Take a 20 gallon hydronic system filled cold at 12 psi and heated from 40°F to 180°F. Amtrol gives a net water expansion of 0.0276 for that range, and a tank acceptance factor of 0.403 for a 12 psi minimum and 30 psi maximum.

  1. Extra water: 20 gal × 0.0276 = 0.552 gal.
  2. Smallest tank at 30 psi: 0.552 ÷ 0.403 = 1.37 gal.

With a working tank. Use absolute pressure: 12 psi gauge plus about 14.7 atmospheric is 26.7 psia (our assumption). A 2.0 gal tank starts with 2.0 gal of air at 26.7 psia. After taking 0.552 gal of water, the air is squeezed to 1.448 gal, so P = 26.7 × 2.0 ÷ 1.448 = 36.9 psia, or 22.2 psi gauge. A 4.0 gal tank leaves 3.448 gal of air: 26.7 × 4.0 ÷ 3.448 = 31.0 psia, or 16.3 psi gauge. Both stay under 30. The 2 gal tank does leave only about 7.8 psi of margin, less than Conbraco's 10 psi, so the larger tank is gentler on the valve.

With a failed tank. The air is gone, so the 0.552 gal has nowhere to go. Water barely compresses, so pressure shoots up to 30 psi and the valve lets the surplus out, roughly 0.55 gal (about 2.1 liters) on each heat-up. The fill valve then refills it, and the cycle repeats. That is the daily drip. This ignores the small stretch of pipes and any air in the system.

Field card: Boiler Relief Valve Dripping or Discharging: Causes, Testing and Replacement

A drip is a symptom. Find the pressure source.

  • Typical setting is 30 psi; valve Btu/h must exceed boiler output.
  • Check the gauge: cold pressure, hot pressure, and when the drip starts.
  • Causes: expansion tank, fill valve, indirect coil, overfiring, dirty seat.
  • Never cap, plug or close a valve in front of it.
  • Discharge line: same size, short, sloped, no valve, plain end, 6 in above drain (Weil-McLain).
  • Lever test at least yearly with a line attached; no flow means call a pro.
  • Example: 20 gal, 40 to 180°F = 0.552 gal extra water.
