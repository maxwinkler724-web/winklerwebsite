Cooling Your Home at Night With Window Fans: Placement, Limits and Safety

Last updated: 2026-11-04

On a summer evening the air outside often cools off before the house does. A couple of ordinary window fans can swap that cool air for the hot air stored indoors, and the only cost is the fans' electricity. This lesson covers how to set the fans up, when to skip the idea, and a worked airflow example.

When it works

The federal energy department's page on natural ventilation and cooling describes fresh-air cooling, also called night flushing, as using cool night air to cool the house and its thermal mass, so the home can stay comfortable into the next day. It says this works best where nights are cool and breezes are regular, and that fans can pull in cool outside air and spread it through the house. An older university extension bulletin puts the rule plainly: window or attic fans are very effective when outside air is cooler than the temperature inside. Our rule of thumb is simple: if it is cooler and drier outside than inside, ventilate. If not, keep the windows shut.

Setting up the fans

  • Intake on the cool side. Open windows on the shaded side of the house, often the north or east side in the evening, and on the lower floor. Air coming in there is the coolest available.
  • Exhaust upstairs or on the far side. Put fans facing outward in upstairs windows or at the far end of the house. Warm air collects high, so the fans remove the hottest air first.
  • Make a path. Cross-ventilation needs air to travel from an inlet to an outlet, so open interior doors along that path. The federal energy page notes that operable windows placed to take advantage of prevailing breezes can provide cross-ventilation.
  • Open more than one inlet. A university extension guide on whole-house fans says to open windows so the fan does not create strong suction in one spot. The same idea applies to a window fan: a small inlet starves the fan and cuts its airflow.
  • A fan blowing on you is not cooling the house. Pointing a fan at a sleeper is a comfort choice. The cooling comes from replacing the indoor air.

Closing up in the morning

Once the outdoor air warms to near the indoor temperature, turn off the fans and shut the windows, blinds and drapes on the sun side. The cooled walls, floors and furniture then absorb heat through the day, the idea in our thermal mass lesson. If you open windows at noon you let the stored cool back out.

Turn the air conditioning off while windows are open

Running the air conditioner with windows open tries to cool the outdoors and wastes energy. Set thermostat to off while the fans run, and switch back to normal when you close up, if you still need cooling.

Limits and when to skip it

  • Humidity. A university extension publication on whole-house fans says a fan does not reduce humidity. Running it in humid weather brings moisture indoors, which can encourage mold and mildew and makes the air conditioner work harder later. It works best when outdoor air is cool and dry. Cool but muggy night air can leave a house clammy.
  • Pollen. A federal health checklist advises that when pollen counts are high, you stay inside with the windows closed and use the air conditioning. If anyone at home has seasonal allergies, check the pollen forecast first.
  • Wildfire smoke and poor air. A federal environmental agency fact sheet says to stay inside with doors and windows closed during smoke. It adds that if you must open windows to cool off, close them once the indoor air is cool and then filter the air. Check the federal air quality map or your state or local air agency before opening up. See Wildfire Smoke Days.
  • Combustion appliances. The extension fan guide warns that running a large fan in a closed house can backdraft combustion appliances. Keep plenty of windows open so the fans do not pull flue gases into the house.

Safety

  • Child window falls. The federal consumer product safety agency estimates 5,600 children aged 12 and under were treated in emergency departments in 2024 after falling out of a window. It warns never to rely on screens to keep children in, and advises window stops so windows open no more than four inches, opening windows from the top when possible, and keeping furniture away from windows. A fan that needs a wide opening does not belong in a child's room.
  • Escape routes. The same agency advises window guards that adults can open easily in an emergency, for windows on the sixth floor and below.
  • Security. This one is our own advice: do not leave low windows wide open when no one is awake. Prefer upstairs windows for the fans, and fix the sash in its partly open position with a stop made for the purpose.
  • Cords. Also our own advice: route cords where no one can trip over them or where the sash cannot pinch them, and stop using any fan with a damaged cord or plug.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example. Every input is our assumption, not a measured value. A two-story house has 2,000 square feet of floor area and 8-foot ceilings. Two window fans face outward in upstairs windows. We assume each fan delivers 900 cubic feet per minute (cfm) once installed in a real window with a screen, which is less than a free-air label number. Lower-floor windows on the shaded side are open as inlets.

  1. Volume: 2,000 × 8 = 16,000 cubic feet.
  2. Airflow: 2 fans × 900 cfm = 1,800 cfm.
  3. Air changes per hour: 1,800 × 60 ÷ 16,000 = 6.75 air changes per hour.
  4. Time for one change: 16,000 ÷ 1,800 = about 8.9 minutes.
  5. Heat removed, using the common sensible-heat approximation (1.08 × cfm × temperature difference): if the indoor air averages 10 degrees F warmer than the incoming air, 1.08 × 1,800 × 10 = about 19,400 Btu per hour at first. That shrinks as the house cools toward the outdoor temperature.

Compare that with the sizing rule in a Midwestern state university extension guide: a whole-house fan should exchange the house's air every one to two minutes. For our 16,000 cubic foot house, that means 8,000 to 16,000 cfm, or 30 to 60 air changes per hour. Our two window fans give roughly one fifth of the lower figure, which is why they cool slowly and work best when run for several hours overnight. The federal energy page gives a similar rule of at least half the house volume in cfm, which is 8,000 cfm here. Our result is a rough estimate. Real flow drops with small inlets, closed doors and wind pressure.

Window fans versus a whole-house fan

A whole-house fan is one large fan, usually in the ceiling, that pulls air through open windows and exhausts it through the attic. It moves several times as much air and is quieter at low speed, but it needs attic vent area, a sealed cover in winter, and installation. The federal energy page says attic ventilation should be two to four times the normal vent area, about one square foot of net free area per 750 cfm of fan. Window fans cost little and can be moved, but they move less air and use up window openings. For the cost question see Whole-House Fans vs Attic Fans, and for large buildings see Night Flush and Night Purge Cooling.

Field card: Cooling Your Home at Night With Window Fans: Placement, Limits and Safety

Cooler and drier outside than inside, then open up. Otherwise close up.

  • Intake low on the cool, shaded side. Exhaust fans upstairs or on the far side.
  • Open interior doors to make a path. Open more than one inlet.
  • Fans off and windows shut once outdoor air warms up. AC off while windows are open.
  • Skip it on humid nights, high pollen days and smoky days. Check air quality first.
  • Child safety: window stops at four inches or less. Screens do not stop falls.
  • Our example: 2 fans × 900 cfm in 16,000 cubic feet is about 6.75 air changes per hour.
