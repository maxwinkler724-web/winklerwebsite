Reading Contact Ratings on HVAC Relays and Controls: Pilot Duty, Resistive Amps, FLA, LRA and Horsepower Ratings

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A relay that says 10 A on its label can still burn up when you hang a 9 A fan on it. The number on the label means something specific, and a motor does not behave like the load that number describes. This lesson shows how to read the rating types, why low-voltage controls switch coils instead of motors, and how to pick a replacement that fits the load.

Check the label on your part

Check the label on your part. Rating formats differ by maker and by standard. This page is general education. Read the actual device label or data sheet, and follow the equipment wiring diagram and the rules where you work before you change a control.

The rating types, side by side

A contact rating is a promise about one kind of load at one voltage. The chart below compares a typical label with a motor load. Here is what each rating type means.

  • Resistive amps. The contacts may carry and switch this current into a plain heating element. Current rises instantly to its steady value and there is no stored magnetic energy. A federal technical guide on relays describes a contact rating as the electrical load on the contacts, expressed in terms of closing surge current plus steady-state and breaking values, so even the "simple" number depends on the load behind it.
  • Pilot duty (VA). A rating in volt-amperes for switching small inductive loads such as relay and contactor coils, valve coils and solenoids. It covers the pulse of current while a magnet pulls in, which is larger than the current once the coil is sealed in. Thermostat and control contacts are usually rated this way. We use pilot duty in this lesson as the rating for coils, not motors.
  • Full-load amps (FLA). The current a motor draws at its rated output while running. A motor-rated device lists this, or a horsepower rating that stands in for it.
  • Locked-rotor amps (LRA). The current the motor draws in the instant it starts, before the rotor turns. A Michigan State University extension tech note says locked-rotor current is typically five to eight times full-load current for most induction motors. An older reprint of the National Electrical Code motor article lists a conversion value of 58.8 A for a 1/2 hp single-phase motor at 115 V.
  • Horsepower rating. The device maker has already tested the contacts against the starting and running current of motors of that size at that voltage. Article 430 of the National Electrical Code ties motor controllers to horsepower ratings, so a horsepower mark is the cleanest way to show a device is meant for a motor.

Why a thermostat switches a coil, not a motor

A wall thermostat has small contacts and light wiring meant for low-voltage control current. A relay or contactor coil is a small inductive load. Assume a 24 V coil that draws 5 VA (our assumption). The current is 5 VA ÷ 24 V = about 0.21 A. The thermostat only has to handle that, while the relay or contactor, built with heavier contacts, handles the heavy line-voltage load. This is also why the transformer VA matters; see Relays, Transformers and 24V Control.

Inductive loads are hard on contacts for two reasons. At closing, a motor draws its locked-rotor inrush. At opening, the collapsing magnetic field tries to keep current flowing and produces an arc. The federal relay guide says that opening an inductive DC load forces stored energy to be dissipated as arcing at the contacts unless something absorbs it, and that on AC the arc usually ends at the current zero crossing. It also says closure damage is usually caused by current surges and that a microscopic weld or bridge often forms at the point of closure.

Line-voltage thermostats and resistive heaters

Some thermostats switch line voltage directly, mostly for electric baseboard and similar heaters. Their label gives amps (and sometimes watts) at 120 V and 240 V. Heaters are resistive, so the arithmetic is simple. A 1500 W heater at 120 V draws 1500 ÷ 120 = 12.5 A, which is over a 10 A rating. A 2000 W heater at 240 V draws 2000 ÷ 240 = about 8.3 A (our arithmetic). Do not use that same thermostat on a motor unless its label shows a horsepower rating. A resistive amp rating does not describe a motor's starting current. For the heater side of this, see Electric Baseboard Heater Not Working: Thermostat, Limit and Breaker.

Worked example: a 1/2 hp, 120 V exhaust fan

Our own example. We want a control relay to switch a 1/2 hp, 120 V single-phase exhaust fan. Assumption: the relay on the shelf is marked 10 A resistive at 120 V and nothing else. We have no nameplate for the fan, so we use published values; on a real job, use the fan's own nameplate.

  1. Running current. The Michigan State tech note gives 9.8 A at 120 V for a 1/2 hp single-phase motor. That is a table-style value, not a measurement.
  2. Starting current. The older code reprint lists 58.8 A for the same motor class at 115 V. Our check: 58.8 ÷ 9.8 = 6.0, which sits inside the five to eight times range in the extension note.
  3. Compare to the label. Running: 9.8 A against 10 A leaves only 0.2 A (2 percent) of margin, and a resistive rating does not claim to cover motor use. Starting: 58.8 ÷ 10 = 5.9, so the contacts would be asked to close on almost six times their label for a moment, with every start.
  4. Decide. This relay is not suitable for the fan. Use a contactor or a motor-rated relay whose label shows at least 1/2 hp at 120 V (or an FLA of at least 9.8 A and an LRA of at least 58.8 A). Then let the control relay, thermostat or timer switch only that device's 24 V coil. In the chart above, the label bar looks fine next to the running bar, but the starting bar shows the real problem.

Also confirm the branch circuit, disconnect and overload protection separately. Those follow the motor article of the National Electrical Code and the fan nameplate, and are covered in Reading an HVAC Nameplate: MCA, MOCP, RLA, LRA and Motor Starters and Overload Relays.

Welded and pitted contacts

When a relay is undersized, the damage is usually visible. Each close can form a tiny weld at the contact point, and each open tears it apart and moves or loses metal. The federal guide says that on AC there is usually a net loss of contact material, and on DC metal often transfers from one contact to the other. Over time the faces roughen and pit, resistance rises, the contacts run hot and may stick closed. A fan that will not shut off, or a coil that clicks but the load does not start, are common signs. The same guide says that contact protection can raise life expectancy by as much as three orders of magnitude, which is why snubbers and properly rated parts matter. Replacing the part and not the rating mismatch brings the failure back.

Picking a replacement relay

  1. Match the coil voltage and type (24 V AC or DC, 120 V AC and so on). Wrong coil voltage fails or overheats the coil.
  2. Match the contact arrangement: normally open or closed, and how many poles.
  3. Read the rating at your voltage and load type. A load that is a motor needs FLA and LRA or a horsepower mark. A heater needs a resistive rating at least equal to the load current. A coil needs a pilot duty rating in VA.
  4. Do not go below the old part's ratings. Going up is fine if the size, coil and base fit.
  5. Check that the new coil's VA does not overload the 24 V transformer.

Contactor construction is covered in Capacitors and Contactors, and the ladder drawings that show where each contact sits are in Reading a Ladder Diagram. For the relay boards that combine a transformer and relay, see Fan Center Relay Boards and Isolation Relays.

Field card: Reading contact ratings

Match the rating to the load type, not just the amps.

  • Resistive amps: heaters only.
  • Pilot duty VA: coils and solenoids.
  • FLA and LRA, or hp: motors. LRA is roughly 5 to 8 times FLA.
  • Thermostat switches the 24 V coil; the contactor switches the motor.
  • 1/2 hp, 120 V fan: about 9.8 A running and 58.8 A starting; a 10 A resistive relay is not enough.
  • Welded or pitted contacts mean the rating did not match the load.
  • Replacement: coil voltage, contact form, then rating for your load type.
