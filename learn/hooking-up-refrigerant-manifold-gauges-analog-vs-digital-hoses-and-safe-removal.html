Hooking Up Manifold Gauges: Hoses, Ports and Safe Removal

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Searching "how to hook up AC gauges" gets you a dozen videos and very little about why each step matters. This lesson covers the parts of a manifold, analog versus digital, the hoses, protective gear, connecting, reading, and taking the hoses off without dumping refrigerant on yourself or the sky.

Check the maker's manual first

Your manifold's manual and the equipment's service manual come first. The steps here are general, built from maker manuals and federal pages read in 2026. They are not a charging procedure, and they do not replace training or EPA 608 certification. Where makers disagree or are silent, ask your supervisor.

The parts of a manifold

A manifold is a block with valves and ports that lets you read pressure on two sides of a system and connect other tools. Most have three hose ports. The low-side port (blue) goes to the suction side and the high-side port (red) goes to the liquid line side. Fieldpiece's SMAN manual, for example, has you hand-tighten the low hose to the suction line service port and the high hose to the liquid line service port. The middle port, usually yellow, is the center or utility port. It connects to a recovery machine, a vacuum pump or a cylinder. Four-port models add a second large port for vacuum.

Each hose end that goes on the system has a service-port fitting. Many have a small valve core depressor inside. Yellow Jacket describes its SealRight low-loss fitting as one that "traps refrigerant in the hose when disconnected." The system side is a Schrader valve (like a tire valve) or a service valve with its own port. That core is what the fitting presses open.

Analog or digital

An analog manifold shows pressure on dial gauges, and its dials are printed with temperature scales for specific refrigerants. If the refrigerant is not on the dial, you need a separate chart. A digital manifold has pressure sensors and a built-in refrigerant list, so it shows pressure and the matching saturation temperature, and some add clamp temperature probes to calculate superheat. Testo describes its 550s as A3 and A2L compatible with over 90 stored refrigerants. Fieldpiece says new refrigerants arrive by firmware update, so keep the firmware current and confirm your refrigerant is on the list.

Whichever you own, check the pressure limits. The Fieldpiece SM380V manual says not to apply more than 800 psig to any port and lists a sensor range of 580 psig. Treat the lower number as your working ceiling. Also note the fluid: a manifold used on one refrigerant should be cleaned before use on another. Fieldpiece says to purge the manifold with nitrogen before connecting to a system with a different refrigerant.

For A2L systems, the Fieldpiece manual says to ground properly when testing A2L, A2 and A3 refrigerants and says only personnel trained in handling those refrigerants should test them. See R-454B and R-32 vs R-410A at the Gauges for how pressures compare, and Refrigerant Safety for the flammability groups.

Protective gear

Liquid refrigerant boils off fast and freezes what it touches. The Fieldpiece manual warns that liquid refrigerant can cause frostbite and that breathing high concentrations of vapor can block oxygen to the brain. Yellow Jacket's TITAN manual says to always wear eye and skin protection when working with refrigerants. Federal rules point the same way: OSHA 1910.133 requires appropriate eye or face protection when employees are exposed to hazards such as liquid chemicals, and 1910.138 requires suitable hand protection against hazards such as dangerous temperatures. In practice that means safety glasses or goggles and gloves. Never point a hose at skin.

Hoses, low-loss fittings and what they save

Use hoses made for refrigerant and check the rubber seals at both ends for damage. Fieldpiece says to inspect them, and a cracked seal leaks. Yellow Jacket's catalog rates its 1/4 inch PLUS II hose at 800 psi maximum working pressure with a 4000 psi minimum burst. It says the nylon barrier meets SAE J2196 for CFC, HCFC and HFC refrigerants. Ratings differ by maker and hose, so read the label on yours.

The same catalog lists how much liquid a foot of hose can hold: 0.29 ounce per foot for 1/4 inch PLUS II hose with R-410A at 80°F. A 5 foot hose full of liquid holds 5 × 0.29 = 1.45 ounces. A 25 foot hose holds 25 × 0.29 = 7.25 ounces. That is what could be lost, or must be recovered, if the hose empties every time it comes off. Short hoses and low-loss fittings that trap what is in the hose matter for the same reason: less refrigerant to release or recover.

One caution. Fieldpiece says not to evacuate through hoses with low-loss fittings, and to use the shortest, largest-diameter vacuum-rated hoses. See Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics.

Connecting, step by step

  1. Wear your glasses and gloves, and read the unit's data plate for the refrigerant.
  2. Close all manifold valves (Fieldpiece's superheat steps start with this) and make sure the service ports are clean.
  3. Hand-tighten the low hose to the suction line port and the high hose to the liquid line port, as Fieldpiece describes.
  4. Purge the hoses as the manual directs. Fieldpiece says to purge hoses as you open the high and low valves. Follow your manifold maker's steps for your model, and keep releases to the minimum.
  5. Let the system run steadily, then read.

On releases, EPA's Section 608 venting page says de minimis releases are allowed while making good-faith efforts to recapture refrigerant, and says that covers releases when connecting or disconnecting hoses. It is not permission to be careless. The venting and recovery rules are in EPA 608 Certification Basics.

Worked example: pressure to temperature to superheat

A pressure only becomes useful when you convert it. Say the label shows R-410A, and with the system running your low gauge reads 118.4 psig. The Chemours (Opteon) pressure-temperature guide lists 118.4 psig for R-410A at 40°F. That is the saturation temperature. You clamp a thermometer on the suction line and read 52°F.

Superheat = line temperature − saturation temperature = 52 − 40 = 12°F.

These numbers are an example only. Whether 12°F is right for a given unit depends on the metering device and conditions, and the target comes from the maker. See Pressure-Temperature Basics and Superheat and Subcooling Basics.

Taking the hoses off

This is the step beginners rush. The principle across makers is the same: refrigerant left in a hose does not belong in the air. Yellow Jacket says its low-loss fitting traps refrigerant in the hose on disconnect, and Fieldpiece says refrigerant remaining in the hoses must be recovered. Close the manifold valves first, and remove hoses slowly, aimed away from you. Your manifold and fitting makers set the exact order and whether the system should be running or off for each hose, so follow their steps. Yellow Jacket also warns that a worn fitting can blow back, and publishes rebuild kits to restore sealing.

Field card: Hooking Up Manifold Gauges

Glasses on, valves closed, read the label, convert, recover.

  • Blue is low side to suction, red is high side to liquid, yellow center goes to the pump, recovery machine or cylinder.
  • Wear eye and skin protection. Liquid refrigerant causes frostbite.
  • Match the manifold's refrigerant list or dial to the unit's refrigerant.
  • Stay under the lower of the manifold's two pressure ratings.
  • Pressure to saturation temp, then line temp minus saturation temp = superheat.
  • Do not pull a vacuum through low-loss fittings.
  • A2L: ground and use trained people. Never swap refrigerants in a manifold without purging.
  • Recover what stays in the hoses. Follow the maker's removal steps.
