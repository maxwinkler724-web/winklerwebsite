Working Across Languages on HVAC Jobs

Last updated: 2026-11-04

On many HVAC jobs the crew speaks more than one first language, and so do the customers. That is normal, and it can work well. The risk is not the second language. The risk is a message that one person thinks was understood and the other person did not get. This lesson covers simple habits that close that gap: plain words, showing instead of telling, repeating back, a short list of crew safety words, careful use of a phone translation app, and numbers written with their units.

Why it matters on the job

A federal worker-health research report on immigrant worker safety says language differences between workers, supervisors and coworkers are one of the most often named barriers to safety. It describes workers who could not tell a supervisor about an unsafe condition, and new workers who got their instructions secondhand through a coworker with no safety orientation at all. A university research group that studied 14 construction crews found that crews with weaker safety results tended to split into separate talk groups along language lines. The fix is not a single class. It is how the crew talks every day.

Plain words and short sentences

Trade talk is full of slang: "pull a vac," "jump it out," "kill it at the box." A worker who learned English or HVAC in another place may know the job well and still not know your shop's nicknames. Use the plain name of the thing and the plain action: "Turn off the power at the disconnect." One instruction per sentence. Say the object, then the action, then the reason if there is time. Avoid "don't not" and other double negatives, and avoid jokes or sarcasm when giving a safety instruction. Speak at a normal volume; louder does not translate.

Show instead of tell

The federal worker safety agency's guide for people who deliver worker training says learning by doing should be central, and that demonstrations, hands-on practice, photos, pictures and short role-plays help workers who read less well. On a job that means:

  • Point. Touch the valve, the breaker or the wire you mean, with the power off.
  • Photo. Take a picture of the nameplate, the panel or the part and point at it on the screen.
  • Sketch. Draw the piping or wiring on cardboard or the back of the ticket. A sketch with arrows beats a long description.
  • Do it once together. Show the step, then watch the other person do it.

Repeat back to confirm

"Do you understand?" almost always gets "yes," even when the answer is no. Instead, ask the person to tell you or show you what they will do. If what comes back does not match, show it again a different way. Do not move to the next step until it matches. Safety words every crew member should know

Some words have to work instantly, in every language on the crew, with no time to translate: stop, power off, gas, fire, get out, hot, help, do not touch, and the name of the emergency number. Build a short two-column card with the crew. Have a crew member who speaks each language fill in their column, read it aloud, and check that everyone says the word the same way. Also agree on a hand signal for stop. See the worked example below for a layout.

Translated safety training and safety data sheets

The federal worker safety agency's policy is that required training must be given in a language and vocabulary the workers can understand. Its 2010 interpretation adds that handing a worker reading material does not count if the worker cannot read it, and that if an employer usually gives work instructions in a certain language, safety training should be given the same way. The agency keeps a directory of Spanish-language training resources and points to trainers who teach in other languages. Getting training in place is the employer's job, not the new tech's, but you can ask for it.

For chemicals such as cleaners, refrigerant oils, sealants and brazing flux, the safety data sheet is the reference. The hazard communication standard requires the sheet to be in English, and the agency's side-by-side comparison of the rule notes that an employer may keep copies in other languages as well. If a crew member reads another language better, ask whether a copy in that language is on file, and still point to the English sheet's sections on hazards, first aid and protective equipment while you talk it through.

Phone translation apps: useful, with checks

A translation app on a phone can get a conversation started. It can also turn a number, a unit or a trade word into something else, and you may not be able to tell. Our own rules of thumb:

  • Use short, plain sentences in the app. Slang translates badly.
  • Do not trust the app with numbers. Write key numbers by hand, with the unit, and point to them.
  • Read back what the other person typed or said through the app, and have them confirm with a nod or by pointing.
  • Never rely on an app alone for a gas, electrical or carbon monoxide warning. Show the hazard, use the crew safety words, and get your lead.

Write numbers and units clearly on tickets

A number without a unit is a guess for the next reader in any language. "118" could be a pressure or a temperature. Write the unit every time: psig, °F, A, V. Keep the decimal point clear (14.2 A, not 142). Label which reading it is (suction, supply air, compressor). A ticket written this way can be read by a tech, a lead or a customer who speaks a different first language, because the numbers and units carry the meaning. If you speak two languages

The university crew study found that bilingual workers sat at the center of the crew's safety communication, linking groups that would otherwise not talk. That is a real strength for a new tech. Offer to fill in the safety word card, help a coworker through a toolbox talk, or explain a finding to a customer. Two cautions: you are not the official translator for required training unless your employer sets that up, and if you are not sure of a word, say so and show it instead of guessing.

Worked example (our own)

This is our own example with made-up readings. The numbers show the ticket format only; they are not targets or normal values for any system.

Part 1: safety word card layout. Two columns, one row per word. The left column is English. The right column is left blank for a crew member who speaks the other language to fill in and read aloud.

EnglishCrew language (filled in by the crew)
Stop__________
Power off__________
Gas smell__________
Get out__________
Hot, do not touch__________
Help__________

Part 2: units check on a sample ticket. Assumptions: one ticket with 8 key readings, written first in a hurry and then checked.

ReadingBeforeAfter
Suction pressure118 (no unit)118 psig
Discharge pressure365 (no unit)365 psig
Supply air temperature55 °F55 °F
Return air temperature75 (no unit)75 °F
Outdoor temperature90 °F90 °F
Compressor current14.2 (no unit)14.2 A
Fan motor current1.1 A1.1 A
Line voltage238 (no unit)238 V

Step by step:

  1. Count the readings: 8.
  2. Count the ones missing a unit before the check: suction, discharge, return, compressor, line voltage = 5.
  3. Readings with a unit before: 8 − 5 = 3. Check: supply, outdoor, fan = 3. Matches.
  4. Share missing before: 5 ÷ 8 = 0.625, or 62.5%.
  5. After the check, missing units: 0. Share missing: 0 ÷ 8 = 0%.
  6. Readings fixed: 5 − 0 = 5, which equals the 5 that were missing. Nothing was skipped.

Five of eight numbers on the hurried ticket could not be read safely by anyone else. A thirty-second check before the ticket is closed fixed all five.

Field card: Working Across Languages on HVAC Jobs

Say it plain, show it, have it repeated back, write it with units.

  • One instruction per sentence. Plain names, no shop slang.
  • Point, photo, sketch, then do it once together.
  • Ask them to tell or show you the step. "Do you understand?" is not a check.
  • Crew safety words in every crew language: stop, power off, gas, get out, hot, help.
  • Required training must be in a language and vocabulary workers understand.
  • The safety data sheet must be in English; other-language copies may also be kept.
  • Translation apps: short sentences, numbers written by hand, never alone for gas, electrical or CO.
  • Every number on a ticket gets its unit: psig, °F, A, V.
