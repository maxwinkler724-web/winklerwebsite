NJ Black Seal Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the NJ Black Seal and Boilers Guide

New Jersey licenses people who operate certain boilers. This lesson explains in general terms what a black seal license is and, more importantly, where to look for the official and current rules.

Read this first

Licensing rules are set by the state and they change. Fees, forms, exam arrangements, experience requirements and processing steps may be updated at any time. Nothing here is an official statement. Before you spend money or time, check the New Jersey Department of Labor and Workforce Development, and contact the bureau directly if anything is unclear.

What is a black seal?

In New Jersey trade talk, people say "black seal" when they mean a boiler operator license issued by the state. State materials describe several kinds of licenses, including stationary, power and refrigeration engineer licenses as well as boiler operator and special operator licenses. The state's rules say the black seal marks a boiler operator license, and they list both a low pressure boiler license and an operator-in-charge of high pressure boiler license among the classifications (the rules are shown on the Cornell Legal Information Institute site, which is a copy, not the state's own page). Which license applies to a given plant depends on the plant. For that reason you should rely on the state's definitions, not on what coworkers or training sellers say.

Who runs it

Boiler licensing is handled by the Mechanical Inspection Bureau of Boiler and Pressure Vessel Compliance, part of the Division of Public Safety and Occupational Safety and Health at the New Jersey Department of Labor and Workforce Development. The same bureau is responsible for the safety inspection of boilers and pressure vessels, so it is the one office to ask about both the equipment and the people who run it.

On the equipment side, the Bureau’s own information guide says boilers get a certificate inspection at least every 12 months, and hot water heating boilers internal every 24 months with an external check every 12 (we did not read the rule text that sets this, so confirm with the Bureau). The framed certificate is displayed where the equipment is. For owners and operators, see NJ Boiler Inspections, Certificates, Logbooks and Posting for Owners.

Where to find official information

  • The bureau's own page lists the license application, the renewal form, notices about testing and processing, and related materials.
  • The state's license application form lists license classifications and gives contact details for the bureau.
  • The state's administrative code contains the rules for eligibility, including the section on low pressure operator eligibility. Remember that a published copy may lag behind the official version.

All of these are linked in the sources below. Always prefer the state's own pages over a third party.

Typical steps, in general

Without promising any specifics, licensing programs of this kind usually involve choosing the right license class, showing qualifying experience or training, completing an application, passing an examination, and later renewing. Each step has its own rules and documents. Read what the state says for the class you want. If a rule is not clear to you, ask the bureau before you act, and keep a record of the answer.

Two details worth knowing. Exams are given monthly in Trenton and at other times when warranted, and the Bureau schedules you after your application is processed; we found no published calendar. Minimum passing averages are set by licence type and run from 60 to 80 percent in the rule (N.J.A.C. 12:90-8.15); we could not confirm which figure covers a black seal, so ask the Bureau. If you count training time toward eligibility, the state’s 2018 advisory says six weeks is 30 working days and three months is 12 weeks, and time counts only while the boilers are running and only as documented when it happens.

Training and study material

Many people prepare with classes, on-the-job training and study guides. Be a careful buyer. A study aid from any private company is not issued by the state and does not give you a license or guarantee you will pass. Make sure the material matches the license class you are seeking and check its date against current rules.

Beyond the license

A license shows you met the state's standard. It does not replace training on your own plant, manufacturer instructions, lockout procedures or your employer's safety program. Good operators keep learning about combustion, water treatment, controls and safety devices long after the exam.
