HVAC in Glass Plants: Hot-End Heat, Spot Cooling and Natural Draft

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A glass plant melts sand-based batch at roughly 2800°F and keeps it molten all the way to the forming machine. The building around that process gets hot, and the people at the hot end work inside the heat. This lesson walks through what an HVAC tech should understand about container and flat glass plants: radiant heat, spot cooling, natural draft, batch dust, control rooms and worker heat stress.

Where the heat comes from

A federal energy profile of the U.S. glass industry gives the melting furnace temperature for both container and flat glass as about 2800°F, with the container forehearth holding glass near 2000°F and furnace exhaust leaving through a stack at about 1200°F. The same profile says that, in a typical furnace energy balance, about 30 percent of the energy is lost through the furnace structure and about 30 percent goes up the stack. That structural loss is why the hot end is a heat problem: the walls, crowns and glass-carrying channels give off heat into the building all day.

Container plants shape glass into bottles and jars on forming machines fed by gobs of molten glass. Flat glass plants form a ribbon on a tin bath at about 1125°F, per the same profile, and then run it through an annealing lehr, an oven-like tunnel that cools the glass slowly. The profile shows both electric and gas-fired lehrs. Each of these steps leaks heat to the room, so a tech on a service call sees the same pattern in the hot end, the forming floor and the lehr area: a lot of warm surfaces and a lot of heat to remove.

Radiant heat is different from hot air

Federal worker safety pages list radiant heat as one of the environmental heat factors and name furnaces as an artificial source. They add that a weather report cannot tell you the conditions inside a building, so on-site measurement matters. The black globe on a wet-bulb globe temperature meter is what picks up radiant heat. A federal health hazard evaluation of a glass bottle plant shows the effect: at one forming machine the dry bulb was 87.0°F while the globe read 115.7°F, a difference of 28.7°F (115.7 minus 87.0). Air at 87°F was not the whole story; the hot surfaces were warming the person directly.

The practical lesson is that moving room air helps with convection and sweat evaporation, but it does not block radiation. Shields, reflective barriers and distance block radiation. Federal guidance for hot indoor work lists reflective shields, insulating hot surfaces, ventilation and fans, and air conditioning among the controls.

Operator spot cooling and cooled booths

Where a worker has to stand near a forming machine, plants commonly aim cooled or outside air at the station. The bottle plant evaluation above mentions large air movers called man coolers and evaporative coolers, and workers said the evaporative units were poorly maintained and some did not work. The report recommended putting them on a preventive maintenance schedule. That is an HVAC task in plain terms: filters, pads, water supply, belts and fans need the same routine care as any air handler, and a unit that is down on the hottest day is the one that matters.

Pulpits and control booths follow the same idea as the steel mill case in our lesson on cooling steel mill pulpits: shield the glass, keep the booth tight and supply filtered, cooled air. A cooled booth can also serve as the cool rest area that heat guidance calls for.

Roof ventilators and natural draft

Because hot-end heat loads are large, many plants lean on buoyancy. Warm air rises, leaves through roof ventilators, and is replaced by cooler outdoor air entering low. A university natural ventilation page gives the airflow relation as an effective opening area times the square root of 2 times gravity times the temperature difference over the indoor absolute temperature times the height between the openings. It describes a discharge coefficient of about 0.65 for windows and notes that a neutral plane marks where flow changes from inward to outward. Two cautions: the relation assumes one average indoor temperature, and real hot ends are warmer near the roof, so treat results as a first check, not a design. Our pressure lesson covers the same effect in tall buildings: building pressurization and stack effect.

Natural draft only works when low openings exist. If dust collectors or process exhaust pull air out of the building and nothing replaces it, the building goes negative and the draft stalls.

Batch house dust and makeup air

The energy profile describes raw materials being weighed, mixed by computer and charged to the furnace, with crushed recycled glass added. Those are dry, finely divided materials, so the batch house normally has dust collection at transfer points. In general terms, every cubic foot per minute a collector pulls from the room has to come back as makeup air, and in winter that makeup air needs tempering. Which collector, filter or ventilation rate a plant needs is a matter for its safety staff and the rules that apply there, so we leave those numbers to them. See also radiant heat in metal foundries for the same makeup air logic around hot exhaust hoods.

Control room pressurization

A furnace control room holds electronics that dislike heat and dust. The usual approach is a filtered, cooled supply with a bit more air supplied than leaves, so the room stays slightly positive and dusty hot-end air does not leak in. Doors that stand open defeat it, so door closers and a pressure indicator are the first service checks. For the general method see plant control room HVAC.

Worker heat stress

Federal guidance says heavy work carries the highest heat illness risk, and nearly three of four heat illness deaths happen in the first week on the job. It advises new workers to start at 20 percent of normal exposure and add no more than 20 percent per day, and to drink about a cup of water every 15 to 20 minutes. The bottle plant evaluation recommended hourly heat measurements in hot weather, rest breaks in a cool place and annual heat training, and noted that new hires starting in summer had no time to acclimatize. A tech working in these plants follows the plant's heat rules, not just the ones for HVAC jobs. Our heat stress lesson covers warning signs.

Worked example (our own, with assumed inputs)

All inputs here are our own assumptions, except the 0.65 discharge coefficient and the equation form, which come from the university page. We want a first check on how much air a plant bay can move by natural draft.

  • Assumed height between lower and upper openings, H: 30 ft = 9.14 m.
  • Assumed indoor average 100°F = 310.9 K, outdoor 85°F = 302.6 K. Difference: 15°F = 8.33 K.
  • Assumed wall openings: 600 sq ft. Assumed roof ventilator throat: 400 sq ft. Discharge coefficient 0.65 on both.

Step 1, effective areas. Wall: 0.65 × 600 = 390 sq ft. Roof: 0.65 × 400 = 260 sq ft.

Step 2, combine two openings in series (our own, standard orifice-in-series rule): 1 / √(1/390² + 1/260²) = 1 / √(6.574e-6 + 1.479e-5) = 1 / 4.622e-3 = 216 sq ft.

Step 3, driving velocity. √(2 × 9.81 × 9.14 × 8.33 / 310.9) = √(4.806) = 2.19 m/s, which is 7.19 ft/s.

Step 4, flow. 216 × 7.19 = 1,556 cubic ft/s; × 60 = about 93,400 cfm.

Step 5, heat carried out. Using the common sensible heat constant 1.08 (0.075 lb/cu ft × 0.24 Btu/lb-°F × 60 min/hr): 1.08 × 93,400 × 15 = about 1.51 million Btu/h. If the bay heat load is larger than that, indoor temperature rises until the draft grows to match, or the plant needs fans and spot cooling.

Spot cooler at one operator station. Assume a 4 ft by 4 ft zone (16 sq ft) and a target of 300 ft/min of air across it (an assumed target, not a standard): 16 × 300 = 4,800 cfm. If that air arrives at 85°F and leaves the zone at 95°F, it carries away 1.08 × 4,800 × 10 = 51,840 Btu/h, about 4.3 tons at 12,000 Btu/h per ton. This shows the air side only. It does not remove radiation, so shields still matter.

Field card: HVAC in Glass Plants: Hot-End Heat, Spot Cooling and Natural Draft

Block radiant heat, move cool air over the person, and give the building a low inlet.

  • Melting furnace near 2800°F; roughly 30 percent of furnace energy is lost through the structure.
  • Globe reading well above dry bulb means radiant heat.
  • Spot coolers and evaporative units need maintenance before summer.
  • Natural draft needs low openings; exhaust without makeup air stalls it.
  • Control rooms: keep doors closed and the room slightly positive.
  • Heat acclimatization: start at 20 percent, add no more than 20 percent a day.
