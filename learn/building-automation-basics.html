Building Automation Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Part of the Commercial Cooling Guide

Large buildings do not run on a single thermostat. A building automation system, often called a BAS, watches the equipment and adjusts it all day. Knowing the vocabulary helps you read what it is telling you.

The three kinds of parts

  • Sensors report conditions. Analog inputs report a value that varies, such as temperature, humidity or pressure. Digital inputs report on or off, such as a flow switch, a door contact or a status contact.
  • Controllers are small purpose-built computers with inputs and outputs. Some run a whole plant, and others, such as terminal unit controllers for VAV boxes and fan coils, come pre-programmed for one job.
  • Actuators carry out commands. Analog outputs set a position or a speed, such as a valve, a damper or a variable frequency drive. Digital outputs turn a relay or a load on and off.

Setpoints

A setpoint is the target the system tries to hold. The controller compares the sensor reading to the setpoint and moves the output to close the gap. A hot water valve opens or closes to hold a supply temperature. Morning warm-up can start early so the space reaches its setpoint right when people arrive. The same idea appears in Outdoor Reset and Weather Compensation.

Schedules and alarms

The BAS can run equipment on a schedule for occupied and unoccupied hours, and raise an alarm when a reading goes outside limits or a device reports a fault. Alarm logs are a useful record. They show what happened, when, and in what order, which helps you avoid guessing.

Trends

A trend is a recorded history of a point, such as a space temperature or a valve position over days. Trends are one of the most useful tools in a control system, because many problems come and go. A graph that shows a damper hunting back and forth, or a supply temperature that drifts every afternoon, tells you more than a single glance at the screen. Use trends to see cause and effect before you change anything.

How devices talk

Devices share information over a network using a protocol, which is an agreed language. BACnet is an open protocol from ASHRAE widely used in commercial and institutional buildings. LonTalk (LonWorks) is another protocol you may meet; it began as a proprietary Echelon protocol and was later published as a standard. Newer systems also use wireless options. The idea is that equipment from different makers can share points on one front end. See Communicating Controls Basics.

A sensible way to troubleshoot

  • Start with what the screen says, then check that the sensor reading matches reality with a trusted instrument.
  • Look at the trend and the alarm history.
  • Check that the output command and the actual device agree. A command to open a damper is not proof it opened.
  • Check the control power and wiring, which are the basics in Relays, Transformers and 24V Control.
  • Be careful with overrides. A forced output left in place is a common cause of long-running problems, so note and release it.

Safety note. A software command can start a fan, valve or heater without warning. Before working on equipment, lock out and tag out at the disconnect, and do not rely on the software or a point being off. Never bypass a safety device such as a freeze stat or smoke detector through the control system. Follow your site's rules for who may change settings.

The rule that overrides everything on this page: the manufacturer's instructions for the equipment in front of you, and the rules where you work, always govern. This lesson explains concepts. It gives no setpoints, pressures or procedures.
