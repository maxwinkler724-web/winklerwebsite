Gas Combustion Efficiency From Net Stack Temperature and Oxygen: Dry Flue Gas Loss, Moisture Loss, Excess Air and Why It Is Not AFUE

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An analyzer prints an efficiency number a few seconds after you push the probe in the flue. It is a useful number, but it answers a narrow question: how much heat is leaving up the stack right now. This lesson shows how that number is built from net stack temperature and oxygen, how to read it from a published table, and how to use it to judge a cleaning or a tune-up.

Targets come from the maker. Combustion adjustment belongs to a qualified technician following the appliance maker's instructions and local rules. Nothing here sets a target or a CO limit. If a CO alarm is sounding, get outside and call for help.

Net stack temperature, step by step

Net stack temperature is the flue gas temperature minus the temperature of the air going into the burner. The DOE efficiency table we use below labels its columns exactly that way, as flue gas minus combustion air temperature. The subtraction matters because the stack loss is the heat carried out beyond what the air brought in. A boiler in a cold garage and the same boiler in a warm mechanical room will show different flue temperatures at the same net value. Our practice note: read the air temperature where the combustion air enters, and read the flue temperature at the same spot each visit, after the appliance has warmed up. The oil-side lesson on the oil burner test sequence covers waiting for steady state, and the analyzer readings lesson covers what each sensor measures, so we do not repeat either here.

The two losses that make up the estimate

A Canadian government energy agency builds the stack loss for natural gas boilers from two parts and adds them. We use its method as the explanation of what the analyzer is doing.

  • Dry flue gas loss. The dry products of combustion (carbon dioxide, nitrogen and unused oxygen from the extra air) leave hot. The loss grows with the amount of dry gas and with the net stack temperature. More excess air means more dry gas, so it raises this loss directly.
  • Moisture loss from burning hydrogen. Natural gas contains hydrogen, which burns to water. That water leaves as vapor, carrying the energy it took to evaporate. In the Canadian method this term depends on the flue and air temperatures and the fuel, not on the oxygen reading, and the source says it can exceed 10 percent of the fuel's energy depending on flue temperature. A condensing appliance recovers part of it; see the higher vs lower heating value lesson for why.

Combustion efficiency is then 100 percent minus those stack losses, with small terms for carbon monoxide and unburned hydrocarbons that the Canadian method treats as negligible when each is under about 50 ppm. That is why both a hotter stack and too much excess air cut efficiency, while the moisture term mostly rides along with temperature.

Reading the DOE table

The DOE steam tip sheet "Improve Your Boiler's Combustion Efficiency" prints combustion efficiency for natural gas against net stack temperature and excess air. Its stated assumptions are natural gas, complete combustion and no water vapor in the combustion air. Here are the rows we use, copied from the table:

Columns are net stack temperature (flue gas minus combustion air). Values are combustion efficiency in percent.

Two things show in the chart. Every line slopes down, so a hotter stack always costs efficiency. And the lines spread apart as the stack gets hotter: at 200°F net the 3 percent and 10 percent O2 rows are 2.4 points apart (85.2 vs 82.8), and at 600°F they are 7.2 points apart (75.4 vs 68.2). Excess air and stack temperature hurt together, because extra air is more gas to heat and more gas to carry the heat out. The same tip sheet gives a rule of thumb: efficiency rises about 1 percent for each 15 percent cut in excess air, or each 40°F drop in stack temperature, and it says about 10 percent excess air is achievable on well-designed natural gas systems.

The tip sheet does not say which heating value its percentages use. Our own cross-check, using the Canadian agency's formulas (which count the higher heating value) with a 70°F combustion air assumption, lands within about 0.5 point of the table for 2 to 5 percent O2, and farther off at 10 percent O2 (82.3 vs 82.8 at 200°F, 66.4 vs 68.2 at 600°F). Treat the table as a good estimate, not a laboratory value, and do not read it for condensing operation, where flue gas is cooled below its dew point.

Worked example (our own)

This example is our own. The readings are assumed, not measured on a real job.

Assumptions. A natural gas hot-water boiler at steady state, firing at its normal rate. Combustion air is 70°F in both visits. The analyzer is set to natural gas. We read the DOE table directly and only at its printed points, so the readings are chosen to land on them. CO is assumed to stay acceptable to the maker's limit in both visits; we do not give a limit here.

Before the tune-up. Flue gas reads 470°F and O2 reads 5.0 percent.

  1. Net stack temperature = 470 − 70 = 400°F.
  2. Table row for 5.0 percent O2 (28.1 percent excess air), column 400°F: 79.5 percent.

After cleaning the heat exchanger and tuning the air. Flue gas reads 370°F and O2 reads 3.0 percent.

  1. Net stack temperature = 370 − 70 = 300°F.
  2. Table row for 3.0 percent O2 (15.0 percent excess air), column 300°F: 82.8 percent.

Change. 82.8 − 79.5 = 3.3 points. To make the same heat, fuel input scales as one over efficiency, so the new input is 79.5 / 82.8 = 0.960 of the old, about 4.0 percent less gas, assuming the same heat output and the same unit.

Where the gain came from. Using only table values: at 400°F, dropping from 5.0 to 3.0 percent O2 moves 79.5 to 80.4, +0.9. At 5.0 percent O2, dropping from 400°F to 300°F moves 79.5 to 82.1, +2.6. The temperature change is the bigger lever here. The tip sheet's rule of thumb agrees: a 100°F drop is 2.5 points (100 / 40), and the excess air cut from 28.1 to 15.0 percent is 13.1 points (13.1 / 15 is about 0.9), for about 3.4 against the table's 3.3.

Our cross-check of the split. With the Canadian agency's formulas at 70°F air, the before case works out to about 9.2 points of dry flue gas loss plus 11.6 points of moisture loss (79.3 percent), and the after case to about 6.0 plus 11.1 (82.9 percent). That fits the idea above: the moisture loss barely moves because it follows temperature and the fuel, while the dry gas loss is where air and temperature both show up.

Why this is not AFUE

The analyzer figure is a steady-state estimate of stack losses only. AFUE is an annual number. The DOE home heating fact sheet describes it as based on the fuel an appliance uses over a full year, and a DOE home heating and cooling guide describes it as the heat actually delivered to the house compared with the fuel supplied. The federal test procedure for furnaces and boilers (10 CFR 430, Subpart B, Appendix N) defines AFUE through a national test standard, and the appendix includes burner off-period flue measurements and a jacket loss factor. Those are losses a steady-state flue reading cannot see. So we expect an analyzer figure to run higher than the AFUE of the same appliance, and the two should never be compared, quoted as a rating, or used to judge a nameplate. A fair use of the analyzer number is the appliance's own before and after.

Using the trend after cleaning or tuning

  • Log the conditions with the number. Fuel setting, firing rate, warm-up time, probe position, combustion air temperature, net stack temperature, O2 and CO.
  • Compare like with like. Same firing rate and same probe spot, or the change tells you about the test, not the appliance.
  • Falling stack temperature after a cleaning points to cleaner heat transfer surfaces. Rising stack temperature over a season at the same rate and O2 points to fouling, soot or scale slowing heat transfer in the heat exchanger.
  • Lower O2 is a gain only if CO stays in bounds. The DOE tip sheet warns that too little air leaves unburned combustibles, soot, smoke and carbon monoxide. Stop trimming when CO rises or the maker's settings say stop.
  • Remember it is an estimate. Look for steady movement across visits, not a single reading, and treat a small difference as within the noise of the test.

Field card: Gas combustion efficiency from the stack

Net stack temperature and O2 in, a stack-loss estimate out. Not AFUE.

  • Net stack temperature = flue gas temperature minus combustion air temperature.
  • Stack loss = dry flue gas loss + moisture loss from burning hydrogen.
  • More excess air raises dry gas loss; a hotter stack raises both losses.
  • DOE table: 5.0% O2 at 400°F net is 79.5; 3.0% O2 at 300°F net is 82.8.
  • DOE rule of thumb: about 1 point per 15% less excess air or 40°F less stack temperature.
  • The analyzer figure is steady state; AFUE is annual and includes other losses.
  • Compare before and after at the same firing rate; watch CO whenever you trim air.
