ICRA Construction Dust Containment in Occupied Healthcare Buildings

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When a ceiling is opened or a wall is built in a working clinic or hospital wing, the dust does not stay put. The air system can carry it anywhere. This lesson is for the facility tech who has to keep the work zone sealed off from the HVAC and from the people next door: the ICRA classes in general terms, sealed grilles, negative air machines, pressure checks, and putting the system back after.

Check the project plan

Check the project plan. The infection prevention team and the facility's written ICRA permit decide the precautions for each job. This page is general education built from public sources, not a replacement for that permit or for local rules. Follow it, and ask before changing it.

Why the HVAC tech is in the loop

CDC's environmental infection control guidelines say construction and repair work markedly increases airborne Aspergillus spore counts indoors, which matters most for patients with weak immune systems. CDC calls for an infection-control risk assessment (ICRA) before the work starts, and it says the barrier type must fit the scope of the project.

ICRA classes in plain terms

The ICRA matrix published by the American Society for Health Care Engineering (ASHE) sorts every job two ways. First, the construction type: A is inspection and non-invasive work, B is small, short, low-dust work, C is moderate to high dust or work that cannot finish in one shift, and D is major demolition or construction. Second, the patient risk group of the affected area: low, medium, high or highest, where the highest group includes operating rooms, intensive care and isolation rooms. If more than one group is affected, the higher one applies. The matrix then gives a precaution class from I to IV. In that document, Class I is minimizing dust, Class II adds active dust control, sealed vents and door mats, Class III adds complete barriers and HEPA negative air, and Class IV adds sealed penetrations and an anteroom with shoe covers and coveralls. Infection control approval is required for Class III and IV.

The ASHE matrix gives no pressure number and no air change rate. It only says the work area must be negative to its surroundings and kept so with HEPA-equipped units.

Seal the air path first

  • Return and supply in the zone. CDC says to block and seal return air vents when rigid barriers are used. The ASHE Class II list says to block and seal air vents or isolate the system. Sealing a grille means plastic and tape on the face, or a blank-off at the duct, not just a loose sheet.
  • Check the system after the barrier goes up. CDC says to confirm the air-handling system in the affected area works properly after barriers are erected and before negative pressure is set. Sealed grilles shift airflow elsewhere, so look for starved zones.
  • Recirculated air and intakes. CDC asks the team to find out whether the facility can run temporarily on recirculated air, and to seal nearby outside air intakes if so.
  • Roughing filters. CDC says to check roughing filter banks often and replace as needed, and to bag dust-filled filters as soon as they come out. See one-inch filters vs four-inch media cabinets for the pressure drop side.

Negative air machines

CDC says to create and maintain negative air pressure in work zones next to patient areas, and to monitor negative airflow inside rigid barriers. For above-ceiling work it describes a plastic enclosure with a negative pressure system, using an industrial-grade portable HEPA unit that moves air at 300 to 800 cubic feet per minute, or exhausting directly outside. Per the OSHA silica rule, a HEPA filter is one rated at least 99.97 percent efficient on 0.3 micrometer particles. Where the unit exhausts matters: put its discharge outdoors or to a space the plan approves, away from any air intake. CDC says exhaust outlets should be more than 25 feet from air intakes, and outdoor intakes at least 6 feet above ground or 3 feet above a roof.

The idea is simple. The machine pulls air out of the zone faster than the building lets air in. Replacement air then has to come in through the door, anteroom and small gaps, so air always moves from the occupied side toward the dust. Our own caution: a unit that only recirculates room air cleans it but does not make the zone negative. Only exhausted air does.

Checking that the zone is negative

CDC lists no numeric differential for construction work zones. For airborne infection isolation rooms, CDC's guideline lists a differential greater than 2.5 Pa negative, and the room pressure lesson linked below covers how those gauges work. For this page we use the same 2.5 Pa as a labeled planning assumption for a work zone, not a CDC construction requirement; confirm the project's target with the infection prevention team. By our own conversion that is about 0.01 in. w.c. (2.5 divided by 249.09). That is tiny, so use a manometer with fine resolution. Run the tube through the barrier and log the reading at the start of each shift.

A smoke pencil or tissue strip at the door gap is a quick extra check: the smoke should be pulled into the zone. CDC also says particle counts can help show whether barriers are working. If the reading falls to zero or goes positive, stop dust-making work and look for an open door, a failed seal, a loaded filter or a tripped machine.

Worked example

Assumptions (our own): work zone 20 ft by 30 ft with a 9 ft ceiling; a target of 6 air changes per hour (ACH), chosen for this example and not taken from CDC or ASHE; a machine rated 1,000 cfm; and loaded filters that give about 70 percent of the rated flow in real use.

  1. Volume: 20 x 30 x 9 = 5,400 cubic feet.
  2. Required flow at 6 ACH: 5,400 x 6 = 32,400 cubic feet per hour, divided by 60 = 540 cfm.
  3. Real flow of one machine: 1,000 x 0.70 = 700 cfm.
  4. Machines needed: 540 / 700 = 0.77, so one machine. Actual ACH with one machine: 700 x 60 / 5,400 = about 7.8.

If the plan asks for 12 ACH instead (the figure CDC lists for isolation rooms, used here only as a comparison), the need is 5,400 x 12 / 60 = 1,080 cfm. That is 1,080 / 700 = 1.54, so two machines, which would give 1,400 cfm real. The exhaust has to be made up by air leaking in through the door and anteroom, so the pressure reading, not the cfm math, tells you if it works.

Anteroom, mats and daily habits

For Class IV work, ASHE calls for an anteroom and either vacuuming workers in it or coveralls and shoe covers changed on every exit. CDC lists tacky mats just inside the entrance, wet-wiping tools and carts before they leave the zone, covering and securing debris before it moves, daily cleaning of the zone and its entrance, and designated routes for crews where practical. For anything that makes silica dust, such as cutting or drilling concrete, masonry or tile, the OSHA construction silica standard (29 CFR 1926.1153) sets a permissible exposure limit of 50 micrograms per cubic meter as an 8-hour average, and prohibits dry sweeping where it could add to exposure. It calls for wet methods or HEPA vacuums for listed tasks.

After the work: cleaning, filters and balance

CDC says to clean the zone, and in its list puts barrier curtains up before rigid barriers come down. ASHE says to keep barriers until infection prevention and safety inspect, then clean, HEPA vacuum and wet mop before restoring HVAC. For the system, CDC says to restore the air changes, humidity and pressure differentials, to clean or replace filters, and to commission the HVAC in renovated space before occupancy. In practice: pull the grille seals, replace roughing filters, inspect coils and duct openings for dust, and check airflow against design. The test and balance report lesson shows how to read that comparison. If dust got into the ductwork, see commercial duct and air handler cleaning.

Field card: ICRA Construction Dust Containment

Seal it, exhaust it, measure it, restore it.

  • Get the ICRA permit and the class before you touch a grille or a damper.
  • Seal returns and supplies in the zone; recheck the air system after the barrier goes up.
  • HEPA unit must exhaust outdoors or to an approved space; recirculating alone does not make it negative.
  • Confirm negative with a manometer or smoke at the start of every shift; log it.
  • Our example: 5,400 cu ft, 6 ACH = 540 cfm; one machine at 700 cfm real flow.
  • Tacky mats, wet-wiped tools, covered debris, daily cleaning.
  • After: clean, replace filters, restore pressure and air changes, verify airflow.
