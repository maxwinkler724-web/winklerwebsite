Window Air Conditioners in Winter: Remove, Cover and Seal

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A window air conditioner is built to sit in a window for a few warm months. Left there all winter, it becomes a hole in the wall with a fan in it. This lesson covers the two good choices (take it out, or seal it in), why you should not run it in cold weather, and what to do with the water, the position and the cord before next summer.

Why a unit left in the window leaks

A federal energy renovation guide points out that window units often lack insulation and air sealing around them because they are usually temporary installations. The unit itself is mostly thin sheet metal with vents that open to the outdoors. A federal installation guide for window air conditioners says the same thing from the other side: if the unit is left in, air leaks through the unit itself, so it should be removed or sealed up completely on the inside once the cooling season is over. The same guide notes that air leaking in around a unit often carries humidity into the home. So a unit left in place can bring in drafts and moisture, even in a season when you are not using it.

Cold air also does not leak only through one spot. It finds the gap between the sash and the cabinet, the gap at the folding side panels, the seam where the sash meets the glass and the vents in the unit body. Inside, that shows up as a cold floor near the window, a chilly draft and a heating system that runs longer. For other leaks in the same room, see Drafty Doors and Windows.

Best choice: take it out and store it upright

If you can lift it safely, removing the unit is the cleanest fix. Window units are heavy and awkward, so get a second person and do not lean out of an upper-floor window with it. Once it is out, close the window fully and check that the sash seals. Our own shop advice is to store the unit upright, in the same position it sat in the window, rather than on its back or side. We say upright because the compressor sits in the base and a unit tipped over for months can move oil where it should not be; check the owner's manual for the maker's storage position. A dry closet, basement shelf or garage shelf off the floor works, with the original box or a breathable cover over it. Avoid a sealed plastic bag that traps damp air.

Drain the base pan before storage

Cooling units collect water from the indoor coil, and some can sit in the base pan. Before storing, drain the pan the way the owner's manual shows (many units have a drain plug) and wipe it dry. Standing water can freeze and crack parts, and it can smell musty by spring. Our own rule of thumb: if the unit sloshes when you move it, find the drain before you carry it. This is general practice; follow the maker's instructions for your model.

If it must stay: cover and seal inside and out

Some units are too heavy, the window is too high or the building rules say the unit stays. Then treat the unit as part of the wall. The federal installation guide says to seal it up completely on the inside, and it describes cutting rigid foam panels to fill the spaces beside the unit once the folding side panels are removed. It also says to tape the foam and the top and bottom of the unit, and that exterior-grade tape can cover the outside face of the foam. Our own summary of that approach:

  • Outside: fit an insulated, weather-resistant cover over the cabinet, held with straps or exterior-grade tape. Keep rain from pooling on top.
  • Inside: fit a snug insulated cover or a rigid foam panel over the front grille area, taped at the edges.
  • Side panels and sash: seal the accordion panels and the gap at the sash with foam or backer rod, and seal where the sash meets the glass. The federal guide recommends closed-cell backer rod over the thin foam strip that comes with many units, because that strip tends to leak.
  • Plan to undo it: use tapes that release cleanly, and label what you remove.

A cover on the outside only will not stop leaks through the unit, and a cover on the inside only leaves the outdoor side exposed to wind. Doing both is what our own advice, and the federal guide's call for sealing "completely," points to.

Do not run it in cold weather

A window air conditioner is made for cooling. Running it when it is cold outside is not what the machine is for, and the owner's manual lists the outdoor temperature range it is made to run in. Our own plain reason: with cold outdoor air, the indoor coil can get cold enough to ice over, and a covered unit can trap ice or restrict air. If you want heat, use a heating system built for it. Do not use the air conditioner as a heater unless your manual says that model has a heating mode, and never run a unit that is covered or sealed in.

Worked example (our own): how big is the gap?

This is our own example, with assumed numbers, not a measurement from a source. Assume the unit leaves a gap about 1/8 inch wide, and the gaps added together run about 6 feet in total (sash, side panels and top).

  1. Convert length to inches: 6 ft × 12 in/ft = 72 in.
  2. Convert width to a decimal: 1/8 in = 0.125 in.
  3. Open area = length × width = 72 in × 0.125 in = 9 square inches.
  4. Check the math: 72 ÷ 8 = 9, the same answer, since multiplying by 1/8 is dividing by 8. A hole 3 inches by 3 inches is also 9 square inches.

So a small-looking crack adds up to the open area of a 3 by 3 inch hole. We are not claiming how much air flows through it, since that depends on wind and temperature difference. The point is that thin gaps are still holes. Sealing them is cheap and quick, and it is one reason the sash and side-panel sealing step is worth doing carefully.

Check the cord and plug before summer

When you pull the unit out of storage, look at the power cord before you plug it in. A federal consumer safety page on household cords says to look for visible signs of excessive wear or damage to the plug, sockets and insulation, to inspect the cord along its entire length and to replace damaged cords. That page also warns that overheating often comes from connecting appliances that use more watts than a cord can handle. Our own checklist: no cracked or cut insulation, no bent or loose prongs, no scorch marks, and a plug that fits snugly in the outlet. If anything looks wrong, do not use the unit until it is repaired or replaced. For cords, plugs and upkeep, see the related lesson below.

Why sealed-in units can still let in drafts and moisture

A sealed unit is better than an open one, but it is not as good as a closed wall. Foam shrinks, tape lets go in the cold and wind finds new gaps, which is why the federal guide says air sealing around the window and the unit is critical. Check the seal in the first cold snap and again midwinter. If you feel air at the edges or see damp spots on the sill, the seal has failed or air is moving through the cabinet, and that is a sign to remove the unit if you can.

Field card: Window Air Conditioners in Winter

Out, drained, upright, or sealed inside and out.

  • A unit left in the window leaks air through the cabinet and around it.
  • Best: remove it, close the window fully and store it upright and dry.
  • Drain the base pan before storage; follow the owner's manual.
  • If it stays: insulated cover outside and inside, side panels and sash sealed.
  • Do not run it in cold weather; check the manual's temperature range.
  • Example: 1/8 in × 72 in = 9 sq in of open gap.
  • Before summer, check the cord and plug along their full length.
