Tempered Water for Emergency Eyewash and Safety Showers

Last updated: 2026-10-16

When a worker gets acid or caustic in the eyes, the next 15 minutes decide the outcome, and the water has to be comfortable enough that the person stays under it. That makes the plumbing behind an eyewash or safety shower a real mechanical-room job: mixing valves, heaters, freeze protection and a weekly flush. This lesson walks through what the rules say and what a facility tech checks.

What OSHA requires and what it leaves to a consensus standard

The federal rule is 29 CFR 1910.151(c). OSHA's page for it says that where the eyes or body of any person may be exposed to injurious corrosive materials, suitable facilities for quick drenching or flushing of the eyes and body must be provided within the work area for immediate emergency use. The rule says nothing about water temperature, gallons or inspection schedules.

The details come from a consensus standard, Z358.1, which OSHA did not adopt. In a 2002 interpretation letter OSHA says it has often referred employers to that standard as a source of guidance, that it has no flow-rate requirements of its own, and that it is the employer's responsibility to assess conditions such as water temperature. So a plant that follows Z358.1 is following the best available yardstick, and the plant's own safety staff decide how to apply it. Always check what your site's safety program specifies.

The numbers the standard is quoted for

A university environmental health and safety guideline that restates the standard gives these figures. Tepid water is 60 to 100 F (16 to 38 C). Equipment must deliver tepid fluid for a full 15 minutes. Plumbed eyewash units deliver at least 0.4 gpm at 30 psi, eye/face washes 3.0 gpm at 30 psi, and showers at least 20 gpm at 30 psi. It also says thermostatic mixing valves should be installed so the water stays tepid, and that outdoor units need freeze protection. The same guideline says an eyewash should be reachable within 10 seconds.

OSHA's own interpretation of the old outdoor-installation question adds two points, though that letter is marked archived and may no longer reflect current policy. It says the equipment must not harm the eyes or body through water temperature, and that a frozen shower cannot deliver a usable flow. A line that overheats in a hot mechanical room or a sunny pipe rack is a hazard as well, which is why the whole range matters, not only the cold end.

Why a mixing valve, and what happens when it fails

Cold supply in many plants runs well below 60 F in winter, and a hot loop runs far above 100 F. Neither is acceptable straight from the pipe. A thermostatic mixing valve blends them to a set outlet temperature and corrects for changes in supply temperature and pressure. See Mixing Valves and Injection Mixing Basics for how the blending works.

Failure mode is the design question. If the hot supply is lost, a valve built for this service closes the hot side and lets cold through, so flushing continues rather than stopping. The sketch below shows that idea as a cold-water bypass. How a particular valve behaves, and whether it is allowed to pass cold water at all, depends on its design and listing, so read its installation manual and your site's procedure. We list this as an item to confirm rather than a rule.

Heating the water: tankless, storage, and remote stations

A station near the boiler room can be fed from the building's hot-water system through a mixing valve. A remote station, on a far corner of the plant or a loading dock, may have no hot line at all. Two options are common. An electric or gas tankless heater makes tepid water on demand but must respond to a sudden 20 gpm draw. A storage heater holds a volume that is already tempered and delivers it without needing a big burner. Plumbed units also rely on the heater and valve staying in service, so they are on the weekly list below. Central hot-water plants are covered in Central Domestic Hot Water Plants.

Worked example (our own numbers)

Assumptions, not from a standard or a site: a safety shower flowing 20 gpm for 15 minutes (the shower figure and duration above), a 45 F cold supply, and an 85 F tepid target, which sits inside the 60 to 100 F range.

  1. Volume: 20 gpm x 15 min = 300 gallons.
  2. Temperature rise: 85 F - 45 F = 40 F.
  3. Heater output: Btu/h = 500 x gpm x dT = 500 x 20 x 40 = 400,000 Btu/h. The 500 is the rounded factor for water (about 8.33 lb per gallon x 60 min x 1 Btu per lb per F).
  4. Fuel input is larger than the output because no heater is 100 percent efficient. At an assumed 80 percent efficiency, input is 400,000 / 0.80 = 500,000 Btu/h.

That is a very large burner or a lot of electric elements for something that runs a few minutes a year. Now compare storage. Heating the 300 gallons takes about 300 x 8.33 x 40 = 99,960, call it 100,000 Btu, of heat. A small heater with 25,000 Btu/h of output can supply that in about 4 hours. With storage, a modest heater keeps a tank at temperature, the tank covers the 15-minute event, and the heater recovers afterward. This is why many sites use a storage tank, and why a tankless heater sized for the full instantaneous load is often impractical. Real design adds heat loss from the tank, a recirculation or stagnation plan, and sizing by the plant's engineer.

Freeze protection for outdoor units

An outdoor shower or eyewash in a cold climate needs freeze protection, as the university guideline and OSHA's interpretation both say. Typical approaches are freeze-resistant equipment, electric heat tracing with insulation, or a drain-down design. Heat tracing is part of the mechanical system: check the controller, the sensor and the tag that shows its last test as part of the rounds.

Legionella and stagnant water

The CDC says stagnant water in a plumbing system can raise the risk of Legionella growth, that stagnant hot water can cool into the growth range of 77 to 113 F (25 to 42 C), and that disinfectant levels can fall. Compare that with the tepid range: 60 to 100 F overlaps the growth range. A branch that feeds a rarely used eyewash is exactly the kind of dead leg that sits in that zone. Regular flushing replaces the standing water. The CDC also says to minimize splashing and aerosol generation while flushing. For the wider picture, see Water Heater Temperature Setting: Scald Risk vs Legionella.

Weekly activation and annual inspection

The university guideline calls for weekly activation of plumbed eyewash and eye/face wash units and annual inspection of all equipment, with records showing location, date and who did it. It lists showers on a monthly schedule, while another university page calls for weekly checks of both eyewashes and showers. OSHA's infosheet defers to the manufacturer's directions on how often and how long to activate plumbed systems. In practice a site follows the strictest of these that its safety program adopts.

What facility techs check

  • The unit flows when the valve is opened, quickly and without a tool.
  • The water comes out clear after the stagnant slug clears, and is not hot or cold on the skin.
  • The mixing valve outlet temperature with a thermometer, recorded against the site's target.
  • Heater and tank status, relief valve, and the thermostat setting.
  • Heat tracing and insulation on outdoor units, and drains that are not frozen.
  • Access: nothing blocking the path, and signage and caps in place.
  • The tag or log, with date and initials. A unit that fails gets tagged out of service and reported.

For personal protection on the job, see Eye and Face Protection for HVAC Techs. For small tankless units and their flow limits, see Tankless Gas Water Heater Problems.

Field card: Tempered water for eyewash and safety showers

Tepid, 15 minutes, and it has to work when needed.

  • 29 CFR 1910.151(c) requires quick drenching or flushing facilities where corrosives are present.
  • Z358.1 guidance: tepid is 60 to 100 F, 15-minute flush, shower 20 gpm, eyewash 0.4 gpm.
  • Thermostatic mixing valves blend hot and cold; confirm failure behavior in the manual.
  • Our example: 20 gpm for 15 min = 300 gal; 45 F to 85 F needs 400,000 Btu/h output.
  • Storage tanks cover the event with a small heater.
  • Outdoor units need freeze protection.
  • Stagnant lines can sit in Legionella growth range (77 to 113 F per the CDC); flush on schedule.
  • Activate weekly (or as your program says), inspect annually, keep records.
