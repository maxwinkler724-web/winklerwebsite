Line-Voltage Thermostats for Electric Baseboard Heat: Single-Pole vs Double-Pole, Amp Ratings, Smart Models and Safe Replacement

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A thermostat that runs a furnace handles about as much power as a doorbell. A thermostat on an electric baseboard heater can be switching thousands of watts of full house voltage. Same wall plate shape, completely different part. This lesson explains how line-voltage thermostats work, how to read their ratings and how to swap one safely.

Line voltage vs 24 volts

A low-voltage thermostat only sends a small control signal, normally 24 volts from a transformer, to a relay or gas valve that does the real switching. A line-voltage thermostat is the switch itself. The U.S. Department of Energy says that with these thermostats the thermostat directly controls the power supplied to the heating device. That is why the wiring looks different: thick conductors joined with wire connectors instead of thin wires on terminals.

That is also why you never put a 24-volt thermostat on a baseboard circuit. Its contacts are built for a tiny current. On 120 or 240 volts they can arc, weld shut or burn, and the case and wiring are not made for that voltage. The reverse is wrong too: a line-voltage unit cannot be used as a furnace thermostat. If the cable in the wall has two or four heavy conductors and a breaker feeds it, treat it as line voltage until a meter says otherwise. For the 24-volt side, see Electrical Basics for HVAC.

120 volts or 240 volts

Baseboard and wall heaters come in both. Small rooms are often on 120 volts; larger heaters and long runs are usually 240 volts, because the same watts need half the current. The heater nameplate and the breaker tell you which you have. A 240-volt circuit uses two hot conductors, called L1 and L2, from a double-pole breaker. A 120-volt circuit uses one hot conductor and a neutral. Match the thermostat to the circuit: many models are rated for both voltages, but some are not, so read the label.

Single-pole vs double-pole

A pole is one switched path. A single-pole thermostat opens only one conductor. A double-pole thermostat opens both L1 and L2 at once.

  • On 120 volts, a single-pole unit breaks the hot conductor. That is the usual arrangement.
  • On 240 volts, a single-pole unit breaks only one leg. The heater element can then stay connected to the other live leg even though the heater is not running. Anyone who reaches into the heater thinking it is off can still be shocked.
  • Double-pole models break both legs. When the knob or key is set to Off, the heater is cut off from both hot conductors. This is the true off position.

Even with a true off position, the thermostat is not your lockout point. Its Off setting is a convenience for the occupant. For work on the wiring, the breaker is the energy isolating device, as shown below. Article 424 of the National Electrical Code covers fixed electric space-heating equipment, and local amendments and the inspector decide what is required at your site, so check them before a job.

Wall-mount vs built-in thermostats

A wall thermostat sits on an interior wall and switches one or more heaters. A built-in thermostat is part of the heater, usually in the end cap. The Department of Energy notes that built-in units often do not sense the room temperature accurately, and that a remote thermostat on an interior wall is preferred. Colorado State University Extension also points to remote line-voltage thermostats as one of the most accurate ways to control electric systems. A built-in dial is still a fine choice for a small, single-heater room; it just cannot see the whole room.

Amp and watt ratings

Every line-voltage thermostat is marked with a maximum load, in amps, watts or both, at a stated voltage. The rating is a ceiling for what the contacts can switch for years without overheating. The connected heater load must stay under it, and a margin is smart because heaters run for hours at a time. Power in watts is volts times amps, so the same load draws half the current at 240 volts as at 120. The U.S. Department of Energy teaches the energy side as kilowatt-hours equal to kilowatts times hours, with cost equal to the rate times the energy.

Mechanical vs electronic and smart models

  • Mechanical bimetal. A coil of two metals bends with temperature and tips a switch. It needs no power of its own and has only a coarse temperature swing, but few things go wrong.
  • Electronic and programmable. A sensor and a small circuit hold temperature closer and allow schedules. The circuit needs a little power to run, and what it switches is still line voltage.
  • Smart or Wi-Fi. Radios and screens need steadier power. Some models need a neutral conductor at the wall box; many older baseboard circuits have none in the box, so check the maker's wiring diagram before you buy. Our own caution: a model that does not need a neutral may pass a small current through the heater when idle, so still treat the heater as live until the breaker is off. For a low-voltage cousin of this problem, see how smart thermostats get power.

Safe replacement steps

OSHA's electrical safety-related work rule, 29 CFR 1910.333, says live parts must be deenergized before an employee works on or near them, the circuits are to be locked out or tagged, and a qualified person must test with equipment to verify they are dead, including checking for any energized condition. OSHA's lockout/tagout standard, 1910.147, defines an energy isolating device as a mechanical device that physically prevents the transmission of energy, with circuit breakers and disconnect switches as examples. It also says push buttons and selector switches are not isolating devices, which includes a thermostat Off setting. The lockout/tagout standard excludes electrical hazards covered by Subpart S, so check which rule applies to your workplace. The practice is the same for anyone.

  1. Turn the correct breaker off. On a 240-volt heater, that is a double-pole breaker. Put a lock or tag on it.
  2. Take the cover off the thermostat and test with a meter that you checked on a known live source. Test both legs: L1 to L2, each leg to ground, and each leg to neutral if present. You want zero everywhere.
  3. Photograph or label the wires before you disconnect them: line (from the breaker side) and load (to the heater).
  4. Match the new unit to the voltage, the pole count and the load rating. Match the wire gauge and connectors to the maker's instructions.
  5. Reassemble, remove your lock, power up and confirm the heater runs and shuts off.

If the breaker trips on restart, the heater still has a fault or the thermostat is wrong; see Electric Baseboard Heater Not Working: Thermostat, Limit and Breaker for the troubleshooting side.

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

These numbers are our own assumptions for practice, not from any product. One room-pair is on a single 240-volt circuit and one thermostat.

  • Assumed heaters: two at 1,500 W and one at 1,000 W.
  • Assumed thermostat rating: 22 A at 240 V (the label on the unit you buy is what counts).
  • Assumed use: the heaters run at full output the equivalent of 6 hours a day for 30 days, at 18 cents per kWh.

Step 1, total watts: 1,500 + 1,500 + 1,000 = 4,000 W.

Step 2, amps at 240 V: 4,000 ÷ 240 = 16.7 A. At 120 V the same load would draw 4,000 ÷ 120 = 33.3 A, which is why bigger loads go on 240.

Step 3, rating check: 16.7 A is below 22 A, so the load fits under the thermostat ceiling.

Step 4, the 80 percent continuous idea: Heaters can run for three hours or more at a stretch, so a common rule of thumb is to keep a continuous load to about 80 percent of the rating. 22 × 0.8 = 17.6 A. 16.7 A is under 17.6 A, so it passes, but only narrowly. One more 500-watt heater (2.1 A more) would push it to 18.8 A and fail; you would use a higher-rated thermostat or a relay and a second thermostat.

The same rule of thumb applies to the circuit: 16.7 A is over 80 percent of a 20-amp breaker (16 A), so a 20-amp breaker would be too small for this load. An electrician sizes the breaker and wire against the code, so treat that as a flag, not a design.

Step 5, monthly energy and cost: 4 kW × 6 h × 30 days = 720 kWh. 720 × $0.18 = $129.60 per month under these assumptions. Change the hours or rate and the answer scales in a straight line.

Why it matters

Most of the trouble on these jobs comes from three mistakes: a 24-volt part on line voltage, a single-pole switch that leaves a 240-volt heater half live, and a rating that was never added up. Do the arithmetic, kill the breaker, test both legs.

Field card: Line-Voltage Thermostats

Line voltage is the switch itself. Never put 24 V parts on it.

  • Line-voltage thermostats switch the heater power directly; 24 V thermostats only send a signal.
  • 240 V heaters: double-pole gives a true off by opening L1 and L2.
  • Watts = volts × amps. Rating must exceed the heater load, with margin (about 80 percent for continuous load).
  • Smart models may need a neutral; read the wiring diagram first.
  • Breaker off, lock or tag it, test both legs, then touch the wires.
