Crawl Space Ducts and HVAC: Vented vs Sealed Crawl Spaces

Last updated: 2026-10-16

If you searched "ducts in crawl space sweating," the short answer is that humid air in the crawl space is touching a duct that is colder than that air's dew point. This lesson explains why crawl spaces are a hard place for ducts and equipment, how vented and sealed (closed, conditioned) crawl spaces differ, and how to check a duct with real numbers.

Why a crawl space is hard on ducts

A crawl space is a ground-contact space under the floor. Soil gives off moisture, and in summer the air that enters is often warm and wet. Advanced Energy, a North Carolina energy research group, says sweating ducts there are more often the result of high humidity from the ground or from wall vents than of a fault in the cooling system, and that bare or thinly insulated ducts make it worse. The U.S. Department of Energy's Building America program explains the old idea behind foundation vents: outdoor air was supposed to flush out moist air. In humid climates, though, the outdoor air is often wetter than the crawl space air, which invites rot, mold and pests.

A cold supply duct is the coldest surface in that space. If you want the basic physics of condensation first, read Sweating Ducts and Vents. This page is about what is different underneath the house.

Duct leaks pull crawl air into the house

Ducts in a crawl space leak, and the question is how much. A Washington State University Extension Energy Program article (Nordeen, 2008) says to put ducts inside the conditioned house when you can, and otherwise to seal them with mastic and test them. The Building America crawlspace guide adds a useful point: return-side leaks matter more than supply-side leaks. A supply leak dumps conditioned air into the crawl space, which actually helps condition it. A return leak pulls crawl space air, with its moisture and dust, into the system and then into the house. The same guide says return leaks also raise the risk of backdrafting for atmospherically vented fuel-burning equipment. For sealing materials and leak locations, see Sealing Duct Leaks.

The WSU article reports one tracer-gas finding worth remembering: in a group of typical vented crawl space homes, about 40 percent of the winter air in the house came from the crawl space. The study was small and regional, so treat it as an illustration, not a national figure.

Vented vs sealed crawl spaces

Vented: foundation openings let outdoor air in and out. Sealed (also called closed, unvented or conditioned): the vents are closed off and the space is treated more like a small basement. The DOE says Building America research helped put language into the 2009 and 2012 International Residential Code (section R408.3) that allows unvented, conditioned crawl spaces, and reports that tested homes used 15 to 18 percent less heating and cooling energy and saw humidity drop by more than 20 percent. The Building America guide warns that simply blocking vents without the rest of the work can make things worse, and can be dangerous where naturally vented combustion equipment is present. Local codes differ, so the inspector in your town has the final word.

Sealed is not magic. A 2013 DOE-sponsored study of two cold-climate sealed crawl space homes found humidity above 60 percent from April through November, and the authors suggested the code needs tighter conditions for how these spaces are dried. WSU's article found much higher radon in sealed crawl spaces than in vented ones (12 to 16 pCi/L against under 2) and says radon mitigation is a must. Test for radon if you close a crawl space.

The parts of a sealed crawl space

  • Ground vapor retarder. The Building America guide recommends a puncture-resistant polyethylene sheet over the whole floor (at least 10-mil, or cross-laminated 6-mil), lapped and taped at the seams and run up the walls and piers. The IRC wording it quotes requires a continuous retarder with 6 inch sealed overlaps and edges attached to the stem wall at least 6 inches up. Advanced Energy's minimum for a plain retarder is 6-mil over 100 percent of the floor.
  • Wall insulation, not floor insulation. In a sealed space the boundary moves to the walls, so insulation goes on the walls and band joist. The guide quotes 2009 IECC values of R-10 continuous or R-13 cavity in climate zones 4 to 8. Check your own zone in your local energy code. Use unfaced or facing-removed foam so walls can dry, and check termite rules locally. Floor insulation is the vented-space approach, and WSU's extension paper discusses it alongside the sealed approach.
  • A drying source. The guide states that crawl spaces "must always have a drying mechanism." See the next section.
  • Closed vents and sealed penetrations, with a check that no equipment needs makeup air from those vents.

Supply air or a dehumidifier

The Building America guide prefers semi-conditioning by supply air: at least 1 cfm for each 50 ft² of floor area, or 50 cfm total, whichever is greater, with a passive return path back to the house. It says this meets the IRC supply-air route and that local rules may differ. Where air conditioning is not typical, a permanent dehumidifier draining to a proper point can hold the space at 30 to 50 percent relative humidity. For a 1,000 ft² crawl space the supply rule gives 1,000 ÷ 50 = 20 cfm, but 20 is less than 50, so the answer is 50 cfm. For 3,000 ft²: 3,000 ÷ 50 = 60 cfm, which is more than 50, so 60 cfm. A tech sets this with a damper and measured airflow, not by guessing at a register.

Worked example: will this duct sweat?

Assumptions for the example: crawl space air at 78°F and 75 percent relative humidity on a humid day, and a bare metal supply trunk with a surface of 58°F (an assumption, not a rule). The dew point method is from Lawrence (2005, Eq. 8 and the constants he recommends): convert to Celsius, then compute γ = ln(RH/100) + 17.625·t / (243.04 + t), and dew point = 243.04·γ / (17.625 − γ).

  1. Air temperature: (78 − 32) ÷ 1.8 = 25.56°C.
  2. 17.625 × 25.56 ÷ (243.04 + 25.56) = 1.677.
  3. ln(0.75) = −0.288, so γ = 1.677 − 0.288 = 1.389.
  4. Dew point = 243.04 × 1.389 ÷ (17.625 − 1.389) = 20.8°C, which is 69.4°F.
  5. Compare: 58 − 69.4 = −11.4°F. The duct surface is 11.4°F below the dew point, so water forms on it.

Lawrence's quick rule gives a close check: the dew point drops about 1°C for every 5 percent below 100 percent RH, so 25.56 − 25 ÷ 5 = 20.56°C, about 69.0°F. Now suppose a vapor retarder and a dehumidifier bring the same 78°F air down to 60 percent. The same steps give a dew point of 17.2°C, or 63.0°F. The bare duct at 58°F still sweats, by 5.0°F. Drying the space cuts the problem in half but does not remove it. Sealed seams plus insulation with an intact outer jacket keep the outside surface near room temperature, which is what finally stops the sweat.

Insulation and sealing of the ducts themselves

Sweat on a duct is a duct-surface problem and a humidity problem together. Seal the seams first so humid air does not enter the duct and cold air does not leak out; then add insulation with the vapor retarder side out and the jacket seams taped. The Building America guide says to strap flexible duct at 3 foot intervals so it does not sag and pinch. For mold on ducts or coils, see Mold on the AC Coil or in Ducts. If you are also adding insulation in the attic, Attic Insulation and Air Sealing covers how that changes equipment load.

Equipment in a crawl space

Furnaces, air handlers and water heaters in a crawl space need two checks before anyone closes vents: where their combustion air comes from, and where their condensate goes. The Building America guide says to confirm that mechanical systems do not need makeup air from the foundation vents, and it covers condensate drains for furnaces and air conditioners. Gas or oil equipment in particular is a job for a licensed tech.

Inspection tips for a homeowner

  • Look with a flashlight in dry weather and again on a humid day. Water beads on duct jackets, wet insulation or dark staining on the floor deck all point to a moisture source.
  • Check for a floor vapor retarder that covers all the soil, with torn or missing patches.
  • Look for disconnected or crushed ducts, split seams and sagging flex duct.
  • Wood moisture content over 17 percent deserves investigation, per the Building America guide.
  • Never close vents, or crawl in with standing water, loose wiring or suspect mold, without a pro. The Advanced Energy article says exposed live wiring in crawl spaces should be in a junction box and may need an electrician.

If the whole house feels sticky, see House Feels Humid with the AC Running; for a basement companion, Basement Dehumidifier Sizing.

Field card: Crawl Space Ducts and Sealed Crawl Spaces

Humid air plus a cold duct equals water.

  • Dew point (Lawrence 2005): 78°F at 75% RH is about 69.4°F; a 58°F bare duct sweats.
  • Return leaks pull crawl air into the house; seal returns first.
  • Sealed crawl: closed vents, ground vapor retarder, wall insulation, a drying source.
  • Supply air rule in the Building America guide: 1 cfm per 50 ft² or 50 cfm, whichever is greater.
  • Dehumidifier target in that guide: 30 to 50% RH.
  • Test for radon. Check combustion air before closing vents.
