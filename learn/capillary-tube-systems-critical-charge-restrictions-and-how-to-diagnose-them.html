Capillary Tube Systems: Critical Charge, Restrictions and How to Diagnose Them

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A capillary tube has no moving parts and no adjustment. That makes the small sealed systems that use it cheap and quiet, but it also means the amount of refrigerant inside matters a great deal. This lesson explains why, and how to tell a plugged tube from a system that is simply short on charge.

What a capillary tube does

A capillary tube is a long, thin length of tubing that serves as the metering device. It creates the pressure drop between the high side and the low side, as covered in Metering Devices: TXV and Piston. Embraco, a compressor maker, says these tubes have internal diameters of roughly 0.5 to 2.5 mm. Nothing in the tube changes with load. The only things that set how much refrigerant it passes are its length, its bore and the pressures and liquid condition at its inlet. Because the tube is so narrow, a small kink, bit of debris or drop of water can change its flow a lot. For the basic loop around it, see Refrigeration Cycle Basics.

ACHR News describes capillary tube charging as a practice for small, fractional-horsepower refrigeration systems. Those are the sealed systems you meet in appliances. For another packaged unit with a sealed refrigerant circuit, see PTAC Units: How They Work, Cleaning and Common Problems, and check the service data for the unit in front of you to see how its metering device is built.

Why the charge is so exact

Because a fixed tube cannot adjust, these small systems are often called critically charged. That phrase is common shop language, so treat it as a rule of thumb rather than a defined standard. The practical point is documented, though. ACHR News says weighing in the charge is the more exact method and is preferred by most manufacturers, using the amount printed on the equipment data plate. The article's steps are to remove the old refrigerant, dehydrate the system, then add the charge with an electronic scale or a dial-a-charge. It also warns that adding or changing parts, such as a larger liquid-line filter-drier, may call for a charge adjustment, so check with the equipment or part maker.

That is the same principle as in Charging by Weight and Line Set Length Adjustments, with one big difference. A split-system adjustment is a few ounces on a charge of many pounds. An appliance charge may be only a few ounces in total.

Worked example: how small is small?

Our own arithmetic. A nameplate lists a charge of 4.5 oz of R-134a. A technician weighs in 120 g.

  1. Convert the nameplate: 4.5 oz × 28.35 g/oz = 127.575 g, about 127.6 g.
  2. Find the shortfall: 127.575 g − 120 g = 7.575 g, about 7.6 g.
  3. Express it as a percent of the nameplate: 7.575 ÷ 127.575 = 0.0594, or about 5.9 percent low.
  4. Put a 10 g error in the same terms: 10 ÷ 127.575 = 0.0784, about 7.8 percent of the charge.

A 10 g mistake is larger than the whole 7.6 g shortfall above, and it is nearly 8 percent of the charge. For comparison, 10 g on a 5 lb (2,268 g) system is only 10 ÷ 2,268 = 0.44 percent. Hose fill, a few seconds of a loose connection or a scale that is not zeroed can easily cause 10 g. That is why you zero the scale with the hoses attached and weigh carefully on these jobs.

Restriction or low charge?

The two look alike. ACHR News says a restricted capillary tube can resemble low charge, with both suction and discharge pressures running lower than normal. Embraco says a partial blockage lowers cooling and raises energy use, which also resembles a refrigerant shortage, while a complete blockage stops cooling.

The ACHR article offers a useful clue: when you add refrigerant, the low-side pressure barely moves while the high-side pressure climbs well above normal. That points to a restriction, not a leak. Its recommended test is to recover the charge, weigh in the exact nameplate amount, and see what happens. If the symptoms vanish, the system was low and a leak search is justified. If they persist with the correct charge, suspect the capillary tube.

Moisture and debris

Embraco lists three causes of obstruction: moisture, solid residue and a tube bent too tightly. For a suspected total moisture blockage it says to look for ice at the evaporator inlet and warm that spot. If refrigerant starts to flow again, moisture is the likely cause. The fix it describes is to remove the moisture from the circuit, clear the tube and recharge. It also says a metal-mesh filter-drier helps keep debris out of the system. If you change the drier, recheck the charge, since ACHR News notes a different drier can change the correct amount.

A heat exchanger you can mislead yourself with

ACHR News notes that the capillary tube is often soldered to the suction line, where it acts as a heat exchanger. When charging by superheat, which the article reserves for cases where the original charge is unknown or the system is only slightly short, measure at the evaporator outlet before that junction. The article gives 8 to 12 degrees F as the target and says to allow up to 10 minutes after each addition for the system to settle. It also describes a frost-line method for medium-temperature cases, adding slowly until frost reaches the suction line leaving the case. It calls that method not very accurate. Weighing is the better check.

Federal rules for small appliances

All of this work is covered by EPA Section 608. In 40 CFR 82.152 (read on eCFR) a small appliance is one fully manufactured, charged and hermetically sealed in a factory with 5 lb or less of refrigerant. The same section defines a process stub as a length of tubing that gives access to the refrigerant inside a small appliance or room air conditioner. Section 82.154 requires small appliances to have one to help with refrigerant removal at servicing and disposal. For recovery equipment made on or after November 15, 1993, section 82.156(b) calls for recovering 90 percent of the refrigerant when the appliance compressor works, or 80 percent when it does not, or evacuating to 4 inches of mercury vacuum. Equipment older than that has an 80 percent figure. Section 82.155 applies those levels to disposal. These rules change, so confirm the current text. Field card: Capillary Tube Systems

Weigh it in. Small charges forgive nothing.

  • Fixed tube: length and bore set the flow; it cannot adjust.
  • Prefer charging by weight to the data plate amount, after recovery and dehydration.
  • Restriction and low charge both give low pressures.
  • Adding charge: flat low side with a high side climbing well above normal points to a restriction.
  • Moisture, debris or a bent tube can block the tube.
  • Superheat check: 8 to 12 degrees F, measured before the tube/suction line junction.
  • Small appliance: 5 lb or less, sealed; recovery 90 or 80 percent, or 4 in. Hg.
