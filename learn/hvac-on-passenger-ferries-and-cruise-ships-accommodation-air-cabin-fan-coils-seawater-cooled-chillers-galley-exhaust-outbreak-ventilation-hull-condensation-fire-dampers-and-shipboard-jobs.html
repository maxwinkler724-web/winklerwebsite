HVAC on Ferries and Cruise Ships: Cabins, Chilled Water and Shipboard Work

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A passenger ship is a floating hotel, hospital-grade galley and small city utility plant inside a steel box. Most of the HVAC ideas are the same ones you already know from buildings. What changes is the space, the seawater, the fire rules and the schedule. This lesson is for techs who wonder what marine service involves.

How to use this page

This is general education from public sources, not vessel-specific instruction. Every ship has its own approved drawings, class and flag rules, and company procedures. Those always win over a lesson. For small boats and engine rooms we already have separate lessons: see seawater-cooled marine air conditioners on boats and ship and workboat engine room ventilation. This page covers the passenger side.

Accommodation and lounge air handling

Spaces where people sleep, eat and gather are served by air handlers in fan rooms, with ducts running through decks and bulkheads to lounges, corridors and cabins. A maritime university's engineering manual for a training ship describes this layout: each fan room has a cooling coil just ahead of its fan, and each space gets fresh air mixed with return air in the ductwork.

A federal public health agency's construction standards for cruise ships list requirements for the air supply side by heading: accessible equipment, complete drainage, air intakes, makeup air, diffusers, condensate collection pans, and engine room and mechanical spaces. The same standards list exhaust headings: independent systems, negative air pressure, sufficient exhaust, access panels and a written balancing report. We read the headings, not the full requirement text, so use them as a map of what inspectors care about, then read the current edition for details.

Chilled-water fan coils in cabins

Many passenger vessels cool cabins with a closed chilled-water loop. The training-ship manual describes a secondary-refrigerant plant: chillers cool a water-glycol solution that pumps out to the spaces, and the cooling valve opens only when both a thermostat and a humidity sensor call for cooling. A cabin unit is basically what you know from our fan coil lesson: coil, fan, filter, drain pan and a control valve. Loop temperatures and flow work the way they do ashore; see chilled-water pumps and delta T.

Seawater-cooled chillers and condensers

The same manual says seawater flows through the chiller condenser tubes and is discharged overboard, supplied by dedicated circulation pumps. Seawater is a gift of cold, unlimited heat sink and a constant fouling and corrosion problem. On a large ship, expect the condenser to be one item on a planned maintenance list that also includes pumps, strainers and tube cleaning.

Galley and laundry exhaust

The federal cruise ship construction standards have a hood section whose headings include direct duct exhaust, preventing condensate, filters, access and cleaning cabinets. That tells you galley hoods are inspected as sanitation equipment as well as fire and comfort equipment. For the basics of makeup air and hood balance, read our commercial kitchen ventilation lesson.

Outdoor air and recirculation during illness outbreaks

Federal cruise ship standards define an acute gastroenteritis outbreak as illness in 3 percent or more of passengers or crew, and they have sections on isolating ill people and their immediate contacts. Federal public health guidance for maritime vessels, written during the COVID-19 response, gives the air handling side: open outdoor air dampers beyond minimum settings to reduce or eliminate recirculation, rebalance to increase total airflow to occupied spaces, turn off demand-controlled ventilation that reduces supply while spaces are occupied, and raise filtration as high as possible without significantly reducing design airflow. It also mentions portable high-efficiency filter units for higher-risk areas such as the medical center. Techs should treat those as options the ship's officers and medical staff decide on, not changes to make on their own, because more outdoor air means more cooling load and more load on the chillers.

Humidity and condensation in steel hulls

Steel is a good conductor, so a cold chilled-water pipe, a duct or an uninsulated piece of hull can sit below the dew point of humid cabin air and sweat. The standards' condensate pan, "drain completely" and hood "prevent condensate" headings point at the same problem from the sanitation side: standing water is a health and mold risk. The training-ship control scheme, which requires a humidity signal before the cooling valve opens, shows how humidity control is built into the logic. Field habits that follow: check drain pans and drains, check insulation and vapor jackets for tears, and treat a wet ceiling tile or damp carpet near a pipe as a fault to trace, not a stain to ignore.

Fire dampers and smoke control zones

A federal maritime safety guide to structural fire protection (a circular supplementing the rules) defines an automatic fire damper as a duct closure that closes by a heat-actuated mechanism and can also be operated manually, installed where ducts pass through fire barriers to limit fire spread. It notes dampers are normally held open and close when a fusible link melts or an electric or pneumatic signal activates the actuator. The federal construction rules for passenger vessels we read also require means to close vents and ventilators, and to stop fans serving machinery and cargo spaces from outside the space. In practice that means a tech must know where the damper is, how it resets, and who must be told before a fan or damper is touched. Never wedge or bypass one. The ship's fire plan shows the divisions; follow it.

Noise near cabins

Cabins sit close to fan rooms, trunks and machinery, and guests notice noise at night. We did not find a federal page giving cabin noise limits, so we give no numbers. A fan coil that suddenly gets louder usually has a dirty filter, a failing motor bearing or a loose panel.

Worked example: one small cabin (our own)

Our own example, with assumed inputs. We estimate the cooling load of one small passenger cabin and the chilled-water flow for its fan coil. Every input below is our assumption, picked for the exercise, not a design value for any ship.

  • Two people, assumed 250 Btu/h sensible and 200 Btu/h latent each.
  • Lights and small electrics: 120 W, all on.
  • One round porthole, 12 in. across, with an assumed 150 Btu/h per square foot of glass for sun.
  • One exterior steel wall, 60 sq ft, insulated, assumed U = 0.10 Btu/h per sq ft per F, with an assumed sun-heated temperature difference of 35 F.
  • Other cabin surfaces next to conditioned spaces: assumed zero. Central air handler supply is assumed already treated, so no extra outdoor air load is counted here.

Step 1, people. Sensible: 2 x 250 = 500 Btu/h. Latent: 2 x 200 = 400 Btu/h.

Step 2, lights. 120 W x 3.412 Btu/h per W = 409 Btu/h.

Step 3, porthole. Area = 3.1416 x (0.5 ft)2 = 0.785 sq ft. Then 0.785 x 150 = 118 Btu/h.

Step 4, wall. 60 x 0.10 x 35 = 210 Btu/h.

Step 5, total. Sensible = 500 + 409 + 118 + 210 = 1,237 Btu/h. Add latent 400. Total = 1,637 Btu/h, about 0.14 ton (1,637 / 12,000 = 0.136).

Step 6, water flow. For water, gpm = Btu/h / (500 x rise in F). The 500 is 8.33 lb per gallon x 60 min per hour x 1 Btu per lb per F, which is 499.8. With a 10 F rise: 1,637 / (500 x 10) = 1,637 / 5,000 = 0.33 gpm.

Check. Work backward: 0.327 gpm x 500 x 10 = 1,635 Btu/h, which matches. Sanity check against a rule of thumb: at a 10 F rise, one ton takes 12,000 / 5,000 = 2.4 gpm, and 0.136 ton x 2.4 = 0.33 gpm. Both agree. A water-glycol mix needs somewhat more flow; our delta T lesson is the place to start.

What a shipboard HVAC job looks like

The federal labor statistics page on water transportation workers says ship engineers operate and maintain propulsion systems, boilers, generators and pumps, keep an engineering log and parts inventory, and on large vessels assistant engineers oversee machinery when the chief is off duty. It reports about 8,800 ship engineer jobs in 2025 and says crews on deep-sea ships and barges may spend weeks or months away from home, working on board 7 days a week. It also says ferry operators may work only in summer, and that most jobs need a federal transportation worker credential and a federal mariner credential, with hearing and vision tests. That page does not list an HVAC-only position, so we cannot say how a given cruise or ferry operator staffs refrigeration and air conditioning work. Ask employers directly what rotation, watch and credential rules apply. The federal page on marine engineers and naval architects covers the design side: about 8,400 jobs in 2025.

Field card: HVAC on Ferries and Cruise Ships: Cabins, Chilled Water and Shipboard Work

Same coils and fans, tighter spaces, saltwater, stricter fire and sanitation rules.

  • Cabins: chilled-water fan coils; check filter, drain pan, valve and insulation.
  • Chiller condensers use seawater: expect strainers, pumps and tube cleaning.
  • Water flow: gpm = Btu/h / (500 x rise in F). Our example: 1,637 Btu/h at 10 F is 0.33 gpm.
  • Outbreak response (open outdoor air, more airflow, better filtration) is decided with the ship's officers and medical staff.
  • Fire dampers: know where they are and how they reset. Never wedge or bypass.
  • Ask employers about rotation, watch duties and credentials.
