Evaporator Design TD in Walk-In Coolers: Box Humidity, Product Moisture Loss, Coil Sizing and the Suction Pressure Check

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Every walk-in has a number that nobody sets on a dial but that decides how wet or dry the box feels: the evaporator TD, the gap between the box air and the refrigerant boiling inside the coil. This lesson shows how that gap sets humidity, why leafy produce wilts when it is too big, how coil size and suction pressure move it, and how to check it with a thermometer and a gauge.

What TD means

Design TD is the box air temperature minus the saturated suction temperature, which is the boiling temperature of the refrigerant at the pressure in the coil. One refrigeration maker's engineering manual (2008) describes the same idea: the TD chosen between the evaporator's saturated suction temperature and the room air controls the drying effect, and the TD selected approximates the desired relative humidity. A coil colder than the air's dew point pulls water out of the air as condensate or frost. A colder coil pulls more. The box has to be dried a little to be cooled, and the TD decides how much.

Watch the definition. A university extension handout uses the air temperature drop across the coil, from inlet to outlet, and says the coil's own TD is roughly twice that. When you compare published numbers, check which one the author means.

Small TD, high humidity

The University of Wisconsin extension handout on cold storage has a table of minimum relative humidity by airstream temperature drop, calculated from psychrometric tables. Doubling the airstream drop to get coil TD, as the handout says, and reading its 35 F storeroom column, we get these approximate values: TD 2 F, 96 percent; TD 4 F, 92 percent; TD 6 F, 89 percent; TD 8 F, 85 percent; TD 10 F, 81 percent; TD 20 F, 64 percent; TD 30 F, 51 percent. These are minimums, a floor under ideal conditions. Wet product, door openings and defrost water add moisture, so real boxes usually run somewhat higher.

A 2012 trade-press article on evaporator TD puts the walk-in design near 10 F, giving about 85 percent RH, and a compact reach-in near 20 F, giving about 65 percent. It says that when RH is too low, excessive dehydration occurs, and that reach-ins often lack room for the large coil a 10 F TD needs. Notice that the two sources agree in direction and roughly in size (about 80 to 85 percent at 10 F) but not in exact numbers. Treat any single figure as approximate.

Look at the chart against the band. Reaching 90 to 95 percent takes a TD of only a few degrees, which means a very large coil. That is why humidity-sensitive rooms need much bigger coils than a plain storage box.

What dry air does to product

Produce loses water to air with a high vapor pressure deficit. A University of California extension slide set says water loss is affected mainly by packaging, temperature, humidity and airflow, and that the deficit rises linearly as humidity falls. It lists 3 to 5 percent weight loss as affecting visual quality and more than 5 percent as causing shrivel and lost salability, with romaine marketable until 5 percent and broccoli going soft at about 3 to 4 percent. A chapter from a University of Florida postharvest reading set gives roughly 3 to 10 percent as the loss at which most commodities become unsalable, notes grapes can lose appeal at 1 to 2 percent, and reports a bell pepper test at 8 C in which package humidities of about 99 to 100, 96 to 98, 92 to 95 and 87 to 90 percent gave weight losses of roughly 0.7, 2, 4 and 6 percent. We did not capture how long that test ran, so use it for the trend, not as a schedule.

Some rooms are asked for a TD ceiling. The same maker manual says banana ripening rooms use evaporators selected at 15 F TD or less with the coil held at no less than 40 F. An old Ohio experiment station paper on apple storage advises evaporators of not over 10 F TD, or at the peak cooling load not over 20 F and preferably 15 F, falling below 10 F as the load drops. How coil size and suction pressure move TD

The coil moves heat at a rate that grows with the TD. A 2020 trade-press article says the relationship is close to linear between about 5 F and 20 F: its example is a coil rated 11,400 Btu/h at 10 F TD delivering about 22,800 Btu/h at 20 F. It adds that makers often supply small correction factors, so use the maker's data for real selections.

  • Bigger coil, same load: the coil needs less TD to move the same heat, so the refrigerant boils warmer and the box stays wetter.
  • Smaller or dirty or frosted coil: less surface or less airflow means the TD has to grow to carry the load, and the box dries. See our defrost and icing lesson.
  • Suction pressure setting: a lower suction pressure means a lower saturated temperature, so a bigger TD for the same box temperature. Lower suction pressure is the quick way to get capacity, and it costs humidity.

Worked example (ours)

Assumptions are ours: box air 36 F, a coil rated 10,000 Btu/h at 10 F TD (an assumed number), capacity proportional to TD, and the 35 F column of the handout table as the humidity guide.

  1. Case A: saturated suction 26 F. TD = 36 − 26 = 10 F. Capacity = 10,000 Btu/h. Humidity floor about 81 percent.
  2. Case B: saturated suction 20 F. TD = 36 − 20 = 16 F. Capacity = 10,000 × 16 ÷ 10 = 16,000 Btu/h, which is 1.6 times as much. Humidity floor: the table gives 81 percent at 10 F and 64 percent at 20 F, so a straight-line reading at 16 F is 80.9 − (6 ÷ 10) × (80.9 − 64.1) = about 71 percent.
  3. Sizing the other way: to hold 10,000 Btu/h at a 16 F TD you only need a coil rated 10,000 × 10 ÷ 16 = 6,250 Btu/h at 10 F TD, which is 62.5 percent of the size. That smaller coil is cheaper, and it costs about 10 points of humidity in this example. For leafy produce that trade is a poor one.

The straight-line reading and the proportional capacity are simplifications. Real coils differ with air temperature, frost and fan speed.

The field check

  1. Measure box air temperature in the return air to the coil, with the box at steady state and not just after a defrost or a door-open event.
  2. Read suction pressure with a gauge as close to the evaporator outlet as you can. A gauge at the condensing unit reads lower than the coil does, because of pressure drop in the line, which makes TD look larger than it is.
  3. Convert the pressure to saturated temperature on the pressure-temperature chart for the refrigerant on the nameplate. Follow the chart's directions for blends.
  4. TD = box air temperature − saturated suction temperature. Compare it with the design TD in the maker's data for that coil.
  5. If TD is high, look first at airflow, frost, fan operation and the suction setting. Measure superheat to make sure the coil is properly fed; see our superheat lesson. For the basics of the box see our walk-in basics lesson.

Field card: Evaporator Design TD

Small TD, wet box. Big TD, dry box.

  • TD = box air temperature minus saturated suction temperature.
  • About 10 F TD gives roughly 80 to 85 percent RH; 20 F gives roughly 65 percent (two published sources).
  • Produce is usually held at 90 to 95 percent RH; more than 5 percent weight loss ruins many items.
  • Capacity is close to linear in TD between about 5 and 20 F.
  • Lower suction pressure raises TD and dries the box. A bigger coil lowers TD.
  • Gauge near the coil, convert on the chart for the nameplate refrigerant, then subtract.
