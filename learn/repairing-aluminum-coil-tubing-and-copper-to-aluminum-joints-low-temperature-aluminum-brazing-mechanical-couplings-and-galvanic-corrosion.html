Repairing Aluminum Coil Tubing and Copper-to-Aluminum Joints

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A pinhole in an aluminum coil is not a copper leak with a different metal. The metal melts close to the temperature of the filler you would use, a stubborn oxide skin blocks wetting, and the copper-to-aluminum joint corrodes by itself. This lesson covers the repair options and the metallurgy behind them.

Why aluminum coils are everywhere now

A trade-press article on condensing coils (published in 2002, so its market comments may be dated) quotes coil builders saying some larger residential makers moved to aluminum tube and fins because of cost and weight, and that a copper coil uses about three times as much copper. It also says leaks showing up where aluminum meets copper began appearing in the mid-1980s. Newer designs reduce the problem, but the article says leaks can still occur. For the corrosion side of coil leaks, see Formicary Corrosion and Salt Air. This page is about fixing the tube and the joint.

Why aluminum is hard to braze

Two facts do most of the damage. First, the base metal and the filler are close in temperature. A university lecture sheet lists pure aluminum at 660 C. An aluminum industry training lecture lists alloy melting ranges of 643 to 654 C for 3003, 593 to 652 C for 6061 and 616 to 652 C for 6063, with common silicon fillers (4045 and 4047 types) beginning to melt near 577 C. A patent that reviews the field gives the 6061 solidus as 582 C and says the lowest-melting standard silicon filler becomes fully liquid at 582 C. In plain words, a standard filler can reach full flow at about the same temperature at which 6061 starts to soften.

Second, aluminum grows an oxide skin the moment it meets air. The same lecture says aluminum oxide melts at 2038 C and is neither molten nor reduced at aluminum melting temperatures, and that it re-forms quickly after cleaning, so the gap between cleaning and brazing should be short. Flux does four jobs at once, per that lecture: it displaces the oxide, protects the clean metal from air, lowers the surface tension of the filler and helps the filler wet the base metal. You cannot see the tube heading toward collapse, because aluminum shows little color change as it heats.

Low-temperature fillers: flux or fluxless

Repair fillers get around the narrow window by melting lower. The patent text we read describes a zinc and aluminum filler with a liquidus of about 499 to 521 C and a solidus of 471 to 493 C, and says zinc and magnesium help rupture the oxide film on the filler while it heats. A filler maker's repair article describes a flux-cored zinc-aluminum rod: scrub the area with a stainless steel brush, degrease oily surfaces, heat the tube with the flame tip, touch the rod to the metal, pull the heat back as the filler flows and use the rod itself as a temperature indicator. For a copper-to-aluminum joint it says to heat the copper, which carries heat into the aluminum. It warns against overheating, which can melt the aluminum.

  • Flux-cored or flux-coated rods carry the flux in the rod. Follow the filler maker's instructions for cleaning the joint after it cools.
  • Fluxless rods rely on the scrubbing action and the filler chemistry. Cleaning matters even more, because nothing else removes the oxide.

Check the filler's data sheet for its own flow range and for the base alloys it suits. Do not borrow numbers from a different rod.

Mechanical repair couplings

When brazing is not an option, a maker's installation instruction for aluminum coil repair kits shows a mechanical coupling. In that sheet the kit is for aluminum coils only (using it on copper can cause leaks), and the steps are: find the leak, recover the refrigerant, strip the fin stock to expose 1 to 1.5 inches of tube, clean the adhesive with fine emery cloth (rough emery may cause leaks), cut the tube square, deburr it and keep it round. After assembly, the nut is tightened snugly by hand and then a further 1-1/4 turns with a wrench, with a backup wrench on the fitting body so the tube does not twist. To replace a bad transition, the sheet says to cut near the weld, remove the joint and assemble the coupling between the aluminum tube and a short copper stub, sloped with the copper end down. See also Press-Fit Copper Fittings for what makers allow with press tools, since those rules differ by metal and product.

Galvanic corrosion and why makers use transition joints

A federal technical bulletin explains that galvanic corrosion needs two dissimilar metals that are electrically connected and an electrolyte such as water, condensation or salts; remove any one and it stops. It names copper and aluminum as a pairing to keep apart in wet conditions, and it says a small anode coupled to a large cathode raises the corrosion current density. Aluminum is the less noble metal in this pair, so it is the one that is eaten; that is general metallurgy, not a quote from that bulletin. A factory transition joint puts the dissimilar metals together in a controlled weld away from wet fittings, so field joints are copper to copper or aluminum to aluminum.

The repair sheet adds the field protection: insulate the coupling and tubing one inch on each side, and make sure the seal around the tube and coupling is watertight, to keep water off the dissimilar joint.

When a repair is not worth it

  • Repeat leaks. A second leak in the same coil usually means the tube wall is thinning everywhere.
  • Dirty or old coils. The trade article quotes a coil builder who said that after five years units are often so clogged with dirt that repair does not make sense.
  • Warranty. A leak inside the coverage period may be a maker replacement. Read the warranty and the maker's rules before cutting the coil; we did not find a maker statement on whether a field repair voids coverage, so confirm it.
  • Labor and refrigerant. Recovery, repair, testing and recharge add up fast, as the example below shows.

Testing after the repair

The maker's checkout steps are: add a small charge, recheck for leaks, evacuate, recharge and check performance. Use a nitrogen pressure test first so you are not leak-checking with refrigerant; see Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods and Leaks and Leak Repair Basics for the procedure, and the equipment label or maker's instructions for the test pressure. For brazing practice on copper, see Brazing Copper Refrigerant Lines.

Worked example (ours): temperature margin

We compare published values in degrees C. A tech heating the tube must keep the metal between the filler's liquidus and the base alloy's solidus.

  1. Low-temperature filler liquidus: 499 to 521 C (patent text).
  2. Lowest base solidus we found for common coil alloys: 6061 at 582 C (patent). The lecture table lists 593 C, so 582 is the conservative figure.
  3. Margin = 582 minus 521 = 61 C at the worst case; 582 minus 499 = 83 C at the best. That is about 110 to 149 F (61 and 83 times 1.8).
  4. Standard silicon filler comparison: 4047-type liquidus 582 C against the same 582 C solidus gives 0 C margin; the lecture lists 4045 liquidus at 591 C, so against a 582 C solidus it is 9 C over. Against the 593 C figure, margins would be 2 C (4045) and 11 C (4047).
  5. For 3003 (solidus 643 C), the low-temperature filler margin is 643 minus 521 = 122 C up to 643 minus 499 = 144 C.

The takeaway: roughly 60 to 80 C of room is workable with a torch, and a margin of a few degrees is not, since the metal does not glow to warn you.

That beats $1,720 only when p is below 1,165 divided by 1,720, which is about 0.68. Past that point replacement is cheaper, and a coil with repeated leaks usually sits well above it.

Field card: aluminum coil joint repair

Narrow window, oxide skin, keep water off the dissimilar joint.

  • Aluminum and the filler melt close together; use a low-temperature filler and heat the copper side.
  • Clean right before brazing; the oxide re-forms fast.
  • Do not heat the factory weld; keep the flame off the transition.
  • Mechanical coupling kits are for aluminum coils only; follow the tightening steps.
  • Insulate and seal repaired joints so water stays out.
  • Pressure test with nitrogen, evacuate, then recharge.
  • Repeat leaks or an old, dirty coil: price replacement.
