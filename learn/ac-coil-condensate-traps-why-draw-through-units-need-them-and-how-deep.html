AC Coil Condensate Traps: Why Draw-Through Units Need Them and How Deep

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A trap is a few inches of pipe that looks like a plumbing detail. On an air handler whose blower sits after the coil, it decides whether the drain pan empties or fills. This lesson explains the pressure behind that, how makers size trap depth, and what to do about vents, cleanouts, slope and a dry trap.

Check your manual and local code

Check the manual for the unit in front of you. Makers state trap sizes differently, and code sections are renumbered and amended by state and town. Code points below come from a study summary and a trade article, not the code books, so confirm the edition your inspector enforces. This lesson builds on the drain leak lesson and assumes you know what static pressure is.

Why a draw-through coil needs a trap

In a draw-through arrangement the blower sits downstream and pulls air through the coil. That puts the coil casing and the drain pan below room pressure. The pan is connected to a drain that opens to room pressure, so with no water seal the unit simply draws air up the drain. Two things go wrong. The air flowing in can stop water from leaving, and a trade engineer writing in ACHR News adds that without a trap, pan pressure below atmospheric can keep the unit from draining at all, with sewer vapors a second concern where the line ties to a sewer. One coil maker, Marlo Coil, says air drawn through a drain with no trap can spray water toward the fan inlet.

A trap fixes this by holding a column of water in the line. The negative pressure in the pan lifts the water on the pan side of the trap by some height, and that is why the numbers are in inches of water column, the same unit as static pressure. If the water column is shorter than the suction, air gets through and the pan backs up. A trade-training article puts it plainly: a trap that is too shallow lets air come back in and the condensate back up.

A blow-through unit is the opposite. The blower sits upstream of the coil and pushes air through, so the pan is above room pressure and air tries to leave through the drain. It still wants a trap, but a shallow one is enough. Marlo's rule for it is below. Many furnace-mounted coils are in this position, which is why the trade-training article calls the problem unlikely there.

How makers state trap depth

  • A fixed minimum. Trane's installer's guide for its 3 1/2 to 4 ton 2/4TGB3F42A and 48A air handlers says the primary drain must be trapped with a minimum 2 inch water seal, within 4 feet of the drain outlet, and that those units are approved for draw-through use only. It notes the blower makes negative pressure at the drain but gives no formula. It also says not to use preformed 3/4 inch PVC running traps.
  • A fixed trap height. A Rheem indoor air handler instruction sheet (form 7081940) says to install a 5 inch trap as close to the coil as possible, with only one trap in the line, and to fill it with water before operation. That sheet does not tie the number to static pressure.
  • A pressure-based rule. Marlo Coil's trapping sheet gives formulas. For draw-through (negative pressure): H = maximum negative unit static pressure in inches of water plus 1 inch, and D = H/2. For blow-through (positive pressure): D at least 1/2 inch, and H = maximum total unit static pressure plus 1/2 inch. Use the sheet's own figures to see how H and D are drawn, because makers label the dimensions differently. Marlo says to use worst-case pressure, such as a dirty filter or a faster fan speed.
  • A shop rule of thumb. A trade-training tech tip says trap depth in inches is about double the static pressure in inches of water column: 0.5 inch gives about 1 inch, and 0.7 inch gives at least 1.4 inches. It says residential jobs rarely need more than 2 inches, and tells readers to follow the maker over any rule of thumb.

These do not agree with each other, and they do not have to. They size different products. When the manual gives a number, use it. When it only says "trap the drain," the pressure-based rules above are a reasonable way to check a field build, not a replacement for the maker.

Worked example: size a trap from the pressure

This is a constructed teaching case that applies Marlo's draw-through rule. Say a draw-through air handler reads 0.5 inch w.c. negative at the pan with a clean filter. The filter will load up, and you decide the worst case to plan for is 0.8 inch w.c.

  1. Worst-case negative pressure: 0.8 inch w.c.
  2. H = 0.8 + 1 = 1.8 inches.
  3. D = H / 2 = 1.8 / 2 = 0.9 inch.
  4. Compare with the shop rule: 2 x 0.8 = 1.6 inches. The 1.8 inch result is taller, so using it covers both.
  5. Check the unit's own manual. If it says "minimum 2 inch seal," as Trane's does for its units, 2 inches wins over the 1.8 inches you calculated.

Result: build for at least 1.8 inches, and use 2 inches or the maker's number if it is larger. For contrast, a blow-through unit at 0.5 inch w.c. total static pressure gets H = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.0 inch under Marlo's rule. Too deep is also a fault: Marlo warns that an overly deep trap lets water back up into the system, and Daikin Applied's fan-coil manual warns a too tall trap causes back pressure and condensate backup. Do not just add depth.

Vent, cleanout and slope

  • Vent after the trap. A trade article says vents go after the trap and stay uncapped so air can escape, and that the vent should be taller than the drain pan so a clog backs water into the pan to trip a float switch rather than spilling from the vent. A study summary of the plumbing and mechanical codes likewise says the vent tee belongs downstream of the trap, because an upstream vent lets negative pressure draw air in and defeats the seal. Neither is code text, and the trade article notes some drains have no vent at all.
  • Cleanout before the trap. The same trade article puts cleanouts on the pan side of the trap, accessible and capped when not in use, and suggests pouring about a gallon of water through to prime and test the line. Trane recommends a cleanout tee or cross in the primary drain.
  • Slope. Trane says at least 1/4 inch per foot. Daikin Applied's fan-coil manual says at least 1/8 inch per foot, with local code governing. The trade article says the IMC minimum is 1/8 inch per foot and that 1/4 inch is best practice. A 10 foot run at 1/4 inch per foot drops 10 x 0.25 = 2.5 inches. Older Rheem instructions say 1 inch per 10 feet, so read the manual for your unit and age.
  • One trap, no reducers. Rheem says only one trap in the line. Trane says no reducing fittings. The Marlo sheet adds that a shared trap lets an idle fan coil pull or push air through the others, so give each unit its own.

Priming a dry trap, clogs and algae

A trap only works with water in it. Daikin Applied says it may be necessary to fill the trap by hand at startup, or to run the unit long enough to build a seal. Marlo says evaporation can dry traps out in dry climates and idle seasons, and suggests priming for those cases and antifreeze in the trap, or a drained and plugged trap, in winter. Priming also matters after a cleaning: a trap left empty is the same as no trap. The trap holds standing water, which is where slime grows. For the causes and the safe checks, see the leak lesson. For what protects the ceiling when it clogs anyway, see secondary pans and float switches, and if the water goes up to a pump rather than down by gravity, see the pump lesson.

Field card: Coil Condensate Traps

Draw-through pan is under suction. The water seal has to beat it.

  • Blower after the coil (draw-through) pulls air up the drain, so a trap is required.
  • The manual's number comes first. Trane: 2 inch seal minimum on its units. Rheem: 5 inch trap on its older sheet.
  • Marlo draw-through rule: H = worst-case negative pressure + 1 inch; D = H/2.
  • Worked case: 0.8 inch w.c. gives H = 1.8 inches, D = 0.9 inch.
  • Use worst-case pressure (dirty filter, high fan speed), not the clean reading.
  • Vent after the trap; cleanout before it; one trap per unit.
  • Slope per the manual (1/4 inch per foot is common; 1/8 is a stated minimum).
  • Prime it at startup and after cleaning.
