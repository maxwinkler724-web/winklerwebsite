Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing Boilers

A boiler is a heat exchanger with fire on one side and water on the other. The temperature of the water coming back to it decides whether the combustion gases stay as gas or turn to liquid. That one fact explains a lot of boiler design.

Where the water comes from

Hot water leaves a boiler, travels through radiators or other heat emitters, gives up some heat, and comes back. The cooler water returning is called return water. In a house with big radiators on a cold day the return is fairly warm. After a long idle period, or with a lot of cool water mixing in, the return can be much cooler.

Why burning fuel makes water

Many common fuels, including natural gas, contain hydrogen. When hydrogen burns it forms water vapor, and that vapor leaves with the other exhaust gases. As long as the vapor stays hot, it floats away up the vent. If it touches a surface cooler than its condensation point, it turns to liquid on that surface. That liquid mixes with other combustion gases and can be acidic.

Conventional boilers: keep the surfaces warm

Many conventional boilers, especially older cast-iron and steel ones, are not built to live with that acidic liquid. If the return water is too cool, the boiler's metal surfaces stay cool, flue gas condenses on them and corrosion can follow. Burnham's manual for its V11H boilers says return water cannot be lower than 135 degrees Fahrenheit for prolonged periods, that return water should not be introduced if it is more than 40 degrees colder than the idle boiler (a 150 degree idle boiler should not get return water below 110), and that the temperature drop across the system should be 40 degrees or less. Burnham's HP600 bulletin also gives 135 degrees minimum return and a 40 degree maximum drop through the boiler. Those are numbers for those Burnham models. A hydronics trade article by John Siegenthaler mentions about 130 degrees as a commonly suggested guideline, which is why the maker's higher figure wins here. The real number for a particular boiler is in that boiler's manual, and some boilers have special requirements.

When a system could deliver cool return water, designers add protection. Common ideas include mixing some hot supply water into the return, or controls that hold the return warm until the boiler is up to temperature. The goal is the same: keep the boiler's metal above the point where flue gas condenses on it for any length of time.

Condensing boilers: make condensation work for you

A condensing boiler takes the opposite approach. It is built from materials that tolerate the acidic liquid and has a design that lets the flue gas give up more of its heat, including the heat released when the vapor turns to liquid. That recovered heat is why these boilers are marketed as more efficient. They also have a drain for the condensate, and many need the condensate neutralized before it goes to a drain, depending on local rules and the manufacturer's directions.

A condensing boiler does its best when the return water is cool enough for the flue gas to condense. This is where system design matters. Large emitters, low supply temperatures or controls that lower the water temperature as the weather warms all help keep returns cool. A system built for very hot water at all times can leave a condensing boiler running as an ordinary boiler, without much of the efficiency gain it was bought for.

Two boilers, two rules

  • Conventional boiler: keep returns warm enough to avoid sustained flue-gas condensation.
  • Condensing boiler: keep returns cool enough that condensing happens, and handle the condensate properly.

This is a big reason you should never swap one boiler type for another without a design review. Piping, controls, venting materials and condensate handling all change.

Signs to watch for

On a conventional boiler, moisture or rust streaks around the flue connection, or persistent dampness in the firebox area, can be a hint that condensation is happening. On a condensing boiler, a blocked condensate drain can shut the boiler down. Either way, the right response is to stop guessing and compare what you see to the manufacturer's troubleshooting guide, or call a qualified technician.

Safety first

Boilers involve fuel, flue gases and hot pressurized water. Carbon monoxide is a real hazard when venting is damaged or blocked, so working carbon monoxide alarms are part of any boiler home. Do not change controls, mixing devices or vent parts unless you are trained and the manufacturer's instructions allow it.

Field card: Boiler Return Temperature and Condensing Boilers

Return water temperature decides whether flue gas condenses on the boiler.

  • Burning fuel makes water vapor that can condense into mildly acidic liquid.
  • Conventional boilers need warm enough returns to avoid sustained condensation and corrosion.
  • Condensing boilers are built to condense and do best with cooler returns.
  • Condensing boilers need a proper condensate drain and the right venting materials.
  • Always use the boiler manual for temperatures; examples here are not settings.
