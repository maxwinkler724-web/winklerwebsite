Indoor Go-Kart Track Ventilation: CO and NO2 from Gas Karts, Fan Placement and Alarms

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An indoor kart track puts a row of small gasoline engines in one big room, often with kids in the seats and parents on a balcony. Those engines make carbon monoxide (CO) and some nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and nothing outdoors will dilute it unless the ventilation system does. This lesson covers how health agencies describe the problem, how to size dilution air, where fans and sensors go, how electric karts change the picture, and how to heat the building.

Check the adopted code

Check the adopted code. This page is general education from public government documents read in 2026. It is not legal advice or a design. The mechanical code, fire code and the local health or fire official decide what a given building must have, so confirm requirements with them.

What health agencies found at indoor motor sport buildings

We did not find a government report written about an indoor kart track poisoning, so we are not claiming one. What we did find is close. A state health consultation, prepared under a federal public health agency's agreement, covers an indoor motocross facility where three boys aged 7 to 10 were diagnosed with nonfatal CO poisoning after about seven hours there. Their blood carboxyhemoglobin ran from 9.4% to 13.6%. Monitoring on the track showed a maximum of 60 ppm, with 31 ppm in the lobby and 19 ppm on the spectator mezzanine. The mezzanine reading kept climbing in the last hour even though no bikes were running, which the report reads as a sign that area was poorly ventilated. The consultation says that at other indoor motorized sport buildings owners should monitor CO while people are inside, and stop the activity and ventilate if CO passes 27 ppm.

A Utah Department of Health review summarizes earlier research on the same hazard. In an indoor kart race study it describes, the average CO in the air over a 45-minute race was 41 ppm, and the ten participants' breath CO rose by about 3% carboxyhemoglobin on average. The same review notes that in a rink study with motorized shows, NO2 was mostly below the 0.5 ppm detection limit while CO averaged 33 to 100 ppm. In a CDC weekly report on a large arena, the ventilation system ran at maximum and still could not keep up, and leaving more time between vehicle runs did not lower CO. A separate CDC report on an indoor ice arena traced a poisoning of 74 people to an engine whose exhaust was 4.8% CO while working and 2.3% while idling, against a well-tuned 0.5% to 1.0%.

Exposure limits to know

  • CO: the federal worker-safety agency's legal limit is 50 ppm as an 8-hour average. The federal research institute for worker safety recommends 35 ppm as an 8-hour average with a 200 ppm ceiling, and lists 1,200 ppm as immediately dangerous to life or health.
  • NO2: the legal limit is a 5 ppm ceiling. The research institute recommends 1 ppm as a 15-minute limit, and 13 ppm is immediately dangerous.
  • Children and the public: worker limits assume healthy adults at work. The state consultation names children, pregnant women and heart patients as the people to protect, which is why it uses a much lower 27 ppm action level for stopping activity.

Cut the source first: engine tune and fuel

Source control is cheaper than dilution. The ice arena report recommends regular maintenance of engines or switching to electric machines, and our lesson on the vehicle repair and bus garage covers tailpipe capture. For karts, an exhaust analyzer check of each engine at its scheduled service, a rich carburetor fixed, and a kart pulled from the track when it smokes or smells strong are all practical habits. (Karts cannot be hooked to a hose while racing, so general ventilation does the work on the track.) Do not let karts idle in the pit, because the ice arena data shows an idling engine still puts out CO.

For fuel, the federal workplace rule on flammable liquids limits container size and requires approved safety cans, which it defines as closing, pressure-relieving containers of up to 5 gallons, and it allows cabinets of up to 60 gallons of Category 1 to 3 liquids that are built to a fire test. Store and fill karts away from the spectator side and any ignition source, and ask the fire official which limits apply to your fuel.

Sizing dilution air: the steady-state idea

The federal worker-safety agency's technical manual gives the dilution ventilation relation in this form: required airflow equals the gas generated, times a mixing factor between 2 and 5, times one million, divided by the acceptable concentration. It also says dilution suits gases from portable or mobile sources and that exhausts should be close to the emission, with people upwind. We use the same structure for a steady room, written as

Q = G × 1,000,000 × K ÷ (C − Cout)

where Q is outdoor air in cfm, G is the CO the karts add in cfm of pure gas, K is the mixing factor, C is the CO level you want to hold in ppm, and Cout is the CO in the incoming air. A kart track is a good fit because the karts move around, but the formula assumes the room mixes and the emission is steady, so treat the answer as a floor, not a guarantee.

Worked example (our own example)

Our own example with assumed inputs. We assume 10 gasoline karts run at once, each putting out 6 g of CO per minute on average (an assumption for teaching, not a measured kart value). The building is 200 ft by 100 ft with a 25 ft ceiling. We want to hold 25 ppm, and we assume outdoor air carries 2 ppm. We use 387 cubic feet per pound-mole of gas, the figure the federal manual uses, and 28.01 g per mole for CO.

  1. Gas volume per gram: 387 ÷ 453.6 g per lb = 0.853 ft³ per gram-mole, and 0.853 ÷ 28.01 = 0.0305 ft³ of CO per gram.
  2. Total CO: 10 karts × 6 g/min = 60 g/min, and 60 × 0.0305 = 1.83 cfm of CO.
  3. Perfect mixing (K = 1): Q = 1.83 × 1,000,000 ÷ (25 − 2) = 79,500 cfm.
  4. Poor mixing (K = 2): Q is about 159,000 cfm.
  5. Room volume: 200 × 100 × 25 = 500,000 ft³. At 79,500 cfm that is 79,500 × 60 ÷ 500,000 = 9.5 air changes per hour. The room's time constant is 500,000 ÷ 79,500 = 6.3 minutes, so it takes roughly 20 minutes of running to approach the steady-state level.
  6. Sensitivity: holding 35 ppm instead gives 1.83 × 1,000,000 ÷ 33 = 55,400 cfm. If better engine tune cut CO output to one third (an assumption), the 25 ppm answer drops to about 26,500 cfm.

The lesson in the numbers is that the airflow is large, so the source and the target matter as much as the fans. Heating that air is costly in energy: using the standard sensible-heat relation (1.08 × cfm × temperature difference, our own use of it), 79,500 cfm heated from 20°F to 70°F needs about 1.08 × 79,500 × 50 = 4.3 million Btu/h. That is why tracks lean on tuned engines, electric karts and fans that run only when sensors call for them.

Fan placement and make-up air

  • Bring supply air in on the pit and spectator side and exhaust on the far side of the track, so air moves from people toward karts. The technical manual advises placing exhausts close to the source, keeping people upwind of the dilution zone and exhausting contaminated air outdoors instead of recirculating it.
  • Spread the supply and exhaust points. The manual's mixing factor of 2 to 5 is a reminder that dead corners cost airflow.
  • Do not send CO-laden air to a rooftop unit intake, and do not vent heaters or anything combustion-related into the track air.
  • Run fans on staged speeds so the lowest stage covers a quiet day and the top stage covers a full grid.

CO and NO2 detection and alarms

The state consultation measured CO at the track, a remote-control track, the lobby and the mezzanine, and all four were above background, so sensors belong in each zone people occupy, not just the track. Mount them at breathing height. Our own suggested alarm ladder, to be confirmed with the local authority: a first stage at a low level such as 25 ppm that sends fans to full speed, and a second stage at or near the consultation's 27 ppm action level that stops the karts and clears the building. Reoccupy only after readings return to zero, as the consultation recommends. The 1994 arena report mentions a proposed permit limit of 35 ppm over 15 minutes with no two consecutive readings above 200 ppm, which shows how other jurisdictions have framed it. Add an NO2 sensor because its limits are much lower than CO's, and test and calibrate per the maker's instructions. For general sensor staging see enclosed parking garage ventilation.

Electric karts and battery charging areas

Electric karts remove tailpipe CO, which is why the ice arena report points to electric machines. They add a charging area. The federal workplace rule for industrial truck batteries requires charging in a designated area with ventilation that clears gases given off by batteries, facilities to deal with spilled electrolyte, fire protection, no smoking, and no open flames or sparks. That rule is written for powered industrial trucks, so we use it as a model, not as the rule for karts. We are not stating a gas or an exhaust rate for any battery type here because we did not find a government source for karts. Read the battery maker's charging instructions, and see battery room ventilation.

Heating a large open track building

A tall, open building with big doors is a good candidate for radiant heat. A federal facilities engineering data sheet says infrared heat works best where convective heaters are impractical, warms people and floors instead of the whole air volume, and suits spaces with ceilings of 12 ft or more. It also warns that high-intensity heaters usually vent into the space and recommends about 4 cfm of ventilation per 1,000 Btu/h, and that unventilated installations have noticeably hurt indoor air quality. In a kart building, where CO is already the problem, choose heaters that vent combustion products outdoors. More on large open spaces in aircraft hangar heating and ventilation.

Pit area and spectator separation

The mezzanine and lobby readings in the state consultation show that CO does not stay on the track. Put spectators on the supply side with their own sensor, keep pit and fueling work on the exhaust side or in a room with its own exhaust, and close gaps between the pit and the seating. Watch where doors open, since opening a door changes airflow. For a related large-room case, see bowling center and family entertainment HVAC.

Field card: Indoor go-kart track ventilation

Source first, then dilute, then sense.

  • Gas karts put out CO and some NO2; health agencies found children poisoned at an indoor motocross building.
  • Dilution relation: Q = G × 1,000,000 × K ÷ (C − Cout).
  • Our own example: 10 karts at 6 g/min of CO need about 79,500 cfm for 25 ppm with perfect mixing.
  • Supply on the people side, exhaust on the track side, no recirculation.
  • Sensors in the track, pit and spectator areas; stop and ventilate above 27 ppm, reoccupy at zero.
  • Do not idle karts, and vent heaters outdoors.
  • Charge electric karts in a designated, ventilated area; follow the battery maker.
