Clothes Dryer Vents: Lint Fire Risk, Length Limits and Booster Fans

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A dryer moves a lot of warm, wet air and a lot of lint. If the duct that carries it outside is too long, too bent or packed with lint, the dryer runs hot and slow. This lesson covers why dryer exhaust matters, how the length limit is counted, and when a booster fan is and is not allowed.

Check your local code and the label

Check the adopted code and the dryer's own guide. The code figures below come from published summaries and one city's posted copy of the 2018 International Residential Code (IRC) chapter, not from the printed code book. Your jurisdiction may use a different edition or amendments, and the maker's installation guide for your exact model controls the job. This page is general education.

Why the duct matters

The U.S. Fire Administration studied clothes dryer fires in homes for 2008 to 2010. As summarized by Fire Engineering, it estimated about 2,900 such fires a year, with around 5 deaths, 100 injuries and $35 million in losses each year. The most common contributing factor was failure to clean, at 34 percent, and the most common first item ignited was dust, fiber or lint, at 28 percent. Those are older figures, so treat them as a picture of the problem, not a current count. The Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) says lint built up in the dryer or exhaust duct can block airflow, overheat the machine and start a fire. It also says damp clothes after a normal cycle, or drying that takes longer than it used to, can be a sign of a blocked screen or duct.

Cleaning is half of the story. The other half is the layout. A long or twisty duct slows the air, so lint settles and moisture lingers. That is why codes and makers both limit length.

What the code summaries say

The Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) Building America guide summarizes IRC section M1502 (2009 to 2021 editions) this way:

  • Duct material. Rigid or semi-rigid galvanized steel or aluminum with a smooth interior, 4 inches in diameter. Vinyl, nylon, foil, and PVC are not recommended.
  • Length. No more than 35 ft including the equivalent length of fittings, unless the maker's instructions allow more. A study-guide summary of M1502 describes the same 35 ft default.
  • Elbows. Subtract 5 ft for each 90 degree bend and 2.5 ft for each 45 degree bend.
  • Fasteners. Screws or similar fasteners that poke into the duct and catch lint must not be used. Joints are sealed with foil tape, and the guide says to support the duct at 12 ft intervals and keep it free of sags.
  • Termination. The duct ends outdoors with a backdraft damper. No screen goes on the outlet. The study-guide summary of M1502.3 adds a minimum open area of 12.5 square inches. The PNNL guide also lists 3 ft from building openings and 10 ft from an AC or heat pump condenser.
  • Transition duct. The short connector between the dryer and the wall duct is limited to 8 ft, listed to UL 2158A, and kept visible and accessible, per both summaries.

The Seattle copy of the 2018 chapter reads the length rule the same way: 35 ft from the transition duct to the outlet, and if the equivalent length goes over 35 ft, the maximum length must be labeled within 6 ft of the duct connection.

The maker can say something different

The code lets the maker's instructions set a different length, so always read the install guide. The tables below show how much the makers differ. They are from documents I read, and several are older sheets, so confirm the table for your model.

  • LG. Its help page gives 65 ft with no elbows and says to deduct 6 ft for each added elbow, with no more than four 90 degree elbows.
  • Whirlpool (older sheets). A compact electric model sheet allows only 36 ft of rigid duct with a box or louvered hood and no elbows, falling to 16 ft with two elbows. A Whirlpool gas dryer sheet from the same era gives 64 ft with none and 44 ft with two.
  • Maytag gas guide. Same type of chart: 64 ft rigid with no turns, 35 ft with three. It notes that side or bottom exhaust adds one 90 degree turn.
  • GE (a gas dryer spec sheet). 90 ft of rigid duct with no turns, 45 ft with two, and a turn of 45 degrees or less may be ignored.

Notice that the makers count elbows in different ways: a subtraction, a lookup table, or a free pass for small bends. Use the method printed for your model, and use the same units the guide uses (straight run versus total).

Worked example: how many elbows is too many?

A laundry room has a 4 in. rigid metal duct with 22 ft of straight pipe from the dryer to the wall cap. Use the code default of 35 ft and 5 ft per 90 degree elbow.

  1. Two 90 degree elbows: 2 × 5 = 10 ft of equivalent length.
  2. Developed length: 22 + 10 = 32 ft. The limit is 35 ft, so there is 3 ft to spare. It passes.
  3. Add a third 90 degree elbow: 3 × 5 = 15 ft, and 22 + 15 = 37 ft. That is 2 ft over 35, so the third elbow pushes it over.
  4. Using 45 degree bends does not rescue it by itself. Two 45s cost 2 × 2.5 = 5 ft, the same as one 90. Two 90s plus two 45s is 22 + 10 + 5 = 37 ft, over again.
  5. Fix it by shortening the straight run to 20 ft (20 + 15 = 35 passes exactly), by removing an elbow, or by using the maker's allowance if it is longer. Check the same layout against the maker tables: LG's table lists 36 ft allowed with three elbows, so a 22 ft straight run fits that table. The older Whirlpool compact sheet allows only 16 ft with two rigid elbows, so 22 ft would not fit that model at all.

Same duct, different answer, because the limit follows the model. If the layout cannot be changed, the maker may allow a longer duct only if you follow its stated limit.

Booster fans

A booster fan (a dryer exhaust duct power ventilator in code language) is not something to add without checking. The Seattle copy of the 2018 chapter says these ventilators must conform to UL 705 for dryer exhaust use and be installed per the fan maker's instructions, and the maximum duct length is then set by that maker's instructions. The study-guide summary treats a power ventilator as one of two ways to go beyond the calculated limit; the other is a longer run allowed by the dryer maker. The PNNL guide offers an in-line booster fan only as a retrofit idea for a very long or upward duct. And some dryer makers forbid it: the LG manual I read says, "Do not install a booster fan in the exhaust duct." Fans can also catch lint, so they need cleaning access. The safe order is: read the dryer guide, shorten the run if possible, and only then consider a listed fan the dryer maker does not prohibit.

Gas dryers

A gas dryer burns fuel, so its exhaust path matters even more. The Maytag gas guide says the dryer must be exhausted outdoors, never into a gas vent, chimney, wall, ceiling or concealed space, and to keep the area around the exhaust opening free of lint, dust and dirt. A GE gas dryer sheet says no other fuel-burning appliance shall be installed in the same closet with a gas dryer. Closets and combustion air are covered in Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters, and carbon monoxide basics are in Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do.

Cleaning intervals

CPSC says to clean the lint filter before or after every load, clean the vent and exhaust duct periodically, and check that air actually leaves the outside vent while the dryer runs. Makers give numbers. The Maytag gas guide says lint should be removed every 2 years or more often depending on use. The LG manual says to check the vent for lint monthly and clean it at least once a year. Heavy use, long runs, and pets all argue for the shorter end.

Related ventilation ducts

Bath and kitchen fans have their own duct rules; see Bathroom Exhaust Fans and Kitchen Range Hoods at Home. Never join a dryer duct to those; the GE sheet says not to share a duct with a kitchen exhaust.

Field card: Dryer Vents

Short, straight, smooth, metal, and clean.

  • Duct: 4 in. rigid or semi-rigid metal, smooth inside. No vinyl or foil.
  • Code default (IRC summary): 35 ft total; subtract 5 ft per 90, 2.5 ft per 45.
  • No protruding screws. Seal joints with foil tape.
  • Outlet: backdraft damper, no screen, outdoors only.
  • Transition duct: 8 ft max, UL 2158A listed, visible.
  • The maker's table for the model can allow more or less. Read it.
  • Booster fan: UL 705 listed per the code, and not if the dryer maker prohibits it.
  • Clean the lint filter every load and the duct at least yearly (maker figures vary).
