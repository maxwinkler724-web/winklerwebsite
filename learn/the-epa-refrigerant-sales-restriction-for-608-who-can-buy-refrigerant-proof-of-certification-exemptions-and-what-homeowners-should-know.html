The EPA Refrigerant Sales Restriction for 608: Who Can Buy Refrigerant, Proof of Certification, Exemptions, and What Homeowners Should Know

Last updated: 2026-11-02

EPA Section 608 does not only govern what a technician does with refrigerant. It also limits who may buy it. A supply counter has to decide, sale by sale, whether the person in front of it is allowed to walk out with a cylinder. This lesson walks through that sales restriction as an exam topic and as plain-language background for homeowners.

Check the current rule text

Check the current rule text. We read 40 CFR 82.154 on the eCFR, which showed itself as up to date as of 10/23/2026, and the EPA pages listed under Sources. The eCFR calls itself authoritative but unofficial, and rules get amended. This is general education, not legal advice, and it is not a substitute for the current regulation or for guidance from EPA.

Where the rule lives

The sales restriction is in 40 CFR 82.154, paragraph (c), under Subpart F. Paragraph (c)(1) says a person may not sell, distribute, or offer to sell or distribute certain refrigerants unless one of nine listed conditions applies. The covered refrigerants are class I and class II ozone-depleting substances and, since January 1, 2018, non-exempt substitutes, which is how HFC and HFO refrigerants come under it. EPA describes the restriction as covering refrigerant in cylinders, cans or drums, not equipment that contains refrigerant. The same section, paragraphs (a) and (b), holds the venting prohibition; see the venting lesson.

The conditions, in plain words

  • (c)(1)(i): the buyer is a certified Type I, Type II, Type III or Universal technician.
  • (c)(1)(ii): the buyer employs at least one such technician and provides proof to the seller. EPA's page says the written evidence goes to the wholesaler and that an authorized representative of the employer may make the purchase.
  • (c)(1)(iii) and (iv): the buyer, or an employee, holds the separate motor vehicle air conditioning certification under 40 CFR part 82, subpart B (Section 609), and the refrigerant is acceptable for MVAC use. Paragraph (iv) also says this does not relieve people of the requirements in sections 82.34(b) and 82.42(b).
  • (c)(1)(v): the refrigerant is sold only for eventual resale to certified persons.
  • (c)(1)(vi): the sale is to an appliance manufacturer.
  • (c)(1)(vii): the refrigerant is contained in an appliance with a fully assembled refrigerant circuit.
  • (c)(1)(viii): the refrigerant is charged into an appliance by a certified technician. The text we read describes this as charging during service; read the paragraph itself for who is covered.
  • (c)(1)(ix): a non-exempt substitute intended for MVAC use is sold in a container of two pounds or less, with a unique fitting. Containers made on or after January 1, 2018 also need a self-sealing valve that meets paragraph (c)(2).

What sellers check and keep

EPA's page says the wholesaler is legally responsible for making sure each customer falls into an allowed category. Paragraph (c)(3)(i) requires sellers of covered refrigerant to keep invoices showing the purchaser's name, the sale date and the quantity sold. Where the buyer relies on a certified employee, the seller also keeps the documentation of that employment. Records are kept for three years. The invoice requirement excepts exempt substitutes and the small MVAC cans under (c)(1)(ix). EPA recommends, but does not require, a signed statement from a buyer that refrigerant is for eventual resale to certified technicians, and a list of authorized representatives. Technician-side paperwork is a different topic; see what technicians keep.

The 609 versus 608 split

This is a favorite exam trap. EPA's Section 608 and 609 overlap page says Section 608 certification is required to buy ozone-depleting refrigerants and non-exempt substitutes for stationary equipment. A Section 609 technician may buy refrigerant approved for MVAC use, and EPA's sales page says a 609 technician cannot buy refrigerant intended for stationary equipment, regardless of container size. EPA also says small cans of substitute refrigerant such as R-134a for vehicle air conditioning are excepted for do-it-yourself work on one's own vehicle, when they meet the unique-fitting and self-sealing-valve conditions. That exemption is about vehicles. It does not turn a small can into something a homeowner may buy for a house system. Which refrigerants count as "exempt substitutes" is set by the rule and EPA lists, so check the current rule text instead of assuming.

For the three certification types themselves, see Type I, II, III explained. Disposable cylinders raise their own rules, covered in the disposable cylinder lesson.

Worked example: three buyers (our own example)

Our own example, with stated assumptions. We assume the product is a non-exempt substitute refrigerant for a residential split air conditioner, in a loose 25 lb cylinder. The 25 lb size is our own assumption; the paragraphs we read set no size threshold for stationary refrigerant, and the only size limit we read is the two-pound MVAC can in (c)(1)(ix). We assume each buyer is truthful and the seller is a wholesaler. No dollar figures or quantities beyond that are used.

  1. Homeowner wants the 25 lb cylinder for a split AC. Answer: no. Going down the chart: it is a loose cylinder, so (vii) does not apply; the can is not an MVAC can, so (ix) does not apply; a homeowner with no certification and no certified employee fails (i) and (ii); it is not for resale or a manufacturer, so (v) and (vi) fail. Nothing in the list fits, so the seller may not sell it under (c)(1). The motor vehicle exemption does not stretch to a house.
  2. Certified Type II tech buys for a job. Answer: yes. Paragraph (c)(1)(i) applies to a certified Type II technician. The seller checks the certification and keeps an invoice under (c)(3)(i) with the name, date and quantity. If the tech sends a coworker or buys through an employer instead, the path is (c)(1)(ii): the employer shows proof of employing a certified technician, and the seller keeps that documentation. Whether a given certification card is accepted at a counter is the seller's call to verify.
  3. A shop buys to resell. Answer: yes, if it truly is for resale to certified persons. Paragraph (c)(1)(v) allows a sale made only for eventual resale to certified persons. EPA recommends the shop give a signed statement to that effect. The condition travels with the product: when the shop later sells a cylinder, that is another sale that has to fit one of the categories, so selling it on to an uncertified homeowner would not. If the shop also employs a certified tech, (c)(1)(ii) could separately cover buying for its own use.

What this means for homeowners

  • Buying a new or replacement system is not what the restriction targets. EPA says it does not cover equipment or components that contain refrigerant, and (c)(1)(vii) covers refrigerant inside an appliance with a fully assembled circuit.
  • Adding or charging refrigerant in the system is service work. A homeowner who cannot buy the cylinder has a practical reason to hire a certified technician, who may buy under (c)(1)(i) and charge under (c)(1)(viii).
  • Refrigerant for a car is a separate path. See the MVAC can conditions above, and check the current rule text.
  • If a seller will not sell you a cylinder, that is the rule working as written, not a sales tactic.
  • Asking a contractor who is buying and what is being charged is reasonable. For release rules, see EPA 608 Basics.

Field card: refrigerant sales restriction

Who is buying, and what for?

  • The rule is 40 CFR 82.154(c); check the current rule text.
  • Certified technician (i) or employer with proof (ii) may buy.
  • 609 certification covers MVAC refrigerant only, not stationary equipment.
  • Small MVAC can: two pounds or less, unique fitting, self-sealing valve (ix).
  • Resale only to certified persons (v); pre-charged appliances (vii) are not the restriction.
  • Sellers keep invoices with name, date and quantity for three years (c)(3)(i).
