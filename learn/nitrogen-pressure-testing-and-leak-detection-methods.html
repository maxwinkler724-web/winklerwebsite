Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods

Part of the Refrigeration Service Guide

Last updated: 2026-10-16

You braze a new line set, close it up, put nitrogen in it and walk away. The next morning the gauge reads lower. Did it leak, or did the weather change? This lesson covers how long and how hard to test, how to correct for temperature, and how to find a leak once you know you have one.

What a standing test is for

A standing pressure test is a check that a new or repaired refrigerant circuit holds pressure before you evacuate and charge it. Dry nitrogen is the test gas the manuals below call for. The test does not replace the vacuum step; it comes first. For the vacuum side see Evacuation and Deep Vacuum Basics, and for the flowing nitrogen used while brazing see Nitrogen Purge Flow While Brazing. This page is about the standing test that comes after the brazing is done.

The setup

Daikin's SkyAir training guide calls for a nitrogen regulator that can read up to 700 psi or more when you are testing a system to 550 psig. The same guide warns not to leak test with air.

What pressure, and for how long?

There is no single number. The limit is the weakest part of the circuit, so the equipment maker's manual or data plate decides. The manuals we read show how much the numbers differ:

  • Daikin Comfort, three-phase R-32 condensing unit manual: pressurize to the maximum allowable pressure on the serial plate, hold at least 15 minutes, and if pressure drops, find the leak and repeat. In the electronic detector method, finish charging with dry nitrogen to 450 psig. The manual says never to use oxygen, high pressure air or flammable gases.
  • Danfoss condensing unit manual: the maximum test pressure is 465 psi. It says never to pressurize with oxygen or dry air, and not to use dye for leak detection.
  • Lennox mini-VRF installation instructions: a stress test at 647 psig held for 1 hour, then a lower test at 470 psig held for 24 hours. It says to use only oxygen-free nitrogen.
  • Daikin SkyAir training guide: stages of 150 psi for 3 minutes, 325 psi for 5 minutes, then 550 psi for 24 hours.

Those are different products with different ratings, so do not copy one number onto another unit. Never go above the lowest-rated component, and use the stated figure from the manual in front of you. If a manual gives no number, call the manufacturer before you guess. See also Mini-Split Installation Basics for where this step fits in an install.

Never oxygen, never shop air

Oxygen and compressed air are not acceptable test gases. The manuals above say not to use them. Dry nitrogen is the gas to use, through a regulator, on a circuit that is sealed and isolated from anything you do not want pressurized, such as a charged unit with closed service valves. Follow the manual on valve positions and on what to disconnect first.

Temperature changes the reading

Nitrogen in a sealed circuit behaves like a gas in a rigid box. When it cools, pressure falls; when it warms, pressure rises. The relationship is P2 = P1 × T2 / T1, where pressures are absolute (psia = psig + 14.7) and temperatures are absolute (Rankine = °F + 460). The Daikin SkyAir guide also warns that nitrogen pressure fluctuates with ambient temperature above 300 psi, and gives a quick estimate: (temperature when pressurized minus temperature when checked) × 0.80 equals the expected pressure drop in psi.

Worked example: did it leak?

You pressurize a circuit to 400 psig at 90°F. Next morning it reads 385 psig at 60°F.

  1. Convert the start pressure: 400 + 14.7 = 414.7 psia.
  2. Convert the temperatures: 90 + 460 = 550 R; 60 + 460 = 520 R.
  3. Predict the new pressure: P2 = 414.7 × 520 / 550 = 414.7 × 0.9455 = 392.1 psia.
  4. Convert back: 392.1 − 14.7 = 377.4 psig expected with no leak.
  5. Compare: you read 385 psig, which is about 7.6 psi higher than predicted.

The gauge fell 15 psi, but the cooling alone explains about 22.6 psi. Cross-check with the Daikin quick estimate: (90 − 60) × 0.80 = 24 psi, a similar answer. So this reading shows no sign of a leak. A reading above the prediction usually means the gas inside was warmer than the air you measured, for example line set in a sunny wall or attic. Working backward, 385 psig corresponds to a gas temperature near 70°F. Take temperature at the pipe, not just the room, and think about gauge accuracy too: an ordinary analog gauge on a 0 to 800 psi scale cannot resolve a few psi. If the reading had come in well below 377 psig, you would suspect a leak and start looking.

Finding the leak

  • Bubble solution. Apply to joints while the circuit is pressurized. An ACHR News article notes it works best when the leak location is roughly known and the system is adequately pressurized, and that a homemade mix should not corrode or freeze onto the fittings.
  • Electronic detectors. They sense refrigerant in the air, so they need some refrigerant present. ACHR News says to verify a detector before relying on it. INFICON states that the European standard EN 14624 sets 5 g/year as the minimum sensitivity for electronic detectors used on HVAC/R systems.
  • Ultrasonic. These pick up the sound of escaping gas. ACHR News says they work well as long as nothing else nearby makes sound at the same frequency.
  • UV or fluorescent dye. The dye travels with the oil and stains at the leak. It is not for every system: the Danfoss condensing unit manual above says not to use dye for leak detection. Check the maker's position first.

Manuals differ on adding refrigerant to the nitrogen. The Daikin Comfort manual describes a 10 psig refrigerant charge in its electronic detector method yet also says no refrigerant shall be used for pressure testing. Follow the manual for your unit, and see Leaks and Leak Repair Basics for repair habits and records.

The EPA rule on venting nitrogen

Under 40 CFR 82.154(a)(1), no one servicing an appliance may knowingly vent or release refrigerant, but the same paragraph exempts nitrogen in any application from the prohibition. So releasing clean nitrogen after a test is not a violation. The wrinkle is a nitrogen charge that has refrigerant mixed in. Section 82.154(a)(2) says de minimis releases tied to good faith attempts to recycle or recover refrigerant are allowed, but only when the listed practices (including recovery equipment, technician certification and reclamation rules) are followed. Section 82.154(a)(3) says knowingly releasing refrigerant after it has been recovered is a violation. The regulation text we read does not mention mixtures of nitrogen and refrigerant at all. Keep your test gas clean so the question never comes up, and ask EPA or your employer before you vent a mix.

Field card: Nitrogen Pressure Testing and Leak Detection Methods

Regulator on the cylinder, maker's number on the gauge, temperature in the math.

  • Test pressure comes from the manual or data plate; never exceed the lowest-rated part.
  • Manuals read: 450 psig, 465 psi max, 647/470 psig, 550 psig for 24 hours. All differ.
  • Nitrogen only, through a regulator. Never oxygen, air or flammable gas.
  • P2 = P1 × T2 / T1 in psia and Rankine.
  • 400 psig at 90°F should read about 377 psig at 60°F with no leak.
  • Bubbles, electronic, ultrasonic; dye only if the maker allows it.
  • Clean nitrogen may be vented; refrigerant may not.
