HVAC for Quarry and Aggregate Plant Control Houses: Dust, Positive Pressure and Heat from Drives

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A crusher plant control house is a small room with a big job: it holds the starters, drives and controls that keep the plant running, and it sits in the dustiest air on the site. This lesson covers how rock dust ruins electrical gear, how a filtered, slightly pressurized house keeps it out, and how to size the cooling for the heat the gear gives off.

Why the dust matters

Crushing, screening and conveying stone throws off fine dust. A federal worker safety agency notes that cutting, grinding and crushing stone and rock creates very small particles of crystalline silica that workers can breathe in, and that silicosis is an incurable lung disease. So the control house is a health issue for the operator as well as an equipment issue. A federal mine safety publication on dust control says enclosed booths and control rooms protect people only when they are designed and maintained properly, and that the most effective approach is a filtration and pressurization system with heating and air conditioning built in.

For the gear, dust is also a problem. It coats drive heat sinks and starter parts like a blanket, it clogs cooling fans, and with humidity it can turn to a conductive crust. The lesson on heat sinks and enclosure cooling for drives shows how a dusty sink leads to overtemperature trips.

Positive pressure with filtered outside air

The idea is simple. Bring in more filtered outside air than leaks out, and the house stays at a slightly higher pressure than outdoors. Leakage then flows outward through every gap. The federal mine safety dust publication reports field studies where booths did well while holding about 0.01 to 0.40 inches of water gauge of positive pressure, enough to stop wind from pushing dust in. These are study results, not a code requirement, so follow the plant owner's design.

  • Intake filter efficiency. The same publication says field systems used intake filters rated 95 percent or better on respirable dust, and that in lab tests a 99 percent filter gave about ten times the protection of a 38 percent filter.
  • Recirculation filter. It also describes a recirculation filter in the unit; lab tests showed that even filters in the 85 to 94.9 percent range improved protection a great deal and cut the time for the room to settle after a door closed by more than half.
  • Doors. One study it cites found about nine times more dust in a booth when the door was opened often. A door closer and a sign cost little.
  • Housekeeping. Floors and seats that hold dust get stirred up, and the publication advises moving floor-level heaters so they do not blow across a dusty floor.

The federal dust control handbook for mineral processing has a whole chapter on enclosures. Its headings cover keeping doors and windows closed, outside air inlet location, pre-cleaners, visual indicators for filter changes, and ease of filter change. Take two ideas from that: put the intake where it draws the cleanest air available, and make the filters easy to reach so they actually get changed. For gauges, gaskets and holding frames, see air handler filter racks and bypass gaps.

Filter loading on a dusty site

A filter on a crusher plant can load in weeks, not months. A loaded filter cuts supply airflow, the house loses pressure, and unfiltered dust sneaks in around doors and cable entries. Our own field advice, not from a source: install a differential pressure gauge across the house wall and another across the filter bank, write the clean and change-out readings on the unit, and check the gauges on every visit. If the house reads zero or negative, look at the filters, the door and the fan before anything else. Do not remove a filter to restore airflow. That sends dust straight to the drives.

Wall-mount and packaged units on skid buildings

Many control houses are skid buildings: a steel box with a wall-mount or packaged unit bolted to the outside. These are convenient, but check these points (our own general practice, so follow the maker's instructions):

  • Outdoor coils load with dust too. Plan for coil cleaning on a schedule, and check the airflow clearances, since plants pile material near units.
  • Confirm the unit can bring in outside air through a filter. Many packaged units recirculate only, and need an outside air option for pressurization.
  • Protect the unit from falling rock and conveyor spill, and keep its drain clear. A plugged condensate drain fills a house with water.
  • Rated capacity is stated at standard test conditions. Hot quarry afternoons mean less output, so read the maker's capacity table at your outdoor temperature.

Heat from motor control centers and drives

Electrical gear turns a small share of the power it passes into heat. A drive is never perfectly efficient, and the lost few percent is released into the room as heat; the maker's data gives the real loss for your drive. Our example below assumes 97 percent. Starters, contactors and bus add smaller losses. For the room itself, see the motor control center lesson and the panel cooling lesson. For redundancy, pressurization and gas detection, see plant control room HVAC.

Vibration and loose fittings

Crushers shake the whole plant. Our own field advice: use lock washers or locking hardware, mark fasteners with a torque stripe so movement shows, support refrigerant lines and conduit so they do not fatigue, and add flexible connections where ducts meet a skid. Recheck terminals, fan mounts and panel latches on a schedule, because a loose power connection runs hot. Electrical work belongs to qualified people. A federal mine safety card for contractors says to treat all power as live, lock out and tag out before repairs, and to leave electrical work to qualified people. It also says to investigate overheating wiring, melted insulation, or fuses and breakers that keep tripping, and to take defective equipment out of service.

Seasonal shutdown care

Many quarries shut down in winter. Our own general advice, subject to the maker's instructions: before shutdown, change the filters, clean the coils and blow dust out of the gear with the power locked out by qualified staff. Keep the house closed up, seal cable and duct openings so it cannot fill with dust or small animals, and keep it ventilated or lightly heated per the equipment makers so damp air does not condense on cold electrical gear. Before restart, check the filters, drain, coil and fan, and confirm the house pressure gauge before the plant runs.

Worked example (our own): cooling a control house

This is our own example with assumed numbers, not a design. Assume a 12 ft by 30 ft by 9 ft insulated skid house in a sunny location, with outdoor design air at 95 F and an indoor target of 78 F. We use 3.412 Btu/h per watt and 12,000 Btu/h per ton. A college cooling load note lists the ton as 12,000 Btu/h and uses about 3.4 Btu/h per watt, and the 1.08 sensible heat relation for ventilation air.

Assumptions (ours): drives pass 120 kW of output at 97 percent efficiency; starters, bus and contactors lose 1.5 kW; controls and a UPS give off 1.0 kW; lights 0.4 kW; 2 people; 150 cfm of filtered outside air; roof 360 sq ft with U of 0.05 and an assumed CLTD of 60 F; sun-facing walls 400 sq ft with U of 0.06 and an assumed CLTD of 30 F. Use the method's Q = U × area × CLTD form.

  1. Drive loss: 120 kW × (100% − 97%) = 3.6 kW.
  2. Electrical heat: 3.6 + 1.5 + 1.0 + 0.4 = 6.5 kW. Convert: 6,500 W × 3.412 = 22,178 Btu/h.
  3. People: 2 × 250 Btu/h sensible = 500 Btu/h. The college note lists 230 to 255 Btu/h sensible for seated light work, so 250 is within that range. Latent: 2 × 200 = 400 Btu/h (the note lists 190 to 255).
  4. Roof: 0.05 × 360 × 60 = 1,080 Btu/h. Walls: 0.06 × 400 × 30 = 720 Btu/h. Solar and envelope: 1,800 Btu/h.
  5. Outside air: 1.08 × 150 cfm × (95 − 78) = 1.08 × 150 × 17 = 2,754 Btu/h sensible. We ignore its moisture to keep the example short; a real design would not.
  6. Sensible total: 22,178 + 500 + 1,800 + 2,754 = 27,232 Btu/h. Add 400 latent = 27,632 Btu/h.
  7. Tons: 27,632 ÷ 12,000 = 2.30 tons. With an assumed 10 percent margin: 27,632 × 1.1 = 30,395 Btu/h, or 2.53 tons.

The electrical gear is about 22,178 of the 27,632 Btu/h, roughly four-fifths. That is why this load barely changes with the season: the drives make heat in January too, so winter still needs cooling and the outside air filter should still be sized for it. If the plant wants two units so one can cover the other, each would need to carry about 2.5 tons, so the nominal sizes need to be checked against the maker's capacity at the outdoor temperature.

Field card: HVAC for Quarry and Aggregate Plant Control Houses

Pressure the house, filter the air, watch the gauge.

  • Dust: crushed stone makes fine silica dust; keep it out of people and gear.
  • Positive pressure: studies saw good results at about 0.01 to 0.40 in. w.g.
  • Filters: intake filters at 95 percent or better on respirable dust in field systems.
  • Keep doors closed; frequent opening raised booth dust about nine times in one study.
  • Heat: watts × 3.412 = Btu/h; 12,000 Btu/h = 1 ton.
  • Our example: 27,632 Btu/h, 2.30 tons, about 2.5 tons with margin.
  • Check fasteners for vibration; lock out before electrical work.
