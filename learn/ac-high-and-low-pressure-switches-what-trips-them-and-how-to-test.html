AC High and Low Pressure Switches: What Trips Them and How to Test

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A condensing unit that quits on its own, with a contactor that will not pull in, is often telling you a pressure switch has opened. These switches are the refrigerant circuit's circuit breakers. This lesson covers what they protect, the settings manufacturers publish, how the control board reacts, what usually trips them, and how to test one without defeating it.

What the two switches protect

A high-pressure switch (HPS) senses discharge or liquid pressure and opens its contacts if pressure climbs too high. Goodman's service manual describes it as breaking the control circuit before the compressor motor overloads. A low-pressure switch (LPS) senses suction pressure and opens when it drops too far. On many heat pumps the low-side device is a loss-of-charge switch on the liquid line, set lower because heat pumps run low suction pressures in heating. Both are normally closed switches wired into the 24-volt control circuit. Trane's support page says its high-pressure controls are wired in series between the controller and the compressor contactor coil. When a switch opens, the contactor drops out and the compressor stops.

Published settings, and why they differ

Settings belong to the equipment, so read them from the unit's literature. These are the figures the makers print, for the models those documents cover:

  • Carrier (R-410A "Puron" residential AC and heat pump): HPS opens around 610 psig (plus or minus 10) and closes at 420 (plus or minus 25). The AC low-pressure switch opens near 50 psig. The heat pump loss-of-charge switch opens at 23 (plus or minus 5) and closes at 55 (plus or minus 5).
  • Goodman (GSXC/GSZC series): HPS opens at 610 (plus or minus 10) and closes at 420 (plus or minus 25). LPS opens at 21 and resets at about 50.
  • Lennox (XP17 heat pump): HPS trips at 590 and resets at 418. LPS trips at 25 and resets at 40. Both reset automatically.
  • Rheem (one add-on kit sheet): HPS opens above 425 psig and closes again below 350, then a five-minute compressor delay runs. Treat it as a kit value, not a rule.
  • Copeland (scroll compressor guidance): a maximum high-pressure cut-out of 650 psig for the R-410A scroll compressors in that document. For low pressure, no lower than 20 psig on heat pumps and no lower than 55 on air conditioning.
  • Trane: the support page we read gives behavior but lists no psig setpoints.

Two things stand out. First, the "open" and "close" numbers are far apart, which keeps the switch from chattering. Second, the numbers differ by maker and model and by refrigerant (Carrier lists 400 psig for R-22 where it lists 610 for Puron), so a number from a forum is not the number on your unit.

Auto reset, manual reset, and lockout

An auto-reset switch closes again by itself once pressure falls back past its close point. Goodman says its high and low controls reset automatically. Copeland's compressor guide goes the other way: it says both the high and low controls should have a manual reset for the highest level of protection, and that an electronic controller can lock the system out after repeated low-pressure trips. Many equipment makers get the same effect by letting the switch reset itself and having the control board count the trips.

Trane's page is a clear example. If the low-pressure control stays open for one continuous second, the compressor stops at once and cannot restart for at least three minutes. Four consecutive opens during one cooling call lock the compressor out until someone resets it, and the high-pressure control follows the same lockout rule. Lennox's heat pump control uses a "five-strike" count for both switches, counted within a single cooling demand. The low switch is ignored for the first 90 seconds, a count of four or fewer is cleared when the demand ends, and a lockout clears by cycling power at the R terminal or using the board's test pins. Carrier's variable-speed manual works differently again: it restarts after a high-pressure switch trip once discharge pressure falls below 425 psig and does not escalate those trips to a lockout. The lesson is that the counting logic is specific to the board, so read that unit's sequence. See AC Short Cycling for how safeties fit into the bigger short-cycling picture.

What trips the high-pressure switch

Carrier's service manual lists a dirty condenser coil, a failed fan motor, and condenser air recirculation. Emerson's troubleshooting chart adds refrigerant overcharge. One more belongs on the list:

  • Non-condensables. An ACHR News article explains that air trapped in the condenser does not condense and takes up room, so less surface is available to condense refrigerant and high-side pressure rises. It describes a check with the compressor off and the fan running, and says to rule out dirty coils, poor airflow and recirculation first.

Start with the cheap, visible items: coil condition (cleaning the outdoor coil, dirty condenser and head pressure), then the fan and its capacitor (condenser fan motor and capacitor failures), then charge (undercharge vs. overcharge).

What trips the low-pressure switch

Low charge is the classic cause, and Emerson's chart tells the tech to check for loss of charge, a restriction or a faulty control. Carrier's variable-speed manual advises checking for an undercharge and confirming indoor airflow in cooling. Low indoor airflow and a frozen evaporator coil pull suction pressure down. Copeland even suggests a higher low-pressure cut-out of about 95 psig to prevent coil icing. Low outdoor temperature can also make suction fall at startup; Carrier's manual describes a Winter Start Control that bypasses the low-pressure switch for the first three minutes of low-ambient cooling operation. That is a built-in, designed bypass. See Low-Ambient Cooling.

Testing a switch

  1. Confirm the symptom: no 24 volts reaching the contactor coil, or a board code pointing at the switch.
  2. Remove power at the disconnect and verify it is off.
  3. Carrier's procedure: disconnect the switch leads and check across the switch with an ohmmeter. A good, closed switch shows continuity. Do the check with refrigerant pressure in the normal range, since a switch that is open because pressure really is out of range tells you nothing about the switch.
  4. If the switch is open, compare with a gauge on the matching service port. Pressure inside the setting means the switch or its wiring is suspect. Pressure beyond the setting means the switch is doing its job and the cause is in the system.
  5. Goodman says to replace a switch that does not open and close within its published values. Carrier's variable-speed manual also notes that loose connections or a broken wire can look like a trip.

Gauge connections and refrigerant handling are covered by EPA 608 certification. Reading a P-T chart is in Pressure-Temperature Basics.

Why a jumper is not a fix

Skipping a switch with a jumper wire takes the protection out of the circuit. Our own view: a jumper is at most a very brief diagnostic step by a qualified tech to see whether the contactor and compressor respond, and it comes out immediately. Copeland's guidance is blunt: do not operate the compressor with the low-pressure cut-out disabled. A jumper left in place leaves a compressor with nothing to stop it from running with low charge or runaway pressure. The switch tripped for a reason; find the reason.

Worked example: reading a trip against the setting

A 95°F afternoon, an R-410A condenser, a switch published to open at 610 psig and close at 420 psig. The gauge on the liquid port shows 612 psig just as the compressor drops out.

  1. Compare to the setting: 612 is within 610 plus or minus 10, so the switch opened where it should. The trip is real, not a bad switch.
  2. Convert to saturation temperature with a published R-410A table (Arkema Forane 410A). The liquid column shows 606.4 psig at 149°F and 613.9 at 150°F.
  3. Interpolate: (612 − 606.4) ÷ (613.9 − 606.4) = 5.6 ÷ 7.5 = 0.75. Saturation is about 149.7°F.
  4. Condensing temperature over ambient: 149.7 − 95 = 54.7°F. The Dirty Condenser lesson passes along a rule of thumb of about 15 to 20 degrees over ambient for many modern systems, so this is far out of range and points at the condenser, fan, charge or air in the system.
  5. For contrast, 115°F condensing (20 over) is 392.3 psig on that table. The switch has nowhere near that to worry about.
  6. The close point: 420 psig falls between 419.4 at 120°F and 425.0 at 121°F, about 120.1°F. After a trip the unit will not restart until condensing temperature falls to roughly 120°F, which is why a hot, dirty coil can leave a unit stuck off for a long time.

Note that these are saturation values from one chart; a P-T chart's units and the gauge's calibration matter, so follow your own gauge and the unit's data.

Field card: AC High and Low Pressure Switches

A tripped switch is a symptom. Find the pressure problem before you touch the switch.

  • HPS opens on high pressure; LPS or loss-of-charge switch opens on low pressure. Both sit in the 24 V control circuit.
  • Settings are unit specific. Examples: Carrier and Goodman HPS about 610 open, 420 close (R-410A).
  • Auto-reset switch plus board trip count is common. Read the unit's lockout logic.
  • HPS causes: dirty coil, failed fan, recirculation, overcharge, air in system.
  • LPS causes: low charge, low airflow, frozen coil, restriction, low ambient.
  • Test with power off: leads off, ohmmeter across the switch, then compare with a gauge.
  • Never leave a jumper in place.
