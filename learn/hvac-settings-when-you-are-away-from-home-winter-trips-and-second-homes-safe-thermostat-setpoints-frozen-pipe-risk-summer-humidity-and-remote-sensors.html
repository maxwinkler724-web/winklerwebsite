HVAC Settings When You Are Away From Home: Winter Trips and Second Homes

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A house that sits empty for weeks has no one to notice a cold room, a dripping joint or a damp smell. This lesson covers what to set on the thermostat, what to shut off, and what to watch from afar, so a winter trip or a second home does not turn into a burst pipe or a mold problem.

The main rule: keep the heat on

The most repeated advice from public sources is simple. When you travel in cold weather, do not turn the heat off and do not set it below 55 degrees F. The Indiana Office of Utility Consumer Counselor says not to set the thermostat lower than 55 degrees when traveling. Illinois Extension says to make sure heat is left on and set no lower than 55 degrees, and North Carolina State Extension says the same. Oklahoma State Extension gives no number but says the heating system should be in good repair and left on.

Notice what 55 F is: a floor, not a target. Pipes in outside walls and crawl spaces are colder than the room the thermostat sits in, and a thermostat can read a few degrees off. Our own habit is to add a margin above the floor. That choice is ours, not a rule from those sources.

This is different from the daily setback in Thermostat Setback Schedules. The U.S. Department of Energy suggests turning a thermostat down 7 to 10 degrees for 8 hours a day while you are away or asleep, which it says can trim as much as 10 percent a year from heating bills. A trip is a different job: the goal is not the biggest saving, it is staying above the freezing risk for days or weeks.

Where pipes freeze first

Illinois Extension says the most exposed pipes are typically near outer walls, in crawl spaces or in the attic. Oklahoma State adds basements and garages. The diagram below marks the spots we check first in a typical house.

Open the cabinet doors

Illinois Extension, North Carolina State Extension and Oklahoma State Extension all say to open cabinet doors under sinks so warm room air can reach the pipes, especially where a sink sits against an outside wall. Indiana's guidance ties this to extremely cold weather, zero or below. If you will be gone, open them before you leave, since no one will be there on the cold night to do it. Remove any harmful cleaners first if children or pets may visit, as Oklahoma State notes for homes with young children.

Know your main water shutoff

Several sources say everyone should know where the main shutoff valve is and how to close it: North Carolina State Extension says to know where and how to shut off water from the main valve, and Indiana points to the master shutoff to stop flow into the building. Illinois Extension goes further for absences: when away, you can drain and shut off the water system, except indoor sprinkler systems.

Our own caution: closing the main valve limits a leak from a failed hose or fitting, but it does not stop pipes from freezing unless the lines are also drained, and a drained system must be refilled and checked on return. A home with hot-water heating loops, a boiler or a sprinkler system needs a professional's advice before anyone drains anything. If you shut off the water without draining, still keep the heat on at the floor setting.

Summer and humid-climate trips

The EPA's guide to mold and moisture says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and to use air conditioners or dehumidifiers when needed. It also says to keep air conditioner drip pans clean and drain lines clear, which matters more in a house that runs unattended. The same guide does not address vacant homes, so how warm to set the cooling is our own judgment. We would not shut cooling off in a humid climate. We would raise the setpoint a few degrees and verify the result with a humidity meter, as in Humidity Meters and Wireless Probes.

Remote alarms and leak sensors

The EPA's WaterSense leak-detection guide (a PDF on epa.gov) says households can waste thousands of gallons a year from undetected leaks. It says leak detection devices can alert you by sound, phone or email, that moisture sensors are common for hard-to-see spots, and that devices with automatic shutoff valves can stop the water supply after a major leak. It also notes that utility meter alerts can lag for days, slower than in-home devices.

For HVAC, we add a Wi-Fi thermostat or a separate low-temperature sensor that texts you if the house falls toward your floor. These are our own suggestions; the sources above do not rate any device. A remote alarm only helps if the internet and power stay on, which is why the next section matters.

Outages and generators

If the power fails while you are gone, the heat fails with it, and the house cools toward outdoor temperature. Our rule of thumb is to ask a neighbor to check after any storm. When power returns, see Power Came Back but the HVAC Won't Start. If someone starts a generator at the house, the CDC says never to run it inside a home, basement or garage, and never within 20 feet of a window, door or vent. It also advises a battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide detector.

What not to do

  • Do not turn the heat off entirely. Every source above says to leave it on at 55 F or higher.
  • Do not set a daily-savings setback and forget it. A 7 to 10 degree setback fits a daily routine, and from a 70 F start it would land near 60 to 63 F. Know your actual setting.
  • Do not cut power to an outdoor heat pump for the whole season without reading the manufacturer's instructions. Some units depend on powered components that warm up before running. This is our own caution; check your manual or ask your installer. A heat pump that runs a lot in cold weather may be normal, see Heat Pump Runs All the Time in Winter.
  • Do not skip the check-in. Indiana says to have a neighbor check the home regularly; Illinois and North Carolina State say to have someone check daily that the heat is still on.

Worked example: our own away-from-home checklist

This is our own example, not a published standard. Assume a three-week January trip to a house in a cold climate. The setpoints are our choices; the source column shows what each item rests on.

Check the arithmetic: 55 F floor plus a 3 F margin gives the 58 F setpoint. If the 58 F thermostat reads 2 F high, the room could be near 56 F, still above the floor. An alert at 50 F then fires only if the house is clearly losing heat, about 8 degrees below the setpoint (58 minus 50). For summer, our example setpoint would be a few degrees above your normal 75 F, with a humidity meter confirming the room stays below 60 percent, the EPA ceiling.

Field card: HVAC settings when away

Heat stays on. Know the valve. Have someone look.

  • Winter: never below 55 F; we use a little higher for margin.
  • Open cabinet doors under outside-wall sinks.
  • Know where the main water shutoff is; ask a plumber before draining.
  • Summer: keep humidity under 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50, per the EPA.
  • Use leak and low-temperature alerts, and have someone check the house.
  • Generators stay outdoors, 20 feet or more from openings.
