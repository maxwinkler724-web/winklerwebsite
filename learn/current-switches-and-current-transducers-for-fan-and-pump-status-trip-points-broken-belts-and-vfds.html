Current Switches and Transducers for Fan and Pump Status: Trip Points, Broken Belts and VFDs

Last updated: 2026-10-28

The building automation system (BAS) tells a fan to start. How does it know the fan is really moving air? The cheapest answer is a small sensor clamped around one motor wire. This lesson covers how that works, where it can be fooled, how to set the trip point, and when a pressure or airflow device is the better proof.

What a current sensor proves

A current switch or transducer reads the current in one conductor of the motor circuit. A split-core model opens like a clamp so you can close it around an existing insulated wire without disconnecting anything. A solid-core model is a closed ring, so the wire has to be threaded through it. One state university BAS standard we read calls for current status switches on fans, pumps and motors in both styles, with a digital or analog signal to the BAS.

The idea is simple. A motor that is turning a loaded fan draws a lot more current than a motor that is spinning free. A maker application note says that on a typical fan or pump, the motor itself uses only about 40% or less of the energy; the rest goes into moving air or liquid. When the belt or coupling lets go, the load disappears and the current falls. One maker data sheet puts the drop at up to 50%, and another maker article says roughly 60% on a fan coil. So current proves load, which is better than a starter auxiliary contact that only proves the coil is pulled in.

Switch or transducer

A current switch gives a contact or digital signal: on above the trip point, off below it. A current transducer gives a signal that rises with current. A maker manual for a split-core transducer lists 4 to 20 mA, 0 to 5 V DC and 0 to 10 V DC outputs depending on model, with a slide switch that picks one of three factory ranges. Use a switch for run or no-run. Use a transducer to trend amps and set the alarm threshold in software.

Fixed or adjustable trip

Some switches have a factory-fixed trip point; most BAS-grade ones are adjustable. The maker data sheets we read give minimum trip points from 0.5 A to 2.5 A depending on model, and a typical hysteresis of about 10%, meaning the output does not flip back until current moves past the trip point by that margin.

Setting the trip point

You have two real readings to work from: the current with the motor spinning unloaded (belt off, or a coupling disconnected) and the current at normal running. Set the trip between them. One maker application note says to set it just under normal operating amperage after calibration; that works when the unloaded reading is close to normal. When the readings are far apart, the middle is more forgiving. Take both with a clamp meter first.

Worked example: supply fan, our own numbers

These figures are our own assumed example, not from any product. A belt-driven supply fan motor draws 14 A at normal running. With the belt broken, the motor spins free and draws 6 A.

  1. Gap between the two readings: 14 − 6 = 8 A.
  2. Middle of the gap: 6 + (8 ÷ 2) = 10 A. We choose a 10 A trip.
  3. Room on the low side: 10 − 6 = 4 A, which is 67% above the broken-belt reading.
  4. Room on the high side: 14 − 10 = 4 A, which is about 29% below normal.
  5. Hysteresis check: if hysteresis is about 10% (assumed from the typical data sheet value), the reset is near 11 A, still 3 A below 14 A, so normal running reads solidly "on."

Why not set it at 13 A, just under normal? That leaves only 1 A, about 7%, of room. A low supply voltage or a seasonal change in load could drop a healthy motor under the line and drop the fan out of the proof. Why not 7 A? A broken belt that drifts up a little would be called "running." Ten amps tolerates both. Prove it by testing with the belt off, under lockout rules and with guards back on for any run.

The small fan that reads too low

Now an exhaust fan, again our own assumed numbers. The motor draws 1.2 A when running. Its sensor has a minimum trip of 2 A. The motor is below the sensor's lowest setting, so it always reads "off." The fix is to pass the wire through the sensor window more than once. A maker manual gives the rule: turns × actual current = sensed current. Three turns gives 3 × 1.2 = 3.6 A, which is above the 2 A minimum. The same maker warns that extra turns divide the largest current the sensor can handle, so check the rating.

Now set the trip. Assume the fan draws 0.5 A with the wheel unloaded (our own assumption); with three turns the sensor sees 1.5 A. Pick a trip at 2.5 A as the sensor reads it. That is 2.5 ÷ 3 = about 0.83 A of real motor current, between 0.5 and 1.2. Margins at the sensor are 1.0 A below and 1.1 A above. Rules for the turns:

  • Count passes through the window, not loops outside it. Three wire segments should be visible inside the ring.
  • Use only one conductor of the circuit. A hot and neutral together cancel to about zero.
  • De-energize and lock out before you rewire. The maker manual says power must be off when wiring.

Where a current sensor gets fooled

  • Broken belt, but fan unloaded current close to normal. A motor with a small gap is hard to separate. Below about half load the current curve flattens and gets non-linear, and a DOE motor fact sheet says current is not a useful indicator of load in that low-load region.
  • Partial failures. A slipping belt still draws current. A closed damper, or a fan turning the wrong way after a rewire, can pass while moving little air.
  • VFD at low speed. A drive-fed fan draws less current as speed drops. A maker application note says current follows the volts-per-hertz curve, low at low speed and high at full speed, so a fixed setpoint is hard to use to catch belt loss. DOE material shows how fast load falls: at half speed a fan needs about one-eighth of full power. That note limits belt-loss detection to speeds above 40 or 50 Hz. A full-speed trip can read "off" at low speed on a healthy fan, and a broken belt there may barely change the reading.

Line side or load side of a VFD

Put the sensor on the load side, on a motor lead between the drive and the motor. Two VFD makers state this directly. The line side carries 60 Hz supply current into the drive, which does not directly show the motor. The load side carries the drive's chopped output, so use a sensor listed for VFD use, and note the maker warning that drive fields can disrupt devices. One data sheet reports on and off for drive output from 12 to 115 Hz; another needs the drive to reach 40 Hz when load is lost. Often the better VFD status is the drive's own run signal, paired with a pressure or airflow check.

Compared with pressure and airflow proving

A differential pressure switch across a fan senses the pressure the fan creates. A maker article says current switches avoid cutting duct and running pickup tubing, while pressure switches need periodic service and may lack sensitivity on low-airflow equipment. Our own rule of thumb: current for simple motor-run proof, pressure when air must actually be moving, an airflow measuring station when you need the value. Water systems use the same logic; see the proof-of-flow lesson.

Using it in the BAS

Wire the status to a digital input or an analog input and compare it with the command. Allow a start delay before alarming, because the motor needs time to come up to speed. The delay of 30 to 60 s is our own placeholder; use the sequence of operations. A fan left in Hand can show status with no BAS command; see the hand-off-auto lesson, and the checkout lesson for scaling. Example, again our own assumed range of 0 to 20 A: on a 4 to 20 mA output, mA = 4 + 16 × (amps ÷ 20). So 14 A reads 15.2 mA, 6 A reads 8.8 mA, and the 10 A trip is 12.0 mA. On 0 to 10 V, 14 A is 7.0 V, 6 A is 3.0 V and 10 A is 5.0 V.

Field card: Current switches and transducers for fan and pump status

Current proves load, not airflow. Set the trip between unloaded and normal.

  • Clamp one insulated motor wire; split-core for retrofit, solid-core for new work.
  • Measure unloaded and normal amps first, then set the trip between them.
  • Our example: 6 A unloaded, 14 A normal, 10 A trip.
  • Sensor minimum above motor current: pass the wire through the window N times; sensed = N × actual.
  • VFD: sensor goes on the load side; low speed hides a belt break.
  • Use pressure or airflow proof when blocked air must be caught.
