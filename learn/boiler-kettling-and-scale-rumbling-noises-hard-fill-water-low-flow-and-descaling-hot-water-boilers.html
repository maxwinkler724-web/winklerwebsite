Boiler Kettling and Scale: Rumbling, Hard Fill Water, Low Flow and Descaling

Last updated: 2026-10-29

A hot-water boiler that rumbles, ticks or sounds like a kettle on the stove is telling you that water is boiling in a small spot where it should not. Scale, low flow and fresh fill water are the usual suspects, and they often work together.

What the sound is

Kettling is localized boiling. The burner puts heat into the metal faster than the water touching that metal can carry it away. A thin film of water flashes to steam, the steam bubble collapses against cooler water, and the collapse makes the rumble, pop or whistle. A trade-press article on reading boiler sounds describes kettling the same way: the boiler cannot pass heat into the water fast enough, and the water is not carried away before it overheats. That article names insufficient flow through the boiler as the most common cause and fouling inside the heat exchanger as a less common but more serious one. For the other noises a heating system makes, see Furnace, AC and Boiler Noises: What Each Sound Means.

Why scale makes it worse

Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium. The USGS water science page says heated hard water can leave solid calcium carbonate deposits that shorten equipment life and raise heating costs. A U.S. Department of Energy steam tip sheet (cited as plain text below) says scale conducts heat about an order of magnitude less well than steel, that even a thin layer insulates the tube, and that the insulated metal runs hotter and can fail. That tip sheet is written for steam boilers, so we use it for the physics, not for hot-water numbers.

Look at the diagram. Heat moves from the flame side, through the metal, through any scale, into the water. Scale adds a layer the heat must push through, so for the same heat flow the metal on the flame side has to get hotter. Where the scale is thickest the metal is hottest, and that spot can pass the boiling point of the thin water film next to it even when the bulk water temperature looks normal. That is where the bubble forms.

Why low-mass heat exchangers are sensitive

This part is our own reasoning from the physics, not a quoted claim. A cast-iron boiler holds a lot of water and metal, so it rides through a short flow upset. A modern low-mass or condensing heat exchanger holds very little water and is built with narrow passages, so it has to rely on steady flow to keep the metal cool. Less water volume means less buffer, and narrow passages mean a small scale deposit or a partial blockage has a bigger effect on the flow and on the hot spot. If flow drops, the water in the passage heats quickly and kettling starts.

Common causes

  • Hard fill water. Minerals come in with every gallon. A combination boiler installation manual we read lists a heating-loop hardness band of 1 to 9 grains per gallon and warns that hardness and high dissolved solids can scale the hottest part of the heat exchanger, adding that repeated or uncontrolled fills raise the risk.
  • Repeated fresh makeup. A weeping fitting, a relief valve that discharges, or a failing expansion tank all make the fill valve add new water. Each gallon brings new minerals and new oxygen. A trade-press flushing article says leaks can bring in oxygenated water and shift the pH.
  • Low flow. The trade-press sounds article lists a pump installed backward, closed isolation valves, a broken impeller, debris, or an undersized pump. Air in the pump or the heat exchanger acts the same way, because the pump cannot move water it is not wetted by. A pump that is wrong for the boiler, or that is off while the burner fires, leaves the heat with nowhere to go.
  • Fouling inside the exchanger. Flux, sludge and old scale all reduce heat transfer, so the rated pump flow no longer keeps up, according to the same article.

Check delta-T and flow first

Before you touch chemicals, confirm flow. The combination boiler manual above states a minimum boiler flow in gallons per minute, targets a 20 to 30 degree F temperature rise across the heat exchanger at high fire, and says to verify the rise at both high and low flow. Your boiler's manual sets the real numbers. A rise well above the target at a steady firing rate points to low flow or a fouled exchanger. Use the boiler's display or a flow meter if it has one, and read supply and return temperatures together. The trade-press article also suggests feeling the inlet and outlet pipes when no meter is available, carefully, because the pipes can be hot. Also remember that short burner runs can hide the pattern; see Hot-Water Boiler Short Cycling: Oversizing, Zoning and Fixes.

Fill water limits and testing

Boiler manuals list limits for hardness, total dissolved solids, pH, chlorides, iron and conductivity. The combination boiler manual we read gives, for the heating loop, hardness 1 to 9 grains per gallon, total dissolved solids 10 to 150 ppm, pH 6.6 to 8.5 and chloride below 125 mg/L. Those are one maker's limits for one model; yours may differ. Test the fill water, not just the loop. Boiler Water Tests explains the tests, and the manual may also say how to handle a loop that needs treatment. The same manual says a fill valve set at 12 psig or more is acceptable only where the water is treated and filtered and suits a closed hydronic loop, and it suggests closing the fill valve after filling to reduce leak risk. For the valve itself see Boiler Fill Valve and Backflow Preventer.

Worked example (our own example)

These inputs are assumptions we chose to show the arithmetic: a 40 gallon system, fill water at 15 grains per gallon, and a slow leak that adds 0.25 gallon of makeup each day. The conversion is 1 grain per gallon = 17.1 mg/L (Montana State University Extension water quality fact sheet), and 1 U.S. gallon = 3.785 L (NIST). Hardness in mg/L as calcium carbonate is the same as ppm.

  1. Hardness. 15 gpg × 17.1 = 256.5 mg/L, or 256.5 ppm as calcium carbonate. That is above the 9 gpg upper limit quoted from the manual (9 × 17.1 = 153.9 ppm).
  2. System volume. 40 gal × 3.785 L/gal = 151.4 L.
  3. One full refill. 151.4 L × 256.5 mg/L = 38,838 mg, about 38.8 g of hardness as calcium carbonate.
  4. Daily leak. 0.25 gal × 3.785 = 0.946 L per day. 0.946 L × 256.5 mg/L = 243 mg per day.
  5. One year of leak. 243 mg × 365 = about 88,600 mg, or 88.6 g (roughly 0.2 lb). That is 91.25 gallons of fresh water, or 2.3 system volumes.

So in this example a slow leak brings in about 2.3 times as much hardness per year as one full refill. Even at 0.25 gallon a day, the water you cannot see leaving is quietly refilling the scale source. If the fill water met the 9 gpg limit, the same figures would be 23.3 g for a refill and 53.2 g for the year. These are upper bounds on the hardness arriving. Not all of it deposits, and the real amount depends on the water chemistry, the temperature and the flow. The point is the comparison: many small fills can bring in more mineral than a single careful fill, and each one also brings oxygen. Fixing the leak is as important as treating the water.

Treating, filling and finding the leak

  • Fill with conditioned water. If the supply is hard, follow the boiler manual: softened or treated water within its limits, an approved inhibitor, or propylene glycol where the manual allows it. The manual we read allows only non-toxic propylene glycol and says a 50:50 mix needs about 10 percent more flow and about 20 percent more head than water. Do not assume a softener helps every boiler; check the manual's limits on both too hard and too soft.
  • Find the leak. Watch the system pressure over a day with the fill valve closed. If it drops, look at fittings, the relief valve outlet, air vent caps and the expansion tank. A water meter reading before and after, or a note of how often the fill valve runs, shows how much new water is arriving. Fix leaks before you refill.
  • Flush and descale. The trade-press article on kettling says flushing with water or an approved cleaner may reduce a fouled exchanger, and that multi-path exchangers can be hard to clear because the flush takes the open paths; backflushing may help, and the maker should be consulted. A trade-press flushing article warns that leaving cleaner in too long can damage pipes and the heat exchanger. Use only a cleaner the boiler maker approves, and flush it out completely. The condensing boiler lesson linked below covers the maintenance steps for the heat exchanger itself.
  • Refill and bleed. The manual says to flush and charge the piping completely and bleed all air. Then re-check the temperature rise.

If the exchanger is badly scaled or the metal has already been overheated, cleaning may not restore it. Replacement is a decision for the maker's guidance and your judgment on site.

Field card: Boiler Kettling and Scale

Kettling is boiling in a hot spot. Check flow, water and leaks.

  • The rumble is steam bubbles forming and collapsing where metal is too hot for the water flow.
  • Scale insulates, so the metal behind it runs hotter and boils the water film first.
  • Check pump, valves, air and delta-T at high and low flow before anything else.
  • Read the maker's fill water limits and test the fill water.
  • Every gallon of makeup brings minerals and oxygen. Find the leak.
  • Flush or descale only with what the maker approves, then bleed all air.
  • Our own example: 15 gpg is 256.5 ppm; a 0.25 gal/day leak adds about 88.6 g of hardness a year, 2.3 times one 40 gal refill.
