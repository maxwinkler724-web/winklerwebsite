Soft Soldering Copper Pipe for Hot-Water Heating and Water Lines

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Most copper pipe in a house or small building is joined by soldering, not brazing. The method looks simple: clean it, flux it, heat it, touch the solder to it. But every step has a reason, and the biggest mistake, trying to solder a pipe that still holds water, can be explained with arithmetic. This lesson covers the steps and then works that arithmetic with our own example.

Soldering versus brazing

The two processes are the same idea at different temperatures. A trade-association page on the subject says the dividing line is 842°F (450°C), often rounded to 840°F. A filler metal that melts below that line is soldering; above it is brazing. A university lab handout gives the same rounded 840°F line. The same association page puts brazing temperatures at roughly 1,150°F to 1,550°F. A copper-industry data sheet says most soldering is done from about 350°F to 600°F, and that soldered joints are generally used where service temperatures do not exceed 250°F. A copper tube handbook from the same industry says continuous service above 250°F calls for brazing filler metals instead.

So soldering is the usual choice for domestic water and for ordinary hot-water heating piping, and brazing is the usual choice for refrigerant lines and for hotter or higher-stress service. Refrigerant joints are covered in Brazing Copper Refrigerant Lines and the torch itself in Brazing Torch Setups for HVAC. We do not repeat them here.

Lead-free solder for drinking-water lines

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency explains the federal rule in plain terms. For solder and flux, "lead free" means no more than 0.2 percent lead. For pipes, fittings and fixtures it is a weighted average of 0.25 percent lead across the surfaces that touch water. The rule covers installation or repair of a public water system or of plumbing that provides water for human consumption. The agency page says devices used only for non-drinking purposes, such as manufacturing or irrigation, are exempt. A copper tube handbook says the same about older solders: those with more than 0.2 percent lead are banned for drinking-water systems.

This is general education, not legal advice. A hot-water heating loop and a domestic water line can share a boiler room, and a heating loop can be fed from the drinking-water supply. If you are not sure which rule applies to a line, ask the authority that inspects the work, and read the solder and flux labels before you buy.

Prepare the joint

A copper-industry data sheet lists the sequence: measure and cut, ream, clean, flux, assemble and support, heat, apply solder, cool and clean, test. In our words:

  • Cut square. A tube cut at an angle will not seat to the bottom of the fitting cup, and a short tube may not reach far enough in for a sound joint.
  • Ream. Remove the burr inside and outside the cut end. The data sheet says to ream to the full inside diameter of the tube.
  • Clean. The data sheet calls removing all oxides and surface soil from the tube end and the inside of the cup "crucial" for solder flow. Abrade the surfaces without removing too much copper, and do not touch the cleaned metal with bare hands or oily gloves.
  • Flux. Brush a thin, even coat on both tube and fitting soon after cleaning. The data sheet warns that extra flux can leave residue that causes corrosion, and the tube handbook says some "self-cleaning" fluxes carry a risk in use.
  • Support. Seat the tube fully and hold the assembly so the gap stays even all the way around. Uneven spacing can lead to cracked solder.

Heat the fitting, not the solder

The data sheet says to preheat the tube first, then move the flame to the fitting and sweep it between the cup and the tube. It also says not to overheat the joint or aim the flame into the face of the fitting cup, because overheating can burn the flux. Touch the solder to the joint once the assembly is hot enough. On a horizontal joint, it says to start at the bottom and work around with overlapping passes.

The idea behind it is that the metal has to bring the solder to its melting point, not the flame. If the solder melts from flame contact alone, the copper may not be hot enough for the solder to flow into the joint. Let the hot fitting melt the solder and the solder will be drawn in.

Capillary fill, cooling and wiping

Solder is pulled into the joint by capillary action. The data sheet says that works best when the gap between the surfaces is between 0.002 and 0.005 inch. A trade-association page says a soldered joint is satisfactory at 70 percent fill, meaning no more than 30 percent voids, and that the solder should flow toward the back of the cup. Wipe excess flux from the outside of the joint with a cotton rag while it is still warm, and let it cool naturally. The data sheet warns that shock cooling with water may stress or crack the joint.

Water in the line

The tube handbook says plainly that soldering under wet conditions can be very difficult. It suggests flared, press-connect or push-connect fittings where wet conditions cannot be avoided. The practical rule is to isolate the section, open a drain and a vent, and get the line fully drained before you light a torch. The worked example below shows why.

Valves and heat-sensitive parts

Valves, unions and other parts with seats, seals or electronic bodies can be damaged by heat. We have not found a federal or university source that gives a rule for protecting them, so we do not teach a shortcut here. Follow the installation instructions that come with the valve, and ask your supervisor if the instructions are missing. If the instructions call for a different joint type, use it.

Fire safety and hot work

A soldering torch is hot work. The federal workplace rule for welding, cutting and brazing, 29 CFR 1910.252, says combustibles should be moved at least 35 feet from the work where practicable. If they cannot be moved, they must be protected with flameproof covers or metal guards or curtains, and a fire watch is required when appreciable combustibles are closer than 35 feet. A fire watch continues for at least a half hour after the work ends, and suitable extinguishing equipment must be ready for instant use. Cutting or welding is prohibited in a sprinklered building while that protection is impaired. Wear eye protection; see Eye and Face Protection for HVAC Techs. Your site may also have its own hot-work permit.

Testing the joint under pressure

The data sheet says to test all completed assemblies for joint integrity under the procedures required by the applicable codes. A federal agency master specification for hydronic piping describes a hydrostatic test at 1.5 times design pressure, or an operating test at design pressure and design maximum temperature, with every joint inspected and corrections made. Your job's specification sets the real numbers. Details are in New Hydronic and Chilled-Water Piping: Hydrostatic Testing, Flushing and Pre-Startup Cleaning.

Our own worked example: why a wet line will not take solder

Our own example, with stated assumptions. Take 10 feet of 3/4-inch nominal Type L copper tube.

  • Tube weight: 0.455 lb per foot, from a trade-association copper tube handbook table. Ten feet weighs 0.455 × 10 = 4.55 lb.
  • Inside diameter: 0.785 inch, same table. Water content in that table: 0.0251 gallon per foot.
  • Specific heat of copper: 0.092 cal/g°C from a university physics lab table, which is numerically 0.092 Btu/lb°F. Water is 1.0.
  • Assumption: the room is 70°F and the soldering temperature is 450°F (inside the 350°F to 600°F range above), so the temperature rise is 380°F.
  • Assumption: water weighs 8.34 lb per gallon, and the line is completely full.
  • Heat of vaporization of water at 100°C: 2,256 kJ/kg from a federal standards laboratory saturated-water table. Dividing by 2.326 kJ/kg per Btu/lb gives about 970 Btu/lb.

Dry line. Copper heat = 4.55 lb × 0.092 × 380°F = about 159 Btu.

Wet line. Water per foot = 0.0251 gal × 8.34 = 0.209 lb, so 10 feet holds about 2.1 lb. The inside-diameter check agrees: a 0.785 inch bore has an area of 0.484 square inch, or 5.81 cubic inches per foot, which is the same 0.0251 gallon.

  • Warming the water from 70°F to 212°F: 2.1 lb × 1.0 × 142°F = about 298 Btu.
  • Boiling all of it: 2.1 lb × 970 Btu/lb = about 2,037 Btu.
  • Copper plus water to boiling and gone: 159 + 298 + 2,037 = about 2,494 Btu.

Comparison. Just warming the water to 212°F takes about twice the heat the copper needs (298 versus 159). Boiling it all would take about 16 times the dry-line heat (2,494 versus 159). A real joint is not this simple: only the water near the fitting matters at first, heat also leaks into the rest of the line, and flowing or sloshing water carries it away. Still, the numbers show why the metal stays below solder temperature while water is present, and why a torch aimed at a wet fitting makes steam instead of a joint. Steam pressure in a closed, partly drained line is another reason to drain and vent first.

Field card: Soft Soldering Copper Pipe

Dry, clean, fluxed, heat the fitting, let it cool.

  • Soldering is below about 840°F; brazing is above it.
  • Drinking-water lines need lead-free solder and flux: no more than 0.2 percent lead.
  • Cut square, ream, clean to bright metal, thin coat of flux.
  • Heat the tube and fitting, then touch the solder to the joint.
  • Drain and vent the line first. Water takes far more heat than the copper.
  • Follow valve instructions. Do not shock-cool a hot joint.
  • Move combustibles 35 feet, keep an extinguisher ready, and watch for a half hour after.
  • Test under pressure the way the job specification says.
