Sizing a Tank Water Heater for Your Home: First-Hour Rating vs Gallons, Peak-Hour Demand, Uniform Energy Factor and the EnergyGuide Label

Last updated: 2026-10-16

The number on the front of a tank, 40 gallons or 50 gallons, is not the number that tells you whether a family will run out of hot water. The number that does is the first-hour rating. This lesson shows how to estimate your own busiest hour, compare it with the rating on the yellow label, and decide whether a bigger tank, a different type or a mixing valve fits better.

Two gallon numbers that mean different things

The rated storage volume is how much water the tank holds. The first-hour rating, often shortened to FHR, is the Department of Energy's estimate of the most hot water the heater can deliver in one hour, starting from a full tank of heated water. The federal test method in 10 CFR Part 430, Subpart B, Appendix E defines it that way. In that test the lab pulls water at 3 gallons per minute (for tanks of 20 gallons or more) and ends each draw once the outlet temperature falls 15 degrees F below that draw's peak. The test then keeps drawing for the hour while the burner or element recovers some of the heat. Supply water in the test is about 58 degrees F and the delivery target is about 125 degrees F.

So a rating combines two things: the hot water already stored and how fast the heater makes more. A University of Georgia extension publication notes that most units have a first-hour rating larger than the tank volume, and that gas storage heaters generally rate higher than electric ones of the same tank size. A Washington State University energy program publication says the same of electric tanks: the rating is usually a little higher than capacity. The Department of Energy's sizing page tells shoppers to choose a model whose first-hour rating is at least their peak-hour demand. Our no-hot-water checks cover the case where an existing heater has stopped keeping up for a repair reason rather than a sizing one.

Estimate your peak hour

The Department of Energy worksheet asks you to pick the time of day your household uses the most hot water, morning, noon or evening, and add up the gallons used in that one hour. It is not a daily total. Its average per-use figures are below.

UseAverage gallons per use (DOE worksheet)
Shower20
Shaving2
Hand dishwashing or food prep3
Automatic dishwasher7
Clothes washer, top-loader25
Clothes washer, horizontal-axis15

Walk through your own busiest weekday morning or evening and count real events inside a single hour. Do not count the dishwasher run that happens after dinner if the showers are all done by then. The worksheet's own example, three showers, one shave and one hand-washing session, comes to 66 gallons, so it calls for a rating of at least 66.

Worked example (our own): a family of four

This is our own example, not a recommendation for any home. Assumptions: a busy weekday-morning hour with two showers, one load in a top-loading washer and one dishwasher run. We use the DOE per-use figures above and leave out sink use, which would add a few gallons.

  • Two showers: 2 × 20 = 40 gallons
  • One top-loader laundry load: 1 × 25 = 25 gallons
  • One dishwasher run: 1 × 7 = 7 gallons
  • Peak-hour demand: 40 + 25 + 7 = 72 gallons

Now compare two assumed tanks. Both ratings are made up for this example; real ones are on the label or the maker's spec sheet.

  • Tank A, 40 gallons, assumed first-hour rating 53. Shortfall: 72 − 53 = 19 gallons.
  • Tank B, 50 gallons, assumed first-hour rating 77. Margin: 77 − 72 = 5 gallons.

If the household used a horizontal-axis washer, demand would be 40 + 15 + 7 = 62 gallons. Tank A would still be 9 short and Tank B would have 15 to spare. Another check: the University of Georgia publication suggests roughly 65 to 75 gallons of first-hour rating for four people, and 72 sits inside that range. Note what "short" means in practice. The last person in line gets lukewarm water, or everyone waits while the heater recovers. It is not a safety failure, just a comfort one.

Uniform Energy Factor, draw pattern bins and the yellow label

Efficiency is a separate question from size. Appendix E defines the Uniform Energy Factor, UEF, as the measure of a water heater's overall efficiency. It comes from a 24-hour simulated-use test in which the lab draws a fixed schedule of water at set times and measures the energy used. The schedule depends on the heater's first-hour rating, which sorts it into one of four draw pattern bins:

First-hour rating (gallons)Draw patternWater drawn per test day (gallons)
Under 18Very small10
18 to under 51Low38
51 to under 75Medium55
75 and upHigh84

The daily volumes are the sums of the draw tables in the appendix. Two lessons follow. First, a bin is a test category, not a promise about your house. Second, compare UEF values within the same bin, because a heater in the high bin is tested with far more water and the federal ENERGY STAR criteria for gas storage tanks list a different UEF floor for medium and high patterns. In our example, Tank A (53) falls in the medium bin and Tank B (77) in the high bin.

The yellow EnergyGuide label comes from the Federal Trade Commission's Energy Labeling Rule, 16 CFR Part 305. For a storage water heater the rule says capacity is shown as both rated storage volume and first-hour rating. The label also gives an estimated yearly energy cost based on a national average fuel price, with a cost range for similar models that use the same fuel and a similar first-hour rating. The University of Georgia publication describes it the same way: look at the first-hour rating and the yearly operating cost, and compare against a similar size. Treat the dollar figure as a comparison tool, not a bill forecast. We could not confirm from the rule text we read whether the UEF itself is printed on the label, so look for it on the unit's data plate or the maker's spec sheet.

Recovery rate: gas versus electric

Recovery is how many gallons the heater can raise by a set temperature in an hour. Here is our own arithmetic, with assumed inputs. Use the test's 67 degree F rise (125 minus 58). One gallon of water weighs about 8.34 pounds and each pound needs about 1 Btu per degree, so one gallon needs 8.34 × 67 = about 559 Btu.

  • Electric, assumed 4.5 kW element heating at a time: 4,500 W × 3.412 = 15,354 Btu per hour. At close to 100 percent conversion, 15,354 ÷ 559 = about 27 gallons per hour.
  • Gas, assumed 40,000 Btu per hour input and an assumed 76 percent recovery efficiency: 40,000 × 0.76 = 30,400 Btu per hour. 30,400 ÷ 559 = about 54 gallons per hour.

That is why gas tanks of the same size usually post higher first-hour ratings. It also hints at why Tank A could reach 53 at all: its stored hot water plus about 27 recovered gallons. The rating comes out lower than tank volume plus recovery, in our reading, because each test draw stops when the outlet cools 15 degrees, so not every stored gallon counts as hot.

When to go bigger, add a mixing valve or switch type

  • Bigger tank. If your peak hour beats the rating, move up a size or pick a model in a higher bin. For electric heaters, the Washington State University publication says a larger tank is usually the better choice and that raising wattage helps only marginally. The Georgia publication also warns against buying more tank than you need, since heating extra water costs energy.
  • Mixing valve. A tempering valve lets the tank run hotter while the tap stays at a safer temperature, so each stored gallon is stretched with cold water. That is our own reasoning, and the trade-offs, including scald and Legionella considerations, are in the temperature lesson. Do not raise a setpoint just to solve a sizing problem.
  • Different type. A heat pump model changes space, noise and condensate needs; see that lesson. A tankless unit is sized by flow and temperature rise instead of storage; the Georgia publication gives gas units about 5 gallons per minute and electric about 2 to 3, and the tankless problems lesson covers what goes wrong. If a boiler is already in the house, an indirect tank is another way to get a large first-hour rating.

Whatever you choose, check the vent, gas, electrical and space limits, and see whether a closed system needs an expansion tank. Sizing gets the water; installation details keep it safe.

Field card: Sizing a Tank Water Heater

Match the first-hour rating to your busiest hour, not the tank label.

  • Tank gallons = storage. First-hour rating = hot water in the first hour, tank plus recovery.
  • Peak hour = sum of real uses in your busiest single hour (DOE: shower 20, dishwasher 7, top-loader 25, H-axis 15 gallons).
  • Our example: 40 + 25 + 7 = 72 gallons needed; assumed ratings 53 (short) and 77 (clear).
  • Draw pattern bins by rating: under 18, 18 to under 51, 51 to under 75, 75 and up.
  • Compare UEF only within the same bin.
  • The yellow label shows storage volume, first-hour rating and yearly cost with a range.
  • Gas recovers faster than electric in our arithmetic: about 54 versus 27 gallons per hour.
