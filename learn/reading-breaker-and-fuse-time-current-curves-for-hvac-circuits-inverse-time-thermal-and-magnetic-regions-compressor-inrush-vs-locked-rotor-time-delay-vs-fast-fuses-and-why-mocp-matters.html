Reading Breaker and Fuse Trip Curves for HVAC Circuits

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A compressor can pull close to its locked-rotor amps every time it starts and the breaker never notices. Hold the same current on a stalled rotor for half a minute and the breaker opens. The difference is time, and a time-current curve is the chart that shows it. This lesson teaches you to read one.

Reading a curve is not working on a panel. This lesson is for understanding. Opening an electrical panel, changing a breaker or fuse, or altering a circuit needs a qualified electrician, and you follow the equipment maker's instructions and your site rules.

What a trip curve shows

A time-current curve is drawn on log-log paper. The bottom axis is current, usually as a multiple of the device rating. The side axis is time, from a few hundredths of a second up to many minutes. Pick a current on the bottom, go up to the curve, and read across to find how long the device takes to open. A federal facilities maintenance manual on power breakers notes that makers publish these curves for their breakers, and says the maker's data takes precedence over any general table.

Two things surprise new techs. First, a curve is usually a band, not a single line, because devices vary. The lower edge is the fastest a unit might trip and the upper edge is the slowest. A federal test reference lists trip-time ranges with a tolerance of plus or minus 20 percent, so a range is normal. Second, both axes are logarithmic, so small distances on the chart can mean big changes in time.

Inverse time: more current, less waiting

A molded-case breaker has two trip mechanisms, described in the federal maintenance manual. The first is an inverse-time trip. The word inverse means that as current goes up, the time to trip goes down. Inside, a bimetallic strip sits in the current path. Current above the breaker's continuous rating heats the strip until it bends and trips the breaker. A bigger overload heats the strip faster, so it trips sooner. This is the thermal region of the curve, the sloping part on the left.

A second federal test reference says the strip supplies the time delay and sets the tripping characteristics. That delay is what lets the breaker ignore a brief surge.

The magnetic region: no waiting

The second mechanism is magnetic, also called instantaneous. An electromagnet in the current path pulls a trip bar when current is very high, as in a short circuit or ground fault. The manual explains that fault current is far higher than overload current, so the breaker has to open much faster. On the curve this is the flat drop at the right, where the line falls almost straight down. The manual's example is a 70 A fixed-trip breaker whose instantaneous pickup is roughly 10 to 12 times its rating, or 700 to 840 A. The test reference adds that some breakers let a technician adjust the pickup, to about 5 to 10 times the continuous rating. A typical branch-circuit breaker on a condensing unit is fixed.

Why start inrush rides through

When a compressor motor starts, current jumps to roughly the locked-rotor value on the nameplate (see Reading an HVAC Nameplate: MCA, MOCP, RLA and LRA), then falls as the rotor speeds up. A healthy start lasts a fraction of a second. The thermal strip needs seconds at that current to warm up, so a short surge uses only a sliver of its trip time. A locked rotor is different. The rotor never speeds up, so current stays high, the strip keeps heating and the breaker opens somewhere inside its band. The surge is allowed because it is short, not because it is small.

That is also why a hard-starting compressor that takes a long time to get going can trip a breaker that handles normal starts. If you are chasing that complaint, start with AC or Heat Pump Keeps Tripping the Breaker.

Time-delay fuses and fast fuses

Fuses have curves too. A federal facilities manual on fuses describes a dual-element, time-delay fuse as two elements in series in one cartridge. One element is fast acting for short circuits. The other is a time-delay element that tolerates brief overloads but melts under a sustained overload. The manual says time-delay fuses suit motors and transformers because they ride through starting and inrush surges while still guarding against a long overload. It recommends fast-acting fuses for very delicate parts such as semiconductors, and plain non-time-delay fuses for resistive loads or circuits without surges. It also says time-delay fuses in the common classes must hold at least 10 seconds at 500 percent of their rating. For motor loads it says those fuses are usually sized around 125 percent of full-load amps, and it advises testing the chosen fuse in the real circuit under surge conditions.

The takeaway: a fast fuse on a compressor circuit can open on a normal start, because its curve has no room for the surge. A time-delay fuse has that room. An HVAC fuse is still chosen by the equipment plate, not by general rules of thumb.

Why the nameplate MOCP matters

The maximum overcurrent protection value on the data plate is the largest breaker or fuse the equipment maker allows. Now you can see why. A larger device has a curve shifted to the right: it waits longer at the same current. The wiring and motor windings inside the unit were only evaluated for protection up to the plate value. A bigger device would let a stalled rotor or a fault run longer before opening. The related lessons cover how to read the plate and size the disconnect: AC Condenser Disconnect Switches, Fuses and Breakers: Sizing and Safety. Changing the protective device is a job for a qualified electrician, not a guess.

Worked example (our own example)

Assumptions (all our own, for teaching). A 30 A breaker on a single-phase condensing unit. The compressor plate shows RLA 18 A and LRA 96 A. The plate MOCP allows a 30 A breaker. Our example curve for that breaker holds at 1x, and trips at: 2x in 30 to 150 s, 3x in 8 to 40 s, 5x in 2.5 to 15 s, and 10x and above in under 0.02 s. A normal start lasts 0.5 s. A stall lasts until something opens.

  1. Locked-rotor multiple: 96 A divided by 30 A is 3.2x the rating.
  2. Read the band at 3.2x. Between the 3x and 5x points the band runs from about 7 s (fastest) to about 35 s (slowest). That is our interpolation on the log chart.
  3. Start: 0.5 s divided by 7 s is about 0.07, so the start uses about 7 percent of even the fastest trip time. The breaker holds.
  4. Stall: 96 A stays on the line. Somewhere between about 7 and 35 s the breaker opens. The motor windings are heating the whole time, which is why a compressor's own internal protector often acts first; see compressor internal overload protectors.
  5. Running: 18 A is 0.6x the rating, so the breaker sits far below the curve and never trips.
  6. Fast fuse comparison: a plain fast fuse with no surge allowance could open on the 0.5 s start. A time-delay fuse, per the fuse manual, holds at least 10 s at 5x, so a 0.5 s surge at 3.2x is easy for it.

Reading checklist

  • Convert amps to a multiple of the rating first.
  • Read the band, not a line, and expect plus or minus 20 percent.
  • Ask how long the current really lasts, then compare to the band.
  • If a device nuisance-trips on starts, find out why the start is slow. Do not upsize the protection.

Field card: Reading Breaker and Fuse Trip Curves for HVAC Circuits

Short surge rides through. Long overload does not.

  • Thermal region: inverse time, more current trips faster.
  • Magnetic region: very high current, trips with no deliberate delay.
  • Locked rotor on a start is short; a stalled rotor holds the current and trips.
  • Time-delay fuses tolerate starts; fast fuses may not.
  • Never exceed the plate MOCP. Panel and device changes go to a qualified electrician.
