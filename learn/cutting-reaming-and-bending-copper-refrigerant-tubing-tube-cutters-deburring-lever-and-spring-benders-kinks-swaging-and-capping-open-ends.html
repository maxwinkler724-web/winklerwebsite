Cutting, Reaming and Bending Copper Refrigerant Tubing

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A refrigerant line is only as good as the tube you hand to the brazing torch. Most field leaks and restrictions start earlier: a crushed cutter wheel, a burr left inside, a kinked bend, or an open tube that sat on the truck for a week. This lesson covers the steps before the joint is made.

Cut square, and do not over-tighten the cutter

A city government construction specification we read tells installers to cut copper tubing squarely with tools designed for the purpose and to avoid methods that bend the tube or flatten its walls. A wheel-type tube cutter does that when it is used gently. Our own general practice: snug the wheel on the tube, rotate the cutter once or twice around, then turn the knob only a fraction of a turn before rotating again. Cranking the knob hard squeezes the wall into an oval and rolls a thick lip inside. Soft ACR tube is thin enough that this matters. A hacksaw leaves a ragged, angled cut and chips that are hard to remove, so we avoid it on refrigerant lines.

Ream, deburr and keep chips out of the system

The same specification says to use a reamer to remove the rolled lip from the inside of the cut tube. A copper industry tube handbook (a trade association document, cited as plain text in the sources) also warns that burrs left at tube ends can cause turbulence and wear. In a refrigerant line a burr is worse than a flow problem: it can break off and travel to a metering device, a filter drier or a compressor.

Our own general practice: hold the tube end pointing down while you cut and ream, so chips fall out onto the floor instead of rolling into the line. Ream until the inside edge is smooth to a fingertip, then lightly dress the outside edge too. Tap the tube, look through it, and never ream with the open end pointing up. If chips did fall in, cut the end back or blow the line clear with dry nitrogen before you connect anything.

Bending: lever benders and spring benders

Two hand tools are common. A lever bender clamps the tube between a form and a roller and swings it through the bend; it supports the tube wall from outside and gives the cleanest result on the sizes it fits. Use the bender made for the tube size you are bending. A spring bender is a coil spring that slides over the tube to hold the walls round. It suits soft tube and gentle bends, and the spring needs to match the tube outside diameter. Bend slowly in one smooth motion and do not bend back and forth to correct a mistake, because repeated flexing hardens and cracks the wall.

Minimum bend radius, in general terms

Tube makers publish a minimum bend radius for each size and temper. The same copper tube handbook includes a bending guide table and a figure showing a lever-type hand bender; check the data sheet for the exact tube you have. The city specification gives a simple floor for coiled soft tube: no bend tighter than the radius of the coil as the maker packaged it. Our own rule of thumb: a larger radius is always safer, so if the bend looks tight for the tube, add a fitting instead.

Kinks and flattening

The city specification does not allow kinks, dents, flats or crimps, and says a damaged section must be cut out and replaced. We follow that. A kink cannot be fixed by straightening: the wall is work-hardened and thinned at the crease, and the flattened area restricts refrigerant flow. Cut out the damaged piece and join a new one in with a brazed coupling.

Swaging instead of a coupling

The copper tube handbook describes swaging as expanding the end of one tube so it can be joined to another tube by brazing. With a swaging tool or punch, the end of one tube is opened to fit over the outside of the other, so the joint is tube-in-tube. Our own reasoning: a coupling has two brazed joints and a swaged joint has one, so there is one less place to leak. Both tubes must be the same nominal size, the expanded end must be round and free of splits, and the overlap must be clean and fully heated. The brazing steps themselves are in the related lesson on brazing copper refrigerant lines.

Clean the ends and cap open tubes

The handbook lists cleaning with abrasive cloth, an abrasive pad or a fitting brush before joining. A community college chiller-project specification says to cap or plug open pipe ends during installation, to provide temporary end caps, and to clean the pipe before and after erection. That specification is about chilled and condenser water piping, not refrigerant lines, but the habit carries over. Our own practice for refrigerant lines: refrigerant oil absorbs moisture from air, and water in the system is hard to remove later, so tape, crimp or cap the ends of every open tube at lunch, overnight and whenever you stop. Keep factory caps on tubing, fittings and service valves until the moment you connect them.

Worked example (our own): tube length for a 90 degree bend

This example is our own, with assumed numbers, and ignores the small stretch of the tube wall.

  • Assumption 1: centerline bend radius R = 4 in.
  • Assumption 2: two straight legs of 10 in each, measured from the tube end to where the straight part meets the arc.

A 90 degree bend is a quarter of a circle, so the arc length is pi x R / 2.

  1. Arc = 3.1416 x 4 / 2 = 6.283 in.
  2. Tube needed = 10 + 10 + 6.283 = 26.283 in, about 26 1/4 in.

Now compare with the legs measured to the corner, where the two centerlines would meet if the tube had a sharp elbow. Each leg measured that way is the straight leg plus R: 10 + 4 = 14 in.

  1. Corner measurement = 14 + 14 = 28 in.
  2. Difference = 28 - 26.283 = 1.717 in.

The tube is about 1.7 in shorter than the corner-to-corner measurement, which equals 2R minus the arc: 8 - 6.283 = 1.717 in. If you cut to the 28 in corner total, the finished run would be 1.7 in too long. Mark the bend start and end on the tube with the bender marks, and cut a little long when you are unsure, since you can trim a leg but cannot stretch it.

Field card: Cutting, Reaming and Bending Copper Refrigerant Tubing

Cut square, ream, point down, bend wide, cap it.

  • Tighten the cutter in small steps; do not crush the wall.
  • Ream every cut, with the open end pointing down so chips fall out.
  • Use the bender or spring that matches the tube size; bend once, smoothly.
  • Do not accept kinks or flats: cut them out.
  • Swaging gives one brazed joint where a coupling gives two.
  • Clean the ends, and cap or crimp open tubes the moment you stop.
  • Bend length: arc = pi x R / 2; tube = legs + arc.
