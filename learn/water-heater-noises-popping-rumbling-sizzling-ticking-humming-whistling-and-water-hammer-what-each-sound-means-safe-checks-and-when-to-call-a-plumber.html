Water Heater Noises: What Each Sound Means and What to Check

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A tank water heater is a steel tank with a heat source, and it talks. Some sounds are harmless, some are a maintenance reminder, and a few are a warning. This lesson sorts the common ones: popping, rumbling, crackling, sizzling, ticking, humming, whistling or screaming, and banging. It then lists checks a homeowner can do safely and the point where a licensed plumber should take over.

Start with safety

Three rules come before any listening. First, hot water scalds fast. A federal consumer product safety fact sheet on tap water scalds says an adult can get a third-degree burn in about 2 seconds at 150 F, about 6 seconds at 140 F and about 30 seconds at 130 F. At 120 F the same sheet says a five-minute exposure could do it, and it urges households to set the heater to 120 F. Second, never cap, plug or block the relief valve or its discharge pipe. A federal recall notice on large water heater relief valves explains that the valve exists to let out pressure so the tank and connected valves do not fail, and that a valve that fails to do so risks rupture and burn injuries. Third, if you smell gas, leave the building, then call the gas utility or emergency services from outside. Do not flip switches, light anything or hunt for the leak. That last rule is our own plain safety rule, and it overrides every other step on this page.

Sound by sound

Popping and rumbling (gas tanks)

Minerals in the water settle out as sediment on the tank bottom. In a gas heater the burner heats the bottom of the tank, so the water trapped under the sediment boils into small steam bubbles that push up and pop. Both university extension publications we read say sediment cuts efficiency and tank capacity, and that flushing helps. The sound is a maintenance flag, not an emergency.

Crackling around the elements (electric tanks)

Electric tanks heat with elements sitting in the water, and sediment can bake onto them. Crackling or sizzling near an element as it cycles is the same sediment story as popping. Replacing an element means opening an electrical enclosure, so that is a qualified-person job.

Sizzling on a gas burner

A sizzle at the burner, like water on a hot pan, means moisture is landing on a hot surface. Two common causes are condensation (a cold tank full of cool incoming water sweats while the burner first fires; it should stop in a few minutes) and a drip from above, such as a leaking fitting or the tank itself. If the sizzle continues after warm-up, or you see rust, water on the floor or a burner flame that looks lazy or yellow, shut off the gas control per the maker and call a plumber. A leaking tank cannot be repaired. See Water Heater Leaking From the Bottom for how to tell the sources apart.

Ticking and tapping

Copper and steel pipes grow longer when hot water moves through them and shrink as they cool, so they tick against straps and framing. A university extension publication also describes heat traps, one-way valves in the hot and cold lines that keep hot water from rising out of the tank and cold water from falling in. Parts inside heat traps can also click as they open and close. Ticking after a draw or a long standby is usually harmless. A plumber can add pipe clips if it bothers you.

A hum from an electric element

An element that vibrates can hum, especially when it is loose or scaled. A hum with no other symptoms is usually tolerable, but a hum with tripped breakers or warm wiring is not. Turn off the breaker and call someone qualified.

Whistling or screaming

A thin whistle on a hot draw often means a partly closed valve, a worn washer or a scaled fixture, because water squeezed through a tight gap makes noise. A whistle or hiss from the relief valve itself is different: it means the valve is passing water or steam. That can be pressure from thermal expansion (see the example below), a failing valve, or an overheating tank. If the discharge is steam, or the tank feels very hot, stay clear, shut off the heat source at the control or breaker if you can reach it safely, and call a plumber. A relief valve discharge can be scalding; keep people and pets away from the discharge pipe.

Banging: water hammer

A bang or shudder when a washer, dishwasher or faucet shuts off quickly is water hammer. Moving water has momentum, and a sudden stop sends a pressure shock through the pipes. Loose pipes slap against framing. It is not a heater defect, but repeated shocks stress fittings. Plumbers can add arrestors or secure piping.

Our own worked example: why the relief valve may drip

Our own example. The inputs are assumptions, not measured values. Assume a 40 gallon tank, incoming water at 50 F and a setpoint of 120 F. From standard water property tables, water weighs about 62.41 pounds per cubic foot at 50 F and about 61.71 pounds per cubic foot at 120 F. We chose those values as assumptions; check them against a table before reuse.

  1. The same mass of water takes more room when warmer, so the volume ratio equals the density ratio: 62.41 divided by 61.71 = 1.0113.
  2. Expansion is 1.0113 minus 1 = 0.0113, or about 1.1 percent.
  3. New volume change for 40 gallons: 40 x 0.0113 = 0.45 gallon.

So each heat-up from 50 F to 120 F pushes about 0.45 gallon, a little less than two quarts, that has to go somewhere. In an open system it backs up into the city main. If the house has a check valve or backflow preventer on the supply, the water cannot back out. Water barely compresses, so pressure climbs quickly until the relief valve opens for a moment and drips. A dripping relief valve every time the burner or elements run is a classic sign. Fixing it usually means a properly sized expansion tank, which a plumber installs and sets to the right air charge. See Water Heater Expansion Tank Sizing, Air Charge and Closed Systems.

Safe homeowner checks

  • Look and smell first. Rust streaks, water on the floor, soot, a gas odor or a burning smell change this from a noise question to a call. Gas smell means leave and call.
  • Check the setpoint. Extension guidance says 120 F is enough for most homes and cuts water heating energy use by about 6 to 10 percent. Lower settings also reduce scalding and mineral buildup.
  • Flush a little, per the maker. Both extension publications describe opening the drain valve and letting a small amount of water run into a bucket until it clears, which the second one puts at usually 1 to 2 gallons, yearly or more often in hard water. Follow the maker's instructions first, because some tanks call for turning off gas or power before draining. Water from the bottom of a heater is hot, so use a bucket rated for it, keep a hose cap ready in case the valve will not close, and keep it low and away from skin.
  • Test the relief valve only as the maker directs. Extension guidance says to test it annually per the maker's instructions.
  • Leave the valve alone otherwise. Do not tighten, cap, plug or insulate over it, and keep its discharge pipe pointing down and open.

When to call a licensed plumber

Call a licensed plumber for a relief valve that drips often, hisses, or discharges steam; any leak from the tank itself; a tank that has run many years and now rumbles loudly; a drain valve that will not close; water hammer that keeps returning; repeated pops that continue after a flush; or any element or wiring work. Call the gas utility, from outside, for a gas smell. A flame that looks wrong, soot or a draft problem also needs a pro; see Water Heater Anode Rods for a related tank-health topic. Sounds that come from the furnace or boiler rather than the heater are covered in Furnace, AC and Boiler Noises.

Field card: Water Heater Noises: What Each Sound Means and What to Check

Pops and rumbles are sediment; a relief valve that talks is a safety device working.

  • Popping or rumbling (gas) and crackling (electric): sediment. Flush a little per the maker.
  • Sizzle at a burner: short sizzle at start-up may be condensation; a lasting one may be a leak.
  • Ticking: pipes and heat traps. Usually harmless.
  • Hum: element vibrating. Breaker trips mean a call.
  • Whistle or scream: partly open valve, or the relief valve passing water or steam.
  • Bang: water hammer when a valve shuts quickly.
  • Example: 50 F to 120 F is about 1.1 percent, so 40 gallons grows about 0.45 gallon each heat-up.
  • Never cap or plug the relief valve. Gas smell: leave and call from outside.
