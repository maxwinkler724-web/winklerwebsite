High-Efficiency Furnace Vent Pipes Blocked by Snow or Ice: What to Do

Last updated: 2026-10-16

After a snowstorm, the furnace goes quiet and the house gets cold. If yours has two white plastic pipes through the wall, check whether snow or ice has covered their ends. This lesson covers why that shuts the heat off, the carbon monoxide risk, and how to clear the pipes safely.

Check the label and the manual

Your manual decides. The numbers below come from specific installation manuals for specific models. Yours may differ.

What the two pipes are

A condensing (high-efficiency) furnace or boiler vents through plastic pipe, often out the side wall, and many use two pipes. One pulls outdoor air in for combustion. The other carries the exhaust out. Exhaust gas is the part that carries carbon monoxide (CO). For the full classification, see Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained.

Why a blocked pipe shuts the furnace off

These furnaces have a safety check built in. A small fan called the inducer pulls air through the unit, and a pressure switch confirms that air is really moving. If a pipe is plugged, the switch senses too little airflow and the control board stops the burners. A Calgary furnace service company describes it this way: a blocked intake can make the inducer cycle repeatedly with a "pressure switch failed to close" code, while a blocked exhaust can let the furnace start, run briefly and shut down again. For the technician's side of this, read Pressure Switch and Inducer Diagnosis.

So a shutdown from a snow-covered pipe is the safety system working. Find and clear the blockage; do not bypass the switch.

Why this is a carbon monoxide issue

PSE&G says to keep snow from blocking a sidewall vent, because blocked exhaust can cause heating equipment fumes, including CO, to back up. In a January 2024 release it also asks customers to keep high-efficiency furnace pipes outside the home clear of snow. The Closter Volunteer Fire Department (January 2026) notes that many newer furnaces vent through the side of the home, so drifting snow can block them. New Jersey Natural Gas asks customers to check outside vents for furnaces, water heaters and dryers because blockages can lead to CO buildup. The NFPA's CO safety sheet says to keep vents clear after snowstorms and to have fuel-burning heating equipment inspected by a professional each year before cold weather.

The federal Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) has looked at this hazard in numbers. In a 2000 letter, staff reviewed 38 incidents from 1991 to 2000 involving blocked furnace vents, with 13 deaths and 25 injuries. Those reports covered blocked vents in general, not only snow. Have working alarms; see Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do When One Sounds.

How high the pipes should end above grade or snow

Manufacturers set a minimum height for the pipe openings above the ground or the highest snow you can expect. Here is what each manual we read says, with the model or kit named so you can check.

  • Goodman/Amana. The MSS9 and CSS9 single-stage furnace instructions (IOG-2009P) say all flue and intake terminations must be at least 12 inches above ground level or the anticipated snow level (page 15). A figure on page 20 shows an alternate termination where the intake can be extended upward (snorkeled) to reach 12 inches.
  • Lennox. The SLP98UHV installation instructions (form 506610-01) show 12 inches above grade or average snow accumulation for the vent termination (Figure 20, page 17). For one wall kit, a figure says that if the pipes would be less than 12 inches above snow accumulation, field-fabricated piping must be installed (Figure 31, page 22).
  • Trane. The concentric vent kit guide for condensing furnaces (18-CH34D1-8B, model BAYAIR30AVENTA) calls for 12 inches above the highest anticipated snow level or grade, whichever is greater, on a side wall (Figure 5). On a roof, 12 inches above the highest anticipated snow level, with no more than 24 inches above the roof (Figure 3).
  • CAC/BDP vent kit. A vent terminal kit guide that names no brand (KGAVT0101BRA, copyright CAC/BDP, 2005) uses the same 12-inch figure above the highest anticipated snow level or grade, whichever is greater, and a 24-inch maximum above a roof (page 3).
  • Navien. The NFC-175/200 quick installation guide says to maintain 12 inches minimum above the highest anticipated snow level or grade, whichever is greater. The NCB combination boiler guide gives the same 12 inches for its roof installation.
  • Weil-McLain. The Ultra boiler concentric through-roof vent supplement says the air inlet must end at least 12 inches above the roof or snow line (page 7, item 5b).

Some give a larger figure for Canada. Your own manual's figure controls. In snow country, the snow level, not grade, is the number that matters.

Worked example: does this termination clear the snow?

Suppose your manual uses the Goodman or Navien rule: 12 inches above the anticipated snow level or grade. Your pipe openings are 20 inches above the ground. These snow depths are made up for the exercise.

  1. Snow depth 6 inches. Required opening height = 6 + 12 = 18 inches. The pipes are at 20 inches. 20 - 18 = 2 inches of margin. This meets the 12-inch rule.
  2. Snow depth 12 inches, or a drift that deep against the wall. Required = 12 + 12 = 24 inches. 20 - 24 = -4 inches. The openings are 4 inches short, so this wall can bury or choke the pipes.
  3. No snow: grade counts as 0. Required = 0 + 12 = 12 inches. 20 inches is fine.

A pipe that passes on bare ground can fail in a heavy storm. If yours sit short of your area's usual snow depth, talk to a licensed contractor; Lennox's wall kit calls for field-fabricated piping when pipes would be under 12 inches above snow. Changing venting is installer work.

Frost buildup versus ice that blocks

The sources we read do not call frost on a vent normal, so treat any buildup as a reason to look. Two sources describe how ice forms. The Calgary service company says ice in the intake is often caused by exhaust moisture pulled back into the intake, where it condenses and freezes over hours or days. The Filterbuy article, reviewed by a licensed technician, says condensate can drip and refreeze into an ice ring at the exhaust tip or a mound growing toward the opening, and that frost on the intake opening may mean exhaust vapor is blowing back in.

How to clear the pipes safely

  1. Check your CO alarm first. If it is sounding, leave the house and call 911 from outside.
  2. Turn the thermostat to off. (Filterbuy gives this step.)
  3. Clear snow away from both pipes by hand. NJ Natural Gas recommends gloved hands or a broom and advises against shovels, sharp tools or force, which can damage equipment.
  4. If ice is stuck at the opening, remove it gently by hand. Do not hit the pipe, use a heat gun or open flame, or pour hot water on it (Filterbuy). The Calgary company says not to break ice frozen solid; call a technician.
  5. Turn the thermostat back on and confirm the burners light.

Preventing drifts and ice

  • Keep the wall area around both pipes clear, and look at them after every storm (PSE&G and the Closter fire department both advise regular checks).
  • Do not add screens, wire or perforated covers to the pipe ends. Lennox warns in its venting practices (page 14) that they cause freeze-ups and can block the terminations.
  • Do not cut away or modify the termination parts. Trane warns that leaving the ribs on a vertical rain cap can let ice or snow form on the cap and leave the furnace inoperable.
  • Keep plants and walls out of the exhaust path; Weil-McLain says winds can freeze condensate where flue products hit them (item 4c).
  • Condensate drains can freeze too. Carrier's 59TP6A manual requires a condensate freeze protection kit where temperatures may fall below 32 degrees F (page 17). See Condensate Pump Not Working: High-Efficiency Furnace and AC.

When to call

  • Any CO alarm: leave and call 911. NJNG also lists the NJ Poison Control Center (800-222-1222) and its own emergency line (800-221-0051).
  • Ice inside the pipe, or a vent that exits through the roof (Filterbuy says these need a licensed technician).
  • The furnace locks out again within a day, a pipe is cracked or loose, or you smell exhaust or see soot.
  • The pipes end too low for your snow depth.

Field card: Snow-Blocked Furnace Vent Pipes

Two white pipes, keep both open and above the snow.

  • Blocked intake or exhaust trips the pressure switch, so the furnace stops. That is the safety working.
  • Blocked exhaust is a CO risk. CO alarm sounding: leave, call 911.
  • Manuals read: 12 in. above grade or highest anticipated snow, whichever is greater. Your manual decides.
  • Opening height needed = snow depth + 12 in. (example: 12 + 12 = 24 in.).
  • Clear by hand or broom. No shovel, hammer, heat gun or hot water.
  • Ice inside the pipe, roof vents, or repeat lockouts: call a licensed technician.
