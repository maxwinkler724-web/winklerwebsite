Aquarium and Fish Room HVAC: Evaporation, Humidity, Chillers and Backup

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A room full of open water is a humidifier you cannot turn off. Public aquariums, pet store fish rooms and research fish rooms all share the same HVAC problem: the water surface keeps adding moisture, the tanks need a steady temperature, and the fish depend on equipment that must not stop. This lesson walks through the moisture load with real arithmetic, then the chiller, corrosion and backup power questions.

Evaporation becomes a latent load

Every pound of water that leaves a tank surface carries heat of vaporization into the air as latent load. The EnergyPlus engineering reference gives a public swimming pool equation in inch-pound units: evaporation in lb/h equals 0.1 times the water surface area in square feet, times an activity factor, times the difference between the saturation vapor pressure at the water surface and the vapor pressure at the room air dew point (both in inches of mercury). That reference lists activity factors from about 0.5 for quiet recreational pools up to 1.0 for public pools and spas. It also says the latent load equals the evaporation rate times the heat of vaporization.

The pool equation is a stand-in for tanks, not a fish room rule. A lidded or covered tank evaporates less than a swimming pool, and splashing, aeration and spray bars evaporate more. The University of Missouri extension building guide for recirculating aquaculture makes the same point from field data: in its example of 100,000 gallons with 4,000 square feet of exposed water, evaporation accounted for 38,000 of the 45,200 Btu/h needed to maintain water temperature, and covering tanks cut the heating need by up to about 30 percent. Covers are the cheapest moisture control there is. The wider pool story, including chloramines, is in the indoor pool dehumidification lesson, so we do not repeat it here.

Worked example: a small fish room

Assumptions (ours, not from a source): 400 sq ft of total open water surface across all tanks and sumps; water at 78°F; room air at 78°F and 55 percent relative humidity; an activity factor of 0.5, chosen because the tanks are quiet and partly covered. Change any of these and the answer changes.

  1. Vapor pressures (our own psychrometric calculation). Saturation at 78°F is about 0.965 in. Hg. At 55 percent RH the air holds 0.55 × 0.965 = 0.531 in. Hg, which corresponds to a dew point near 60.5°F. Difference: 0.965 − 0.531 = 0.434 in. Hg.
  2. Evaporation. 0.1 × 400 × 0.5 × 0.434 = 8.7 lb/h.
  3. Latent load. Engineering Toolbox lists heat of vaporization at 70°F as 1,053.7 and at 80°F as 1,048.0 Btu/lb. Using 1,050: 8.7 × 1,050 = about 9,100 Btu/h. A range of 1,040 to 1,060 moves that by only a percent or two.
  4. Pints per day. 8.7 lb/h × 24 = about 209 lb/day. Water weighs about 8.34 lb per gallon, so one pint is about 1.04 lb. 209 ÷ 1.04 = about 200 pints/day.

That is a latent load of about 0.76 ton, and the removal figure is far above what a household portable unit does. ENERGY STAR notes that portable dehumidifiers are now rated at 65°F instead of 80°F, and that capacity falls in cooler conditions because there is less water to remove in cooler air. So a nameplate pints/day number must be checked against the rating condition, and a room this wet is usually a job for a commercial unit, outdoor air, or both. A dehumidifier also puts its compressor heat into the room, which matters when a chiller is working to keep the water cold.

Ventilation or dehumidification

The Missouri guide says recirculating aquaculture buildings are commonly advised to run 1 to 2 air changes per hour, and it gives a quick conversion: divide building volume by 60 to get cfm per air change. In its survey, most sites ran well below that, and most had winter condensation. It reports that one site at 0.30 air changes used desiccant dehumidification and that the guide judged ventilation more economical for moisture removal. Heat or energy recovery on the exhaust is suggested for at least 1 air change. Mild outdoor air makes ventilation cheap, but humid summer air does not dry anything, which is where a dehumidifier or cooling coil earns its place. See desiccant dehumidifiers and sensible heat ratio and latent load.

Research rooms add rules. The NIH Office of Research Facilities guidance on aquatic facilities says HVAC must be designed for the thermal and moisture loads of the water, that moisture can condense on walls, ceilings and tank lids, and that room air exchange is typically governed by thermal and moisture loads. It also warns that water aerosols can spread pathogens, so spray and splash should be kept low. A companion NIH bulletin says air should move from cleaner areas toward work areas, and that the HVAC system should work in tandem with the water supply in controlling room and water temperature.

Chillers and heaters for the water

A Southern Regional Aquaculture Center fact sheet lists immersion heaters, gas or electric heaters, heat exchangers, chillers and heat pumps as ways to control tank temperature, and says changes over 5°F should be made gradually, about 1°F every 20 to 30 minutes. A North Carolina State University paper describes a research system that used electric immersion heaters for heating and a chiller with a heat exchanger for cooling, holding 16 tanks within plus or minus 0.4°C in fixed tests. Its authors also note the design needed an additional chiller to cool the water in some of the daily-swing tests. The Missouri guide shows how big the heating side can be: it estimates about 350,000 Btu/h to heat 100,000 gallons from 50°F to 85°F in three days.

For chiller flow, delta-T and loop basics, use the process chiller lesson. Two fish room twists: the chiller and heater are tied to live animals, so a control fault is an animal welfare event, and a heat pump or chiller that rejects heat into the fish room adds to the room load rather than leaving it.

Salt air and corrosion

Marine rooms put salt aerosol, splash and constant humidity near every coil, fan and electrical box. The NIH guidance says excess moisture can promote microbial growth and metal corrosion, and the companion bulletin calls for corrosion resistant, splash resistant electrical boxes and conduit with a UL wet-location listing, and water resistant ceilings, walls, sills and floors. For coil damage mechanisms and maker coating options, see the salt air and formicary corrosion lesson. A practical habit: keep a dehumidifier or air handler coil out of the splash zone, and put the major life support components outside animal rooms, as NIH suggests, so service does not mean working in the spray.

Backup power and alarms, in general terms

The SRAC fact sheet calls a backup power source an essential component of recirculating systems. It gives an example where a warmwater system at saturated oxygen fell to a stressful 3 ppm within 16 minutes at lower stocking density and under 6 minutes at higher density. It recommends an automatic transfer switch to start the generator when no one is on site, inexpensive automatic phone alarms for power or water level failures, and a pure oxygen tank with a solenoid valve for emergencies. NIH calls for generators or other emergency power that is tested regularly, and for monitoring that detects failures of all critical systems. As HVAC techs we do not design these plans, but we should ask which fans, chillers and controls are on emergency power before shutting anything down for service.

Field card: Aquarium and Fish Room HVAC

Open water is a latent load. Ask before you power anything down.

  • Evaporation (lb/h) = 0.1 × area × activity factor × (water vapor pressure minus dew point vapor pressure), in. Hg.
  • Latent Btu/h = lb/h of water × about 1,050 Btu/lb.
  • One pint of water is about 1.04 lb. Check the dehumidifier rating temperature.
  • Covers cut evaporation and heating load.
  • Ventilation is often cheaper than dehumidification when outdoor air is dry.
  • Ask what is on emergency power and who gets the alarm before service.
