Wood-Fired Pizza Ovens and Solid-Fuel Cooking Exhaust

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A gas fryer line and a wood-fired oven can sit ten feet apart in the same kitchen and still be treated as two different fire problems. Wood and charcoal burn dirty, make creosote and throw sparks, so the exhaust rules are stricter. This lesson covers why, what a tech should look for, and how to check the makeup air.

Check the local authority

Check the local authority. Fire and mechanical codes are adopted and amended locally, and the fire code official decides what applies to a given kitchen. This page is general education built from public pages read in 2026. It is not a design, not a code interpretation and not legal advice.

Why solid fuel is treated differently

The standard most fire officials use for commercial cooking exhaust is NFPA 96, which we name here in plain text only. Its Chapter 14 is written just for solid fuel cooking. A conference paper on solid-fuel testing says Chapter 14 covers exhaust, grease removal, air movement and fire-extinguishing equipment, plus procedures for inspection, cleaning and maintenance. That paper also describes creosote as a tar-like byproduct of incomplete wood burning that is highly combustible, and says it builds up faster with a poor air supply and big temperature swings in the exhaust. Grease mixed with creosote lights more easily and burns harder.

So the hazards differ from a gas griddle. Embers and sparks can travel up the duct, creosote coats the duct walls, and the fire box is a live fuel bed instead of a burner you can shut off.

What the public checklists ask for

Two Georgia city fire departments publish NFPA 96 hood checklists, and both have a solid fuel section. One says the solid fuel exhaust system "shall be separate from all other exhaust systems" and lists spark arrester requirements. The other says the same in shorter form: the exhaust system is separated from all other exhaust systems, and a spark arrester is provided. Both cite NFPA 96 section numbers, so you can ask the fire official which edition applies. The first also says wall terminations are prohibited, so the duct goes up and out through the roof. A Massachusetts fire board decision on a solid fuel grill variance quotes the same rule: wall terminations of solid fuel exhaust systems are prohibited.

On those checklists, fuel stored in the same building as the appliance goes in an area with noncombustible walls, floor and ceiling that extends 3 feet past the pile, with a portable extinguisher allowed for a small pile and sprinklers expected for a larger one. Appliances also need a noncombustible floor that extends 3 feet in each direction, and a small fire box needs a water-type extinguisher. The exact limits are in the standard, so confirm them locally.

Spark arrestors and filters

Do not reuse the grease filters from the neighboring hood. One maker letter for commercial wood-fired ovens says a Type 1 hood over a wood-fired oven needs spark-arrestor steel baffle filters and that mesh-type grease filters must never be used. The same letter gives hood dimensions and distances for its own ovens, so read the manual for the oven in front of you rather than relying on a general rule.

Creosote and cleaning

Two public agency pages agree on the interval. Portland Fire and Rescue's policy sets cleaning for systems using solid cooking fuels at every month, and says unusually heavy buildup may need more. The Massachusetts commercial cooking exhaust inspection page lists inspection of solid fuel systems as monthly. In the Massachusetts fire board decision, the applicant proposed weekly inspections at first to learn how fast buildup occurs, with duct cleaning down to bare metal. For the duct and fan side, see Restaurant Kitchen Exhaust Fans, Upblast Fans, Grease Ducts, Hinge Kits and Cleaning.

Two ways the exhaust can be built

The maker letter describes two options for its oven line. In the first, an 8 inch inside diameter UL 103 HT or grease-duct chimney connects directly to the oven's vent, with no fan. The maker recommends against forced air in that setup and says forced air, or bends over 30 degrees, raise the risk of a chimney fire and would call for a Type 1 hood. In the second, a Type 1 hood sits over the oven opening with a steel-baffle filter, a stated duct velocity of 1,500 to 2,500 feet per minute and a stated airflow of 800 to 1,000 cfm. A rooftop fan belongs to the hood option, which is why our drawing shows one. The maker also calls frequent, thorough flue cleaning the best way to reduce flue fires. A second maker's installation instructions give clearances to combustibles, warn against packing the air spaces with insulation, and say to burn only seasoned, untreated wood.

Makeup air and drafting

An oven only drafts well when the room is not pulled below outdoor pressure. Exhaust fans take air out of the building, and that air has to come back in somewhere. The installation instructions we read, for indoor use, say provisions must be made to supply sufficient combustion makeup air and that combustion air must follow local building codes. They give no airflow figure, so the number comes from the design. Our companion lessons cover the idea in Commercial Kitchen Ventilation: Hoods and Make-Up Air Basics and the equipment in Make-Up Air Units: Direct-Fired vs Indirect-Fired.

Worked example: our own makeup air check

This is a study estimate, not a design. We state every assumption.

  1. Oven exhaust (cited): the maker letter gives 800 to 1,000 cfm for its Type 1 hood option. We use the top of that range, 1,000 cfm.
  2. Grease hood exhaust (assumption): 2,000 cfm. We picked this for the example and it is not from a source. On a real job use the hood label or balance report.
  3. Total exhaust: 1,000 + 2,000 = 3,000 cfm.
  4. Makeup air needed (assumption): we assume a makeup air unit replaces all of it, so 3,000 cfm. Real jobs may take part from transfer air, but every cfm not replaced outdoors is pulled from somewhere else in the building.
  5. Winter delta T (assumption): outdoor air at 15°F heated to a 65°F supply, so delta T = 65 − 15 = 50°F.
  6. Heating load: the sensible heat formula is 1.08 × cfm × delta T. ACHR News builds the 1.08 as 60 × 0.075 × 0.24, and notes it is accurate near room temperature. Then 1.08 × 3,000 = 3,240, and 3,240 × 50 = 162,000 Btu/h. The oven's share alone is 1.08 × 1,000 × 50 = 54,000 Btu/h.

If the makeup air is shut off: with the fans still running, the kitchen goes into negative pressure, because air leaves faster than it comes in. We have no cited numbers for this, so we describe it only. The oven's draft weakens, so smoke and combustion gases can come out of the oven opening instead of up the duct. Doors pull hard, and replacement air sneaks in through cracks, other flues and from the dining room. CDC lists charcoal grills and fireplaces among carbon monoxide sources, so spillage is a health issue as well as a nuisance. The lesson Combustion Appliance Zone Worst-Case Depressurization and Spillage Testing shows the same logic in a house. Our advice: interlock the makeup air with the exhaust fans, and tell the owner the unit is part of the oven's venting.

CO and smoke in the dining room

CDC says carbon monoxide comes from burning fuel, lists charcoal grills and fireplaces, and says never to burn charcoal indoors. Headache, dizziness and upset stomach are common signs, and people who are asleep or impaired can be harmed before symptoms appear. Treat staff headaches or smoke in the dining room as a ventilation problem first: confirm the makeup air is running and the duct is not blocked, then call the fire official or a qualified contractor. For the stove side of this topic, see Wood and Pellet Stoves with Central Heat: Venting, Clearances and Carbon Monoxide.

What we could not confirm

We did not find a public source we could open for a required roof clearance or fan discharge height, so we give no number. Termination rules depend on the adopted code and the maker, and the fire official has the final say. Ask before you set the fan.

Field card: Wood-Fired Oven Exhaust

Own duct, own fan, spark arrestor, monthly cleaning, makeup air on.

  • Solid fuel exhaust is separate from every other exhaust system (public checklists citing NFPA 96).
  • No wall terminations; the duct goes up through the roof.
  • Spark arrestor filters, not mesh grease filters, in a hood over a wood oven (maker letter).
  • Solid fuel systems are on a monthly cleaning or inspection schedule in the public agency pages we read.
  • Fuel storage: noncombustible area, extinguisher or sprinklers by pile size.
  • Heating the makeup air: 1.08 × cfm × delta T.
  • Our example: 1,000 + 2,000 = 3,000 cfm, delta T 50°F, 162,000 Btu/h (assumed values).
  • If makeup air fails, expect weak draft, spillage and CO risk.
