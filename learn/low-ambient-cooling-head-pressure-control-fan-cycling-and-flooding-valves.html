Low Ambient Kit and Head Pressure Control: Fan Cycling and Flooding Valves

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A condenser sized for a hot July afternoon is far too good at its job in January. It dumps so much heat that head pressure sinks, and the expansion valve starts to starve. This lesson covers why that happens and the main ways technicians hold head pressure up: fan cycling, fan speed control, flooding valves and manufacturer low ambient kits.

Why head pressure falls in cold weather

Condensing temperature follows the air passing over the condenser. As outdoor air cools, the refrigerant condenses at a lower temperature, so head pressure drops with it. Sporlan's head pressure control literature makes the same point: head pressure falls with ambient temperature, and the pressure drop across the thermostatic expansion valve (TXV) falls with it. For the pressure-temperature link behind this, see Pressure-Temperature Basics.

A TXV needs a pressure difference to push liquid through its port. An ACHR News article on condenser pressure control explains that condensing pressure can swing widely while evaporating pressure stays nearly constant, so the valve's pressure difference changes, and its capacity falls when head pressure falls. If the difference gets too low, the article says compressors can cut out on low pressure or short-cycle, and the evaporator can be starved. Sporlan's bulletin lists starved evaporators, oil logging in the suction riser and higher discharge temperatures as low ambient risks. Its example shows a TXV rated 36,600 BTU/h at a 219 psi difference falling to 27,300 BTU/h at 122 psi. For how the valve itself behaves, see Metering Devices: TXV and Piston.

Low head is not always a fault. Compare it with the opposite problem in Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure. A clean condenser on a cold day is doing exactly what it should; the control problem is that the system needs a floor.

Low ambient can also make a unit hard to start. Copeland's technical note on ZX refrigeration units explains that after standstill the liquid receiver pressure may not be high enough above evaporator pressure, and the controller then does not see enough pressure to start. Their fix is control settings and a start signal from the room sensor, not hardware. Winter start is a real issue in the field, and each manufacturer handles it differently, so use the literature for your unit.

Where the floor is set

There is no universal minimum. Sporlan says the minimum head pressure is set by the pressure difference the TXV needs to meet the evaporator load. Its flooding bulletin says to set the valves roughly equivalent to a 90 to 100 degree F condensing temperature, and that the valves begin to throttle when ambient falls below about 70 degrees F. The unit's own literature always wins.

Method 1: fan cycling

The simplest control turns condenser fans off as the pressure or the air temperature drops. Less airflow means less heat removal, so head pressure rises back up. A pressure switch senses head pressure directly. A temperature switch senses ambient air instead.

Sporlan's example for an eight-fan condenser runs the fans in four pairs, each with a cut-in and a cut-out pressure. Carrier's older condensing pressure fan control accessory is a temperature type: a remote-bulb thermostat that shuts one condenser fan off, adjustable from 20 to 75 degrees F with a fixed 5 degree F differential. Carrier notes that only one fan cycles, so on a two-fan unit one fan always runs. Cycling gives a rough floor, not a steady pressure. Sporlan says head pressure can still fluctuate with fan cycling, which makes TXV capacity vary.

Method 2: fan speed control

A variable-speed fan control slows the fan instead of switching it off, which smooths the pressure. The ACHR article lists variable-speed fan control alongside on-off control and thermostatically controlled dampers as ways to manage air volume. Carrier's accessory sheet points to a Motormaster I control when ambient goes below the temperature where plain fan cycling is allowed. Check the unit's data for that temperature, since the sheet leaves the number to the product data.

Method 3: flooding the condenser

When fans alone are not enough, valves can back liquid up inside the condenser. Flooded tubes cannot condense vapor, so the active condensing area shrinks and head pressure holds up. Sporlan says so directly: flooding reduces the area available for condensing.

In Sporlan's ORI/ORD arrangement, the ORI (opens on rise of inlet pressure) sits in the liquid line between condenser and receiver. As condensing pressure falls, it throttles toward closed and holds liquid in the condenser. The ORD (opens on rise of differential pressure) sits in a hot gas line bypassing the condenser. Once the ORI creates enough pressure difference, the ORD opens and feeds hot gas to the receiver so receiver pressure stays up. Sporlan's standard ORD setting is 20 psig. Danfoss uses a similar pair, the KVR and NRD. Its service guide says the KVR is normally installed between the air-cooled condenser and the receiver and opens on rising condensing pressure, with a KVD or NRD helping keep receiver pressure up. In a very cold site, that guide mounts the KVR ahead of the condenser with the NRD in a bypass around it. The guide warns that a KVR must never be used as a safety valve.

Flooding has costs. Sporlan notes it can need up to 85 percent of the condenser flooded in extreme cold, takes extra refrigerant, and calls for a larger receiver. It also says to size the receiver so it is about 80 percent full when holding the entire system charge. Flooding valves also do nothing for a system that is short on charge, Sporlan adds. Exact receiver volumes are not covered here; use the valve maker's selection tables.

Method 4: manufacturer low ambient kits

Package and split system makers sell accessories that package one of these ideas. Trane's Low Ambient control for its 3 to 25 ton rooftop units is a controller that reads discharge pressure and cycles the outdoor fans to hold the higher circuit's pressure at a setpoint. Trane says fans run continuously above 50 degrees F, recommends a 245 psig setpoint, and expects discharge pressure to stay within 15 psig either way of it below 50 degrees F. It needs a second thermistor, and heat pump units need a jumper and wiring change. Carrier's accessory above is the temperature-switch version. Always install the kit listed for your exact model, because the sensors and wiring differ.

Worked example: reading setpoints against condensing temperature

Part A: an ORI set to 120 psig on R-134a. Sporlan lists 120 psig as the standard ORI factory setting for R-134a, but its bulletin has no pressure-temperature chart. The NIST Chemistry WebBook gives R-134a saturation pressures (absolute): 90 F is 119.01 psia and 100 F is 138.85 psia. Gauges read gauge pressure, so subtract one standard atmosphere, 14.696 psi.

  1. 90 F: 119.01 − 14.696 = 104.31 psig.
  2. 100 F: 138.85 − 14.696 = 124.15 psig.
  3. The 120 psig setting sits 120 − 104.31 = 15.69 psi above the 90 F value, out of a 124.15 − 104.31 = 19.84 psi span.
  4. 15.69 ÷ 19.84 = 0.79, and 0.79 × 10 F = 7.9 F, so about 90 + 7.9 = 97.9 F by straight-line interpolation.

That is a condensing temperature near 98 F. It falls inside the 90 to 100 F range Sporlan recommends. The valve starts holding liquid back once condensing drops under about 98 F. Interpolation between two chart points is an estimate; read the chart for your refrigerant when you set a real valve.

Part B: a fan-cycling ladder. Sporlan's R-404A example uses these pairs: fans 1 and 2 cut in at 180 and cut out at 160 psig; fans 3 and 4, 190 and 170; fans 5 and 6, 200 and 180; fans 7 and 8, 210 and 190.

  1. Differential for each pair: 180 − 160 = 20 psi (the same for the others).
  2. Spacing between stages: 190 − 180 = 10 psi.
  3. Total ladder from the lowest cut-out to the highest cut-in: 210 − 160 = 50 psi.
  4. Sporlan's bulletin gives 170 psig as 78 F and 190 psig as 85 F for R-404A, so 20 psi spans 85 − 78 = 7 F, about 0.35 F per psi in that band.

So a 20 psi differential is only about 7 F of condensing temperature. Sporlan says that, except on a cold windy day, head pressure with this scheme would not fall below 160 psig. A temperature-type switch works differently: Carrier says its 60 F setting shuts the fan off when condensing temperature reaches 90 F, which is a 30 F gap between ambient and condensing (90 − 60).

Field card: Low Ambient Head Pressure Control

Cold air lowers head, low head starves the TXV.

  • Head pressure follows ambient; TXV capacity falls with its pressure difference.
  • Fan cycling: pressure or temperature switch; simple, but head still swings.
  • Fan speed control: smoother than on-off.
  • Flooding: ORI/ORD or KVR/NRD; needs more charge and a bigger receiver.
  • Flooding valves do not help a system that is short of refrigerant.
  • Worked example: ORI 120 psig on R-134a is about 98 F condensing; 20 psi differential on R-404A is about 7 F.
  • Use the maker's low ambient kit and settings for the exact model.
