Natural-Draft Vent Connectors: Rise, Length, Elbows and Spillage

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A natural-draft gas appliance has no fan to push its exhaust out. The hot gas has to climb by itself, so the pipe between the appliance and the chimney (the vent connector) has to be laid out to help it, not fight it. This lesson covers the rise, the horizontal length limit, elbows, the draft hood, and the spillage test that proves the result.

Check the manual and the code

Check the manual and the code. This is general education, not a ruling on a particular job. Vent work follows the appliance maker's installation instructions and the fuel gas code your local authority has adopted. The code text below comes from a city government's published fuel gas code, which reprints the International Fuel Gas Code (IFGC) chapter on chimneys and vents. Section numbers shift between editions, so we name the topic and let you find it in your own adopted edition.

What a vent connector is

In a category I system (see Furnace and Boiler Vent Categories I to IV Explained) the vent runs under negative pressure and depends on buoyancy. The connector is the short piece from the draft hood outlet or flue collar to the vertical vent or chimney. It is the part that is easiest to get wrong, because it is often routed around a joist, a duct or a water heater on the way to the chimney.

Why the connector must rise

Hot flue gas is lighter than the room air around it, so it moves upward, and a long flat or sagging section gives it nothing to climb. A slope upward toward the chimney keeps the flow moving in the direction it wants to go. A vent maker's sizing handbook hosted by a university extension site says that a greater connector rise gives a stronger draft, and that a vent should be installed with a rise of 1/4 inch per foot so condensate does not collect in it. The adopted code says the same thing in code language: a connector for a category I appliance must slope upward toward the vent or chimney at not less than 1/4 inch per foot, and it must be installed without dips or sags (2021 Seattle Fuel Gas Code, chimneys and vents chapter, in the vent connector section for category I appliances). A sag is a pocket where condensate and slow gas can collect.

How long can the horizontal run be?

The longer the flat travel, the more the gas cools and the more draft is lost. The code limits it as a share of the vertical height, not as a fixed number of feet. In the 2021 Seattle Fuel Gas Code text, the maximum horizontal length of a single-wall connector is 75 percent of the height of the chimney or vent, and for a Type B double-wall connector it is 100 percent of that height. Engineered systems are excluded from both limits. The code text we read did not give a limit tied to connector diameter, so we do not teach one here. Where a limit by diameter shows up in a maker's manual, use that source for that appliance.

Sizing is separate from length. The code sizes connectors through its venting tables or approved engineering methods, and the tables list a lateral length alongside vent height. The same vent maker's handbook notes that a shorter lateral run means more capacity from the system. If a connector has to be made larger to meet an installation limit, the code says the increase is made at the draft hood outlet. The code also caps a connector at two sizes larger than the outlet, and it allows a vent smaller than the outlet only when total vent height is at least 10 feet and the reduction is no more than one table size. Treat these as limits to confirm in your own edition and table method.

Fewer elbows, better draft

Every elbow adds resistance. The venting tables in the code are built on the assumption of two 90-degree elbows. For each extra elbow up to and including 45 degrees, the code reduces table capacity by 5 percent, and for each extra elbow above 45 degrees up to 90 degrees, by 10 percent, so bends add up. The vent maker's handbook adds that more than two pairs of elbows (four elbows) should be avoided if at all possible. Route the connector the shortest way to the vent, and use a gentle bend where a turn is needed.

What the draft hood does

A draft hood is the open fitting on a natural-draft appliance where the flue gases leave and room air is drawn in. The vent maker's handbook says the hood lets dilution air enter the vent system, which keeps the vent working if the appliance malfunctions, and that the extra air dilutes the flue gas and lowers its dew point, so the vent dries between cycles. Code text adds installation rules: the draft hood goes in the same room or enclosure as the appliance, so there is no pressure difference between the hood and the combustion air supply; a supplied hood is installed exactly as the maker furnished it; and the relief opening needs at least 6 inches of clearance from any surface other than the appliance and its venting system. Never block, seal or modify the relief opening. For the air supply side, see Combustion Air for Furnaces, Boilers and Water Heaters in Closets and Basements.

Checking draft: the spillage test

Spillage means flue gas coming out of the draft hood relief opening instead of going up the vent. The draft hood is the typical spillage point on a natural-draft appliance, according to a federal energy-office slide deck on gaps in combustion-safety codes. The same deck says the fuel gas code procedure checks for spillage after five minutes of burner operation. That timing matters, because a vent can look fine early and spill later, especially with an exhaust fan or dryer pulling the house under negative pressure. The full method, including how to test the worst case, is in Combustion Appliance Zone Worst-Case Depressurization and Spillage Testing. Follow the written procedure your employer or program uses, and treat a failed test as a stop sign, not a note on the invoice. A flat or sagging connector, too many elbows and an oversized or blocked chimney are the usual suspects (see Chimney Liners for Gas and Oil Heat: When You Need One).

Worked example (our own, with assumptions)

This is our own example, not from a code book. Assumptions: a single-wall steel connector serving one draft-hood water heater; the vertical vent rises 12 feet above the point where the connector joins it; the layout plan shows 7 feet of horizontal connector run; a table capacity of 40,000 Btu/h.

  1. Single-wall limit: 75 percent of vent height = 0.75 x 12 ft = 9 ft. The 7 ft plan is under 9 ft, so it passes this check.
  2. Minimum rise: 1/4 inch per foot x 7 ft = 1.75 inches. The far end of the connector must sit at least 1.75 inches higher than the draft hood end.
  3. Now assume the vent is only 8 ft tall: 0.75 x 8 ft = 6 ft. The 7 ft run fails by 1 ft. Fixes include moving the appliance closer, a different layout, or a Type B double-wall connector (100 percent of 8 ft = 8 ft, so 7 ft passes) if the maker and code allow it for that appliance.
  4. Elbows: two 90-degree elbows are already allowed for in the tables. A third 90-degree elbow cuts capacity by 10 percent: 0.90 x 40,000 = 36,000 Btu/h.

Even a layout that passes on paper still has to pass the spillage test on site.

Field card: Natural-Draft Vent Connectors

Rise it, keep it short, keep it straight, then test it.

  • Connector slopes up toward the chimney, at least 1/4 inch per foot, with no dips or sags.
  • Horizontal length limit (as in the code we read): 75 percent of vent height for single-wall, 100 percent for Type B double-wall.
  • Use the shortest route and the fewest elbows; extra elbows cut table capacity.
  • Draft hood sits in the same space as the appliance. Never block or alter the relief opening.
  • Spillage test after about five minutes of burner run, then worst-case conditions.
  • Maker instructions and your adopted code win over this page.
