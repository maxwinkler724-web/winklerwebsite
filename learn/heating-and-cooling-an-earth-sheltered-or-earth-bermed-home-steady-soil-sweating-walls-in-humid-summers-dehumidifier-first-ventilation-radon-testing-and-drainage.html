Heating and Cooling an Earth-Sheltered or Earth-Bermed Home

Last updated: 2026-11-04

An earth-sheltered home has soil against its walls, and sometimes over its roof. That soil keeps the house near a steady temperature, so heating and cooling loads are small. The surprise for many owners is that summer humidity, not heat, becomes the main comfort problem. This lesson explains why, and what to do about it.

Steady soil, small loads

Soil changes temperature slowly. A few feet down, it swings far less than outdoor air does across the seasons, so an earth-backed wall loses less heat in winter and gains less in summer than a wall exposed to the weather. Our ground temperature lesson covers how soil temperature changes with depth and season. The practical result is a small equipment need. Many such homes are served by one small heat pump or a ductless unit, though the right size comes from a load calculation for your house, not a rule of thumb.

A bermed home has soil piled against some walls but not others. Its glass faces usually open to the sun side, so it behaves partly like a basement and partly like an ordinary house.

Why cool earth-backed walls sweat

A cold glass of water sweats on a humid day because the glass is colder than the dew point of the air touching it. An earth-backed wall can do the same thing. In summer, the wall stays cool because the soil is cool, while humid outdoor air leaks and drifts into the house. When that air meets the cool wall, water condenses. A university extension page on basement moisture says the same thing: warm, humid air condenses on cool basement walls and floors, and it often gets mistaken for a leak. It also warns that opening windows to air out a basement in summer makes the problem worse.

A federal environmental agency guide on mold says the key to mold control is moisture control. It suggests keeping indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and says condensation on walls, windows or pipes is a sign of high humidity. Damp walls give mold and musty smells a place to start, and a house with a lot of wall touching soil has a lot of such surface.

Worked example (our own): will the wall sweat?

This is our own example. The numbers are assumptions chosen for teaching, not measurements from a real house.

  • Assumption: the inside surface of the earth-backed wall is about 58 F in summer.
  • Assumption: room air is 75 F at 60 percent relative humidity.

We use the standard Magnus formula for dew point, with constants a = 17.62 and b = 243.12 (a common choice, our own pick). Convert to Celsius first: 75 F is 23.9 C.

  1. Compute g = ln(RH) + a×T/(b+T) = ln(0.60) + 17.62×23.9/(243.12+23.9) = −0.511 + 1.577 = 1.066.
  2. Dew point = b×g/(a−g) = 243.12×1.066/(17.62−1.066) = 15.7 C, which is about 60 F.
  3. Compare: dew point of about 60 F is above the 58 F wall, so water condenses on the wall.

Now dry the same 75 F air to 50 percent relative humidity. Then g = ln(0.50) + 1.577 = 0.884, and dew point = 243.12×0.884/(17.62−0.884) = 12.8 C, about 55 F. That is below the 58 F wall, so the wall stays dry, with about 3 F of margin. All of these dew points are approximate, and a real wall will vary from spot to spot, so treat the margin as thin. The lesson: lowering humidity fixed the problem, and cooling the air further would not have.

Dehumidify first, cool second

Because the loads are small and the wall is already cool, a bigger air conditioner is the wrong fix. A cooling unit that is oversized for the load shuts off before it has run long enough to pull much moisture out of the air. A small heat pump or ductless unit plus a properly sized dehumidifier with a drain suits these homes better. See our basement dehumidifier lesson for sizing, setpoint and drainage, and mini-split basics if you are weighing a ductless unit. Steady air movement helps too, since still air in a corner or behind furniture lets a cool wall surface sit damp. Use a simple humidity meter, and aim for the range the federal guide gives.

A caution from the same extension page: a dehumidifier can reduce humidity and odor, but it does not cure a moisture source, and over-drying a damp basement can pull more water through concrete.

Water outside comes before HVAC

No equipment fixes a wall that is wet from the soil side. The extension page recommends fixing surface grading, gutters and downspouts first. It gives a slope of at least 1 inch per foot for the first 6 feet away from the foundation and downspout extensions that discharge at least 4 feet from the wall, and says to consider drainage systems only if moisture persists. Waterproofing, drainage and the roof membrane on a covered roof belong to a builder or waterproofing professional. Settle those before sizing any HVAC.

Glass, sun and overheating

Many of these homes use large south-facing glass for daylight and winter sun. That helps in winter, but in warm months the same glass can overheat the rooms, and a house with little exposed wall has few places to shed that heat. Shading such as overhangs, plus sensible window treatments, helps. This is our own general guidance, so have the glass area counted in the load calculation.

Fresh air

A tight, earth-covered house needs planned fresh air. Bathroom and kitchen fans move moisture outdoors, as the federal mold guide advises, but they pull make-up air through whatever gaps exist. A balanced ventilation system brings in about as much air as it exhausts, and some types also move moisture and heat between the two streams. Our ERV and HRV lesson explains the choices. In humid summers, a unit that does not add moisture is worth discussing with your installer.

Radon: test it

So much of the house touches soil that radon testing matters. Radon is a radioactive gas that can come from the ground. The federal environmental agency page on radon health risk says the agency urges testing, and says to fix the problem when the level is 4 pCi/L or more and to consider fixing between 2 and 4 pCi/L. Read radon mitigation and your HVAC and our walkout basement planning lesson for more.

Fuel-burning equipment and alarms

If any equipment burns fuel, such as a gas furnace, boiler, water heater or wood stove, keep it properly vented with combustion air, and install carbon monoxide and smoke alarms. A federal consumer product safety agency page says to put CO alarms on every level of the home and outside sleeping areas, and that interconnected alarms are preferred so that when one sounds, all sound. See carbon monoxide alarms and what to do.

Field card: Earth-Sheltered Home HVAC

Dry the air, fix the water outside, test for radon.

  • Soil keeps loads small; right-size to a real load calculation.
  • Cool earth-backed walls sweat when the wall is colder than the air dew point.
  • Keep indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50 percent.
  • Grading, gutters and drainage come before HVAC.
  • Plan balanced fresh air; test for radon.
  • CO and smoke alarms with any fuel-burning equipment.
