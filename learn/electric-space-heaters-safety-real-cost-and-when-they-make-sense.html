Electric Space Heaters: Safety, Real Cost and When They Make Sense

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A plug-in heater is the fastest way to warm one cold room, and heating equipment is also a leading cause of winter house fires. This lesson covers what fire-safety groups say about using one, why every 1,500 watt heater makes the same heat, what it costs to run, and the narrow case where it can save money.

Safety first. Keep a space heater at least 3 feet from anything that can burn, plug it straight into a wall outlet, and turn it off when you leave the room or go to sleep. If the outlet or plug gets hot, stop using it and have a qualified electrician look at the outlet. Call 911 for any fire.

How risky are space heaters?

The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) studied home heating fires from 2011 to 2015. As reported by Occupational Health & Safety magazine, space heaters, portable and stationary together, were involved in 43 percent of home heating fires and 85 percent of home heating fire deaths. The same report says 53 percent of heating fire deaths came from heating equipment placed too close to things that burn, such as furniture, clothing, mattresses and bedding. A later NFPA finding, quoted by the consumer group PIRG, is that from 2014 to 2018 nearly half of heating equipment fires happened in December, January and February.

The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) estimate, as PIRG cites it from a 2022 CPSC release, is roughly 1,700 fires, 80 deaths and 160 injuries a year from portable space heaters. PIRG gives no reference period for those numbers, so treat them as a rough scale, not an exact count. The pattern in both sets of numbers is the same: distance from combustibles and leaving a heater running with nobody watching.

The rules, in plain words

  • 3 feet clear. NFPA's advice, as reported, is a 3-foot zone around a space heater, free of kids, pets and anything that burns. PIRG's list names rugs, bedding, curtains, papers, clothing and furniture.
  • Wall outlet only. PIRG's summary of CPSC advice is to plug a heater directly into a wall outlet, not an extension cord or power strip. Missouri's Division of Energy fact sheet also says never to plug an electric heater into a common household extension cord. NFPA's advice, as reported, is to plug only one heat-producing appliance into an outlet at a time.
  • Tip-over and overheat shutoff. PIRG says many newer models switch off if tipped, though the related standard is voluntary, and it advises looking for shutoff features for overheating and tip-over. Yukon's Energy Solutions Centre lists the same features plus screening that keeps fingers off the fan or element. Put the heater on a level, hard, nonflammable surface.
  • Listed by a testing lab. PIRG advises buying a heater tested to current standards and looking for a mark from a lab such as UL, CSA or Intertek (the ETL mark).
  • Not unattended, not while sleeping. NFPA's advice, as reported, is to turn the heater off when leaving the room or going to sleep. PIRG adds that a heater should not be set up near a sleeping person. Based on these sources, the honest answer to "are space heaters safe to leave on?" is no: they are meant to be watched.
  • Breakers and alarms. If a breaker trips, the Missouri fact sheet says to contact an electrician. It also suggests fire detectors in rooms where heaters are used.

Electric heaters burn no fuel, so they do not make carbon monoxide themselves. Fuel-burning heaters and generators do. That is a separate hazard covered in Carbon Monoxide During Winter Outages, so this page stays on electric units.

Types, without a favorite

Heaters differ in how the heat reaches you, not in how much they make. Yukon's tip sheet sorts them into convective types (electric element, liquid-filled and ceramic), which warm the air with a fan or natural air movement, and radiant types (metal rod and quartz), which give off infrared that warms people and objects directly. Missouri's fact sheet uses three groups: radiant units that warm you quickly but heat a room slowly, radiator-type units that give even, steady warmth with a cooler surface, and convection units, often with a fan, that heat an area quickly. Oil-filled, ceramic and infrared models fall in these groups, with trade-offs in speed, noise and surface temperature. This lesson does not recommend a brand or type, and none of them removes the safety rules above.

Why every 1,500 W heater makes the same heat

An electric resistance heater turns the electricity it uses into heat. Yukon's tip sheet says portable electric heaters convert all the power they draw into heat, and Missouri's fact sheet calls all electric resistance heaters about 100 percent efficient. A University of Missouri Extension specialist makes the practical point: at the same wattage, two heaters produce the same amount of heat. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) lists 1 kilowatt-hour as 3,412 Btu. Since 1 kW for one hour is 1,000 W for one hour, 1 W for one hour is 3.412 Btu, so:

1,500 W × 3.412 Btu/h per watt = 5,118 Btu/h

That is true of a $30 box and a $300 designer unit alike. Price buys speed, quiet, looks and controls, not more Btu from the same watts. For scale, the same Missouri extension specialist says a well-insulated 1,500 square foot home needs at least 70,000 Btu per hour, so one heater covers a room, not a house.

What it costs to run

Cost is kilowatts times hours times your rate; Missouri's fact sheet gives the same formula. Your rate is on your electric bill, in dollars per kWh, and it varies by utility and season. The rate below, $0.15 per kWh, is only an example for the arithmetic, not a real current price. Replace it with your own.

Worked example: one night in a bedroom (example rate)

  1. Convert watts to kilowatts: 1,500 W ÷ 1,000 = 1.5 kW.
  2. Cost per hour: 1.5 kW × $0.15 per kWh = $0.225, about 22.5 cents.
  3. Run it 8 hours: 1.5 kW × 8 h = 12 kWh.
  4. Cost: 12 kWh × $0.15 = $1.80 for the night.
  5. Over 30 nights: $1.80 × 30 = $54.
  6. Heat delivered: 12 kWh × 3,412 Btu per kWh = 40,944 Btu.

At an example rate of $0.30, the same night costs $3.60. A thermostat that cycles the heater off part of the time lowers the real number, so this is a continuous-running figure. And since the sources above advise turning a heater off at bedtime, an all-night run is a cost example, not a recommended practice.

When zone heating can save money

The Missouri Division of Energy fact sheet says electric resistance heaters are the most expensive way to heat, and that heating a whole house this way is usually more costly than a natural gas furnace or heat pump. Its savings case is narrow: if you lower the central thermostat a few degrees and use a space heater only for the small area you occupy, you will probably save. If you do not lower the thermostat, adding a space heater raises your total bill. The extension specialist says the same: big-savings claims hold only if you turn down the thermostat and heat just the room you are in, and turning the whole house down too far risks frozen pipes and condensation on walls. See Heat Went Out in Winter: How to Keep Pipes From Freezing before setting a house very low.

If your home already has fixed electric heat, a built-in baseboard unit has its own thermostat and limit control; see Electric Baseboard Heater Not Working.

Field card: Electric Space Heaters

Same watts, same heat. Distance and attention are the safety.

  • 3 feet clear of anything that burns; level, hard, nonflammable surface.
  • Wall outlet only: no extension cord or power strip, one heat appliance per outlet.
  • Look for tip-over and overheat shutoff and a UL, CSA or ETL listing mark.
  • Off when you leave the room or go to sleep.
  • 1 W = 3.412 Btu/h, so 1,500 W = 5,118 Btu/h for every type.
  • Cost = kW × hours × your rate. Example only: 1.5 kW × 8 h × $0.15 = $1.80.
  • Zone heating can save only if you lower the central thermostat.
