Outdoor Design Temperatures for New HVAC Systems: 99% and 1% Values, Design Wet Bulb, and Why Record Highs and Lows Oversize Equipment

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Before anyone can size a new furnace, heat pump or air conditioner, the load calculation needs two outdoor numbers: a cold one and a hot one. Those numbers are not record lows and highs. They are percentile values from many years of hourly weather, chosen so that a handful of hours each year are allowed to go beyond them. This lesson explains what the values mean, how the design wet bulb fits in, where the model codes point, and what you should expect on the few hours past design.

The short answer

The 99 percent heating value is the outdoor temperature that the location stays at or above for 99 percent of the hours in a year. The 1 percent cooling value is the temperature the location stays at or below for 99 percent of the hours. In a year of 8,760 hours, 1 percent is 87.6 hours, so the equipment is allowed to be a little short for about 88 hours a year, spread over a few cold nights or hot afternoons. Sizing to the single coldest or hottest hour on record would add capacity that sits idle nearly every hour of the year.

What the percentages mean in hours

The federal engineering weather data handbook from NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) defines it plainly: the 1.0 percent design value for a location is a temperature that has been exceeded only 1 percent of the time during the whole period of record. It also states that the percentile values are based on the entire year, and its sample station uses a period of record of 1967 to 1996, so a design value is an average over decades, not a forecast for next summer. The handbook sample also lists hours-per-year counts, for example 84 hours above 93 F at that station.

A university HVAC design lecture we read converts the percentiles into hours out of 8,760: the 99.6 percent value is exceeded for about 35 hours, 99 percent for about 88, 98 percent for about 175, and 95 percent for about 438. You can check any of these yourself: multiply the leftover percentage by 8,760. Some training slides describe the heating value as covering 99 percent of the heating season instead of the whole year, so read the footnote on the table you use, because the number of hours behind "1 percent" changes with the basis.

Why the load calculation does not use record highs and lows

A record is a one-hour event that may happen once in several decades. Equipment sized for it would be oversized on every ordinary day. A DOE building technologies brief on residential loads and sizing notes that mechanical systems in residential construction are commonly oversized and that the goal is equipment that is neither too small nor too large. The university lecture frames the percentile as the risk level you choose to accept, with a note to use realistic safety factors rather than stacking extra margin. The cost of too much margin shows up as short cycling and poor humidity control; see AC short cycling and oversizing.

Design wet bulb and the coincident wet bulb

Cooling needs two outdoor numbers, because the coil removes moisture as well as heat. The design dry bulb (the 1 percent value) comes with a mean coincident wet bulb, which is the average wet-bulb reading on the hours when the dry bulb was near the design value. In the NCEI handbook sample, the 1.0 percent dry bulb of 92 F is paired with a mean coincident wet bulb of 76 F. The same sample page has separate wet-bulb hour counts, such as 773 hours above a wet bulb of 73 F, which are used when moisture, not temperature, is the problem. A higher wet bulb at the same dry bulb means more moisture in the air and a larger latent load. Equipment tables then ask for the entering wet bulb; see reading expanded cooling performance tables. To measure wet bulb on a real job, see measuring wet-bulb temperature in the field.

Where the codes point

The values come from the national engineering climate design tables for the station nearest the job, or from the table your load software uses. Codes then connect the pieces:

  • International Residential Code. Section M1401.3 requires heating and cooling equipment to be sized under the national equipment selection method from loads calculated under the national residential load method or another approved method. The DOE brief says the 2009 IRC requires both, while the 2006 edition did not specifically require the national equipment selection method. The IRC climate table, Table R301.2(1), has a winter design temperature entry, and a city adoption we read adds a load-calculation design criteria block with indoor design temperature and heating and cooling temperature differences for the jurisdiction to fill in.
  • International Energy Conservation Code. Section R403.7 is the mandatory residential section on equipment sizing and efficiency rating. A regional training presentation quotes the 2015 and 2018 wording as requiring sizing under the national equipment selection method. Which edition applies depends on what your state or town has adopted, so check the adopted edition.

For a plain-language view of the sizing chain after the load number is found, see picking equipment size after the load calculation, and for the calculation itself see the heat load calculation intro.

Indoor design conditions

The other half of every temperature difference is the indoor setpoint used in the calculation. A 2015 state adoption of the IECC residential chapter that we read says, in Section R302.1, that interior design temperatures for load calculations are a maximum of 72 F for heating and a minimum of 75 F for cooling, and that same section group sends outdoor design values to a table of thermal design parameters. The regional training slides cite the same 72 and 75 F limits. The university lecture suggests 70 to 72 F at about 30 percent relative humidity for heating and 75 to 78 F at about 50 percent for cooling. These are design inputs, not thermostat orders. An owner can set 68 or 80 F; the equipment was just sized from the code numbers. Raising the indoor design cooling temperature or lowering the heating one shrinks the load on paper, so a designer who changes them should be able to say why.

Our own worked example: counting hours in a made-up year

Our own example. Every number here is invented to show the idea, not data for any town.

Imagine a town with 8,760 hourly readings in one year. We sort them into 5 F bins. The coldest bins hold 8, 30 and 50 hours below 15 F, and the hottest bins hold 80 and 8 hours above 90 F. The other 8,584 hours sit in between.

  1. Hours below 15 F: 8 + 30 + 50 = 88. As a share: 88 / 8,760 = 1.0 percent. So 15 F is the 99 percent heating value of our made-up year.
  2. Hours above 90 F: 80 + 8 = 88, also 1.0 percent. So 90 F is the 1 percent cooling value.
  3. Check: 0.01 x 8,760 = 87.6, which rounds to the 88 hours we counted. Middle hours: 8,760 minus 88 minus 88 = 8,584.

Now size the heating. Assumptions (ours): the house loses 500 Btu per hour for each degree F of indoor-outdoor difference (UA = 500 Btu/h per F, envelope and air leakage lumped together), indoor design temperature 70 F, and we ignore solar gain, people and appliances.

  1. At the 99 percent value: delta-T = 70 - 15 = 55 F. Load = 500 x 55 = 27,500 Btu/h.
  2. Suppose someone sizes to a record low 15 F colder, which is 0 F: delta-T = 70. Load = 500 x 70 = 35,000 Btu/h.
  3. The extra is 35,000 - 27,500 = 7,500 Btu/h, or 7,500 / 27,500 = about 27 percent more capacity, bought to cover an hour that, in our made-up year, never happened.
  4. On a mild 45 F day the load is 500 x 25 = 12,500 Btu/h. The design-sized unit would run 12,500 / 27,500 = about 45 percent of the time. The record-sized unit would run 12,500 / 35,000 = about 36 percent, so its cycles are shorter on the days that make up most of the heating season.

What if it is colder than design? On a 5 F night, the load in our simple model is 500 x 65 = 32,500 Btu/h, which is 5,000 Btu/h more than the 27,500 Btu/h unit delivers. Running nonstop, it would settle where 500 x (indoor - 5) = 27,500, so indoor = 60 F. Real houses do better than that, because mass, sun and internal gains help and real sizing adds some margin, but the sketch shows the typical outcome: a cool house, not a failure.

What happens to comfort on the few hours beyond design

On the roughly 88 hours past either design line, a properly sized system runs continuously and the house drifts away from the setpoint, slowly, because the building's mass holds heat or cold. In our example, a few degrees cooler on a few winter nights. In summer, equipment capacity also falls as outdoor temperature rises, so the drift can be a few degrees warm on the hottest afternoons. That is by design, not a defect. A system that cannot hold temperature on ordinary days has a different problem. For how to tell the two apart, see AC can't keep up on the hottest days.

If an owner cannot accept those hours, say a medical need or a server room, the fix is a deliberate decision to use a more extreme percentile or add backup heat or cooling, not an unexplained bigger box. Write down the design values used so the next person can see the choice.

Field card: outdoor design temperatures

Design values leave out the extremes on purpose.

  • 1 percent of 8,760 hours = 87.6 hours; 99 percent heating means about 88 colder hours a year.
  • Cooling design is a dry bulb plus its mean coincident wet bulb.
  • Record highs and lows oversize equipment: 15 F colder in our example added about 27 percent heating capacity.
  • IRC M1401.3 and IECC R403.7 send sizing through the national residential load method and the national equipment selection method.
  • Interior design temperatures in the IECC load-calculation section: up to 72 F heating, at least 75 F cooling.
  • Ask which station, which table and which percentile your load report used.
