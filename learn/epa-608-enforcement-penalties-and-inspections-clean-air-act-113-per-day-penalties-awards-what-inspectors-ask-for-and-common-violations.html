EPA 608 Enforcement: Penalties and Inspections

Last updated: 2026-11-03

Most 608 study material covers what the rules require. This lesson covers the other half: what happens when the rules are not followed. It is a study-guide summary of how federal refrigerant rules are enforced, not legal advice.

Check the current law

Check the current law. Penalty amounts are adjusted for inflation and rules change. We read the federal sources listed below in 2026. For a real situation, use EPA's own pages and a qualified professional.

Where the penalty power comes from

Refrigerant rules under Clean Air Act Title VI are enforced through the Act's general enforcement section, section 113 (42 U.S.C. 7413). In the version published by the Government Publishing Office, subsection (b) lets EPA bring a civil action for a penalty of "not more than $25,000 per day for each violation." That is the figure written into the law. Two phrases matter for study: per day, and for each violation. A violation that continues, or several separate violations, are counted separately.

The $25,000 is not the number in force today. Federal law requires agencies to adjust civil penalties for inflation, and EPA publishes the adjusted figures in a table in 40 CFR 19.4. In the table we read, the row for 42 U.S.C. 7413(b) shows $124,426 for penalties assessed on or after January 8, 2025, up from $121,275 for assessments between December 27, 2023 and January 8, 2025. The Federal Register notice that set the 2025 figures says the rule was effective January 8, 2025. The table itself does not print the words "per day," so the per-day meaning comes from the statute. The same table lists separate, lower figures for administrative cases under section 113(d)(1), which EPA can use instead of going to court.

Because the table is updated from time to time, treat $124,426 as the figure we confirmed for 2025, and check 40 CFR 19.4 on eCFR for the current column before quoting it.

The award provision

Subsection (f) of section 113 lets the EPA Administrator pay an award to a person whose information or services lead to a criminal conviction or a civil penalty. The statute caps an award at $10,000, makes payment subject to available appropriations, and says government officers and employees acting in an official capacity are not eligible. The Administrator may set further rules by regulation. The inflation table we read has no row for subsection (f), so the $10,000 is the statutory figure, not an adjusted one. This is a legal provision to know about, not a program we can tell you how to use.

Who enforces

EPA is the federal agency. It has ten regional offices, Regions 1 through 10, according to its regional offices page. EPA's list of Title VI enforcement actions attributes some cases to a regional office (it names the New England office in one entry). We did not find a page that says which office handles which kind of inspection, so we do not claim one. Cases end in different ways: civil penalties, required compliance spending, and, in the more serious cases on that page, criminal fines and prison terms.

What an inspection can look at

EPA's published guide to its access and inspection authorities says that under the Clean Air Act, EPA may enter the premises of a facility subject to the Act to review and copy records, check monitoring equipment and collect samples. Those powers serve to set standards, determine violations and gather information needed to enforce the law. For refrigerant work, the practical questions fall into three groups, matching the diagram below.

  • Proof of certification. The technician rule in 40 CFR 82.161 says technicians must keep a copy of their certification at their place of business until three years after they stop working as technicians. Certifying programs issue the identification cards. See who needs EPA 608 certification.
  • Service and leak records. 40 CFR 82.166 has owners and operators keep servicing records showing the date and type of service and the quantity of refrigerant added, and has servicers of appliances normally containing 50 pounds or more of refrigerant give the owner an invoice or other documentation of the amount added. Records required by that section are kept at least three years unless the section says otherwise, and the leak-repair provisions have their own records and reports. The details are in EPA 608 records: what technicians keep and leak inspections and chronic leaker reports, so we do not repeat them here.
  • Recovery equipment. EPA's enforcement summaries include cases where facilities did not certify recovery or recycling equipment or lacked certified recovery devices. So equipment is something enforcement looks at.

Common violations in EPA's summaries

EPA's page on enforcement actions under Title VI groups its examples. We list the categories without naming any party:

  • Failing to repair leaks promptly and to verify the repairs, and industrial systems over a 35 percent annualized leak rate.
  • Knowingly venting refrigerant during servicing. The rule is in the venting prohibition lesson.
  • Selling ozone-depleting refrigerant to people who are not certified technicians (see the sales restriction), and selling unapproved flammable substitutes.
  • Imports and smuggling of refrigerant.
  • Not documenting service dates, leak tests or servicing, and not reporting accurately.
  • Servicing appliances without certification.
  • Scrapping or shredding appliances and vehicles without first recovering the refrigerant, or accepting them without verifying the refrigerant was removed. Our disposal lesson covers that side.

The same page reports civil penalties in settlements that range from about $27,000 to $1.55 million. Those are outcomes of negotiated cases. They are not a formula and they are not the statutory maximum.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example, made up for study. It is not a real shop or case. Assume a made-up shop, "Shop A," and two things: (1) one violation that stays uncorrected, such as a leak that is never repaired as required; and (2) the 7413(b) figure of $124,426 per day for assessments on or after January 8, 2025 as the ceiling. We also assume, only for the arithmetic, that every day counts.

The arithmetic is days multiplied by the ceiling:

  • 1 day: 1 × $124,426 = $124,426
  • 10 days: 10 × $124,426 = $1,244,260
  • 30 days: 30 × $124,426 = $3,732,780
  • 90 days: 90 × $124,426 = $11,198,340

This is a ceiling, not a typical amount. It shows only why the words "per day" make a long-running violation large on paper. The settlements on EPA's page, for example, ended far below these numbers, and each case turns on its own facts. A second violation on the same days would be counted separately, which our example does not do.

Shop A's records check list (our own). A shop that wants its paperwork ready for a records request could keep these on hand, using only the items we sourced above:

  1. A copy of each technician's EPA 608 certification, kept at the business.
  2. Service records for each appliance: date, type of service, refrigerant added.
  3. Invoices showing the refrigerant added on appliances of 50 pounds or more of normal charge.
  4. Leak repair, verification test and inspection records where the leak-repair provisions apply.
  5. Proof that recovery equipment is certified.
  6. Disposal verification records where the disposal rules apply.

Item 5 comes from the enforcement summaries rather than a rule citation we read, so check the equipment provisions in 40 CFR part 82, subpart F for exactly what to keep. Keeping required records is the only practical step this lesson covers.

Field card: EPA 608 Enforcement: Penalties and Inspections

Per day, per violation, and the paperwork shows it.

  • Clean Air Act 113(b) statute text: not more than $25,000 per day for each violation.
  • Inflation-adjusted figure in the 40 CFR 19.4 table we read: $124,426, for assessments on or after January 8, 2025. Check for a newer column.
  • Award under 113(f): statutory cap $10,000, subject to appropriations; government employees on duty are ineligible.
  • EPA has ten regional offices.
  • Inspection areas: certification copy, service and leak records, recovery equipment.
  • Records under 82.166 are kept at least three years unless the section says otherwise.
  • A ceiling is not a typical settlement.
