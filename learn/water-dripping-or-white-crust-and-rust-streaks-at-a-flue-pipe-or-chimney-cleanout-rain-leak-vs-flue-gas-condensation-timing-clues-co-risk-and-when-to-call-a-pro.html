Dripping or Crusty Flue Pipe: Rain Leak or Condensation?

Last updated: 2026-11-04

You see a wet spot, white crust or a rust streak at the pipe running from your furnace, boiler or water heater, or at the little cleanout door at the base of the chimney. Water got there one of two ways: it fell in from outside, or it formed inside from the exhaust itself. This lesson shows how to tell which, why it matters, and what you can safely check yourself.

Why a wet flue is not just a wet stain

A flue pipe or chimney carries combustion gases outdoors. The federal consumer product safety agency says the yearly check of fuel-burning heating equipment should include chimneys, flues and vents, looking for leakage and for blockage by debris, and that vent pipes should be secure and not disconnected. A federal environmental agency page adds that flues and chimneys should be connected, in good condition and not blocked. A university extension fact sheet lists cracked or separating exhaust flues, a corroded or disconnected vent pipe and a blocked chimney opening as conditions to look for, and it names spillage of combustion by-products into the home as a dangerous situation. Water is one way those conditions can start (our own reasoning): it rusts thin metal, loosens joints and can leave debris that plugs the pipe.

Cause 1: rain getting in

This part is our own explanation, not a quote from a source. Rain entry comes from the top. The usual culprits are a missing or damaged cap on the vent pipe, failed flashing (the sealed metal skirt) where the pipe passes through the roof, a loose storm collar (the ring that sheds water at the pipe), or cracks in the concrete crown on top of a masonry chimney. Water then runs down inside the pipe or chimney and shows up at the lowest opening, often the cleanout or the area around the appliance's draft hood.

Cause 2: flue gas condensation

Burning natural gas makes carbon dioxide and water vapor. In a warm flue the vapor stays a gas and leaves with the exhaust. If the inside wall of the flue is cold enough, some vapor turns to liquid on it. A national laboratory report on venting replacement furnaces and water heaters in masonry chimneys says a condensing furnace is expected to make condensation, so its vent uses corrosion-resistant stainless steel, while a natural-draft water heater has much hotter vent temperatures and gets enough dilution air that its condensation stays minimal. The same report notes that old masonry chimneys are often oversized and are still used for modern natural-draft appliances.

Here is how a furnace upgrade can set this up, and it is our own reasoning. An older furnace used to send a large volume of hot gas up the chimney and kept the masonry warm. A newer high-efficiency furnace vents through plastic pipe out a wall instead. That can leave a small water heater alone in a big, cold, often unlined chimney. The water heater's smaller flow of hot gas has to warm a lot of masonry, so the wall may stay cool enough for its exhaust to condense, and the liquid can rust metal and leave white mineral crust as it dries. We did not find a source for the crust, so treat it as our own field observation.

Telling them apart: weather and run timing

All of these timing clues, including the seasonal ones, are our own reasoning, not from a source:

  • Rain leak: water appears during or after rain or snowmelt, in any season, including a warm day. It can appear when the appliance has not run at all. A streak that starts at the roof, a ceiling stain or wet masonry on the outside of the chimney points here.
  • Condensation: water shows up in cold weather, after the appliance has been running, and may dry off after a long run. Short, repeated runs tend to leave the most water. It can happen on dry, clear, cold days, and tends to fade in warm weather.
  • Both at once: an old chimney can have a cracked crown and a condensation problem. If the timing does not fit one cause cleanly, a pro should look.

A simple log helps: write down the date, the weather, whether the appliance ran in the last hour, and where the water showed. After a few entries the pattern is usually clear. Do not open or remove any pipe to check; use only what you can see.

Our own worked example: how much water does a water heater put up the flue?

Assumptions (ours, not from a source): the fuel is treated as pure methane; the burner input is 40,000 Btu per hour; there are about 1,030 Btu in each cubic foot of gas; the heater fires for a full hour; and we count only water made by burning, not moisture already in the air.

  1. Methane burns as CH4 + 2 O2 gives CO2 + 2 H2O, so each molecule of methane makes two molecules of water.
  2. One pound-mole of methane weighs about 16.04 lb and makes two pound-moles of water, which weigh 2 x 18.02 = 36.03 lb.
  3. Gas near 60 F at normal air pressure takes up roughly 379.5 cubic feet per pound-mole, so 1 cubic foot of methane weighs about 16.04 / 379.5 = 0.0423 lb.
  4. Water per cubic foot burned: 0.0423 x (36.03 / 16.04) = 0.0423 x 2.25 = about 0.095 lb.
  5. Gas burned per hour: 40,000 / 1,030 = 38.8, call it 39 cubic feet.
  6. Water vapor per hour: 39 x 0.095 = about 3.7 lb.
  7. Water weighs about 8.34 lb per gallon, so 3.7 / 8.34 = 0.44, roughly 0.45 gallon of water vapor each running hour.

Most of that leaves as vapor in a hot flue. In a cold, oversized chimney, some share can condense on the wall, which is why even a small water heater can leave a wet cleanout. How much condenses depends on wall temperature, which this example does not estimate.

Why it matters

  • Rusted vent pipe: thin metal pipe can corrode through, leaving holes that let exhaust into the room. The extension fact sheet specifically flags corroded vent pipe.
  • Blocked flue: rust flakes, crumbling liner or masonry debris can fall and plug the passage (our own reasoning). The federal safety agency notes animals and insects can also nest in vents, so a blockage can have more than one source.
  • Carbon monoxide spillage: when exhaust cannot rise, it can spill into the house. The extension fact sheet describes backdrafting down the chimney as dangerous, and the federal environmental agency describes symptoms from mild headache and nausea to dizziness and confusion, which can look like the flu.

For the deeper pieces, see chimney liners for gas and oil heat and the lesson on natural-draft vent connectors, draft hoods and spillage testing.

Safe homeowner checks

  • Look from the ground. With binoculars, see whether the cap is in place, whether the pipe or chimney top looks straight, and whether the crown or flashing shows obvious gaps. Never climb onto the roof or a ladder to look (our own safety advice).
  • Check the CO alarms. The federal consumer product safety agency recommends an alarm in the hallway near bedrooms in each sleeping area, with batteries checked monthly. An extension publication says alarms last about 5 to 7 years under good conditions and should be replaced or recalibrated at 4 to 5 years. A federal environmental agency page says an alarm is a backup, not a substitute for proper appliance use and maintenance.
  • Look, do not touch. Note rust, white crust, wet insulation or loose joints on the visible pipe. The extension fact sheet advises never altering a combustion appliance, its flue or its gas piping from the original installation.
  • Take photos and notes with dates and weather for the pro.

If an alarm sounds and anyone has symptoms, the federal environmental agency says to get them out and seek medical care. If nobody has symptoms, ventilate the home and turn off possible CO sources. Either way, have a technician confirm that nothing blocks the exhaust path.

When to call a pro or a chimney sweep

  • Any CO alarm activation, headaches that ease when you leave the house, soot, or a burnt smell near the appliance.
  • Rust holes, separated pipe sections, or crust that keeps coming back.
  • Water at the cleanout on cold days that matches the appliance running, which suggests condensation: ask for a vent and liner evaluation, since fixes such as a properly sized liner are a contractor's decision.
  • Water after rain with a visible cap, flashing or crown problem: a roofer or chimney sweep can repair what you cannot safely reach.
  • You recently replaced the furnace and the water heater stayed on the old chimney: ask the installer whether that chimney still suits the water heater.

The federal safety agency recommends a yearly professional inspection of fuel-burning heating systems, and the federal environmental agency says to have it done at the beginning of every heating season.

Field card: Dripping or Crusty Flue Pipe

Wet only after rain: look at the top. Wet only while it runs and cold: think condensation.

  • Rain leak: bad cap, flashing, storm collar or crown; shows up with weather, even if the appliance is off.
  • Condensation: cold, oversized or unlined chimney; shows up while the appliance runs, in cold weather.
  • A water heater left alone in a big chimney after a furnace upgrade is a classic setup (our own reasoning).
  • Our own example: a 40,000 Btu per hour heater makes about 3.7 lb (about 0.45 gal) of water vapor per hour.
  • Look from the ground; never climb the roof.
  • Keep working CO alarms; call a pro for any rust hole, blockage or alarm.
