Cooling Tower Cycles of Concentration: Blowdown and Makeup Water Calculations

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A cooling tower makes cold water by letting some of it evaporate, and everything dissolved in that water stays behind. Cycles of concentration is the number that tells you how concentrated the leftovers have become. This lesson shows how to read it, how to do the water balance, and why raising it saves water until chemistry says stop.

Check your own site

Check your own site. Set points, limits and treatment programs are set by the tower maker, the water treatment provider and your written water management plan. This lesson explains the arithmetic and the reasons. It does not set limits. For how the tower itself works, see Cooling Tower Basics.

What cycles of concentration means

Fresh water, called makeup, has some dissolved minerals in it. In the tower, pure water leaves as vapor and the minerals do not, so the loop water gets saltier every hour. Blowdown, which is water deliberately drained from the loop, takes minerals out. Cycles of concentration (cycles, or COC) is the balance point between the two.

The U.S. Department of Energy’s Federal Energy Management Program (FEMP) best management practice for cooling towers describes it as the ratio of dissolved solids in the blowdown water to those in the makeup water. EPA WaterSense gives the field version: conductivity of the blowdown water divided by conductivity of the makeup water. Our own illustration: makeup at 400 µS/cm and loop water at 1,200 µS/cm is 3 cycles. Baltimore Aircoil (BAC) says the same idea works with a single dissolved ion such as chlorides, and its guidelines give an example of a 150 ppm chloride limit with 45 ppm in the makeup, which allows 3.33 cycles. Conductivity is the practical choice because a probe reads it continuously.

FEMP says many systems run at about two to four cycles and that six or more may be possible, depending on the makeup water and the treatment program. One university campus study set a typical target of 4 to 5.

The water balance

Water goes in through the makeup line and leaves in three main ways. FEMP and BAC both state it as makeup equals evaporation plus blowdown plus drift. EPA WaterSense also lists leaks and overflows. Drift is the small amount of liquid that escapes as droplets. EPA WaterSense gives typical drift as 0.05 to 0.2 percent of flow and says modern drift eliminators can bring it below 0.005 percent. BAC treats drift from well-kept high-efficiency eliminators as insignificant, and so do we in the example below. Our drift lesson, Cooling Tower Fan, Fill, Drift and Basin Checks, covers what to look for in person.

Cycles also have a plain flow-based meaning. When drift and leaks are small, cycles equal makeup divided by blowdown, which is how the FEMP fact sheet on cooling towers defines it. So two flow meters let you check the conductivity readings. FEMP says to install meters on the makeup and blowdown lines and that the two ratios should match the target cycles. If they do not, look for leaks or unauthorized draw-off. EPA WaterSense gives a similar flag: a makeup-to-blowdown ratio more than 10 percent higher than the conductivity ratio points to a leak, an overflow or excess drift.

The formulas

BAC’s technical formulas sheet gives these:

  • Evaporation = flow × range × 0.001. The sheet does not print units; we read flow as gpm and range as °F because that matches its rules of thumb below.
  • Blowdown (bleed) = evaporation ÷ (cycles − 1).
  • Makeup = evaporation + blowdown (+ drift when it matters).

The same sheet gives rules of thumb of 3 gpm of evaporation per 100 tons of refrigeration, or 2 gpm per million Btu/h of heat rejected. BAC’s European guidelines give a metric version, 0.0018 times flow times range in °C. A university campus study that tracked real towers wrote makeup as evaporation × cycles ÷ (cycles − 1), which is the same thing after a little algebra.

Worked example: a 500 ton tower

These are Our own numbers, using the cited formulas. We assume the common rating of 3 gpm per ton and a 10°F range (both stated in Cooling Tower Basics), full load, and no drift.

  1. Our own: flow. 500 tons × 3 gpm per ton = 1,500 gpm.
  2. Our own: evaporation. 1,500 gpm × 10°F × 0.001 = 15 gpm. Cross-check with the 3 gpm per 100 tons rule: 500 ÷ 100 × 3 = 15 gpm. They agree.
  3. Our own: 3 cycles. Blowdown = 15 ÷ (3 − 1) = 7.5 gpm. Makeup = 15 + 7.5 = 22.5 gpm.
  4. Our own: 6 cycles. Blowdown = 15 ÷ (6 − 1) = 3.0 gpm. Makeup = 15 + 3 = 18 gpm.
  5. Our own: difference. Makeup drops 22.5 − 18 = 4.5 gpm (20 percent). Blowdown drops 7.5 − 3 = 4.5 gpm (60 percent).
  6. Our own: per 1,000 full-load hours. 4.5 gpm × 60 min × 1,000 h = 270,000 gallons saved.

For scale, evaporation over the same 1,000 hours is 15 × 60 × 1,000 = 900,000 gallons, which no setting removes. Makeup is 1,350,000 gallons at 3 cycles and 1,080,000 at 6. Our 20 percent makeup cut matches the figure in FEMP and EPA WaterSense for going from 3 to 6 cycles. Both also say blowdown falls by 50 percent, where our no-drift formula gives 60. The sources do not show how they got 50, so treat the formula as the cleaner calculation and the published number as an example.

Notice the diminishing return. Going from 2 to 3 cycles cuts blowdown from 15 to 7.5 gpm, a saving of 7.5 gpm. Going from 3 to 6 saves 4.5 gpm more. Going from 6 to 12 saves only 3.0 − 1.36 = about 1.6 gpm more (Our own).

Where the limit comes from

Water can hold only so much of a mineral. The FEMP fact sheet says every dissolved mineral has a saturation limit, and past it scale forms. Hardness (calcium and magnesium) is the usual troublemaker, because these minerals become less soluble as water warms, so deposits show up on the hot surfaces: heat exchangers and the top of the fill. FEMP’s best management practice adds that if hardness limits the cycles, softening the makeup is an option. BAC’s guidelines take a middle road, asking for a minimum makeup hardness and, when softening is used, blending back to a residual rather than using fully softened water. They also list limits for chlorides, sulfates and conductivity that depend on the tower’s materials, and say to calculate cycles for every parameter and use the lowest result. The sources we read gave no silica number, so we give none; your treatment provider tests for it.

In short, the highest allowed cycles is whichever limit is hit first: hardness, chlorides, sulfates, silica, the conductivity limit for the materials, or the discharge quality your sewer or permit allows. EPA WaterSense reminds readers that the blowdown still has to meet allowable discharge standards. Ecolab’s literature describes polymers that keep scale-forming minerals dissolved as cycles rise, but a treatment program is a design decision for a qualified provider.

Controllers, valves and meters

FEMP says to install a conductivity controller to control blowdown automatically, so the valve opens only when conductivity passes the set point. EPA WaterSense says the set point comes from the maximum cycles the tower can sustain, usually in µS/cm, and that meters on makeup and blowdown can often be installed for under $1,000. The FEMP fact sheet adds that controllers often take water meter inputs and alarm outputs, and that they hold conductivity closer to the target than manual blowdown. A university study found a malfunctioning blowdown control during a review of its towers, so a stuck valve or a dirty probe is a real thing to look for.

A technician’s check: compare the controller display with the logged reading, confirm the valve actually opens and closes, and confirm the meters count. Calibration and set points belong to the water treatment provider.

Sewer credits

Evaporated water does not go down the drain, yet the building pays sewer charges on all the water it buys. FEMP and EPA WaterSense both suggest asking the water utility whether it gives a sewer deduction for evaporation. EPA says the credit is based on metered makeup minus blowdown. Rules differ by utility and neither source gives an amount, so ask yours.

Water treatment and Legionella

Cycles are a treatment decision. Water held at higher cycles is stronger, and the corrosion, scale and microbial control has to be designed for the chosen cycles. BAC’s guidelines say the treatment regime must also meet local Legionella requirements, with weekly dip-slide bacteria testing recommended and results recorded. See Cooling Tower Water Management and Legionella Awareness and the plain-English guide to the NJ law. Saving water is never a reason to skip blowdown that the treatment plan calls for or to ignore a logged conductivity alarm.

Field card: Cooling Tower Cycles of Concentration

Cycles = blowdown conductivity ÷ makeup conductivity.

  • Makeup = evaporation + blowdown + drift (and leaks).
  • Evaporation (BAC) = gpm × range °F × 0.001, about 3 gpm per 100 tons.
  • Blowdown = evaporation ÷ (cycles − 1).
  • 500 tons, 10°F range: 15 gpm evaporation; 3 cycles = 7.5 blowdown, 22.5 makeup; 6 cycles = 3 blowdown, 18 makeup (Our own, no drift).
  • Moving 3 to 6 cycles saves about 270,000 gallons per 1,000 full-load hours (Our own).
  • Limits: hardness, chlorides, silica, materials, discharge rules. Ask the treatment provider.
  • Meters on makeup and blowdown should match the target cycles.
