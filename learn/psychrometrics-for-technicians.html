Psychrometrics for Technicians

Last updated: 2026-10-16

Psychrometrics is the study of moist air. It sounds like a mouthful, but it answers everyday questions: why a cold glass sweats, why a coil drips, and why a house can feel clammy at a comfortable temperature.

Air always holds some water vapor

The air around us is a mixture of dry air and water vapor. How much vapor it holds can be described in several ways, and each one is useful for something different. The ones technicians use most are dry-bulb temperature, wet-bulb temperature, dew point and relative humidity.

The four terms

  • Dry-bulb temperature. What an ordinary thermometer reads, shaded from direct sun. When people say temperature, this is it.
  • Wet-bulb temperature. What a thermometer reads when its bulb is wet and air moves quickly over it. Evaporation cools the bulb. Drier air allows more evaporation, so the wet-bulb reading drops further below dry-bulb. In saturated air the two are equal.
  • Dew point. The temperature at which the air, cooled with its moisture unchanged, becomes saturated. Cool below it and water starts to condense.
  • Relative humidity. How much vapor is in the air compared to the most it could hold at that temperature, as a percentage.

Why relative humidity can fool you

Warm air can hold more vapor than cool air. If the moisture stays the same and the air warms up, relative humidity falls, yet the dew point does not change. If the air cools, relative humidity rises. This is why dew point is a better measure of how much moisture is really in the air. A high dew point means a lot of moisture, whatever the thermometer says.

Why surfaces sweat

When humid air touches a surface colder than its dew point, vapor condenses into liquid water on that surface. If the surface is below freezing, it forms frost. Very humid air only needs a surface a few degrees cooler than the air to condense on it. A drier mix needs a much colder surface.

Why a cooling coil sweats on purpose

An air conditioning coil runs colder than the dew point of the air passing over it. As a result the coil removes heat from the air and also pulls water out of it. That water is carried away by the condensate drain, and the heat removed with it is the latent load. The heat you can measure with a thermometer is called sensible heat. Cooling really removes both, and comfort depends on both. Too little airflow can make the coil colder and wetter, and too much can leave humidity behind.

The psychrometric chart

A psychrometric chart is a graph showing the properties of moist air at one pressure. Dry-bulb temperature runs across the bottom and moisture content runs up the side. If you know any two of the properties, plus pressure, you can find the others on the chart. You do not need to memorize it. Learn to read a point and follow a line as the air is cooled, heated or humidified.

Where this shows up in the field

  • Sweating supply ducts, grilles and refrigerant lines.
  • A cold space that still feels sticky after a short cycle from an oversized unit.
  • Frozen coils where cold air and moisture meet poor airflow.
  • Condensate drain overflows.
  • Mold risk in damp spaces.

Field card: Psychrometrics for Technicians

Moist air in four numbers.

  • Dry-bulb is the ordinary air temperature.
  • Wet-bulb falls below dry-bulb as air gets drier.
  • Dew point shows true moisture content, relative humidity does not.
  • Any surface colder than the dew point will collect condensation.
  • A cooling coil removes sensible and latent heat, and the water goes to the drain.
