Sweating Toilet Tank and Dripping Cold Pipes: Condensation or a Leak?

Last updated: 2026-11-04

On a muggy July day the toilet tank is wet, the cold water pipe under the sink drips, and there is a puddle on the floor. Most of the time nothing is broken. Water vapor from the room air is turning into liquid on a cold surface. This lesson shows why that happens, how to tell it from a real leak, and what actually fixes it.

Why a cold tank sweats

Air always carries some water vapor. Warm air can hold more of it than cool air. The dew point is the temperature at which a given batch of air is fully loaded; cool it below that and the vapor has to come out as liquid. A federal weather service page explains dew point as the temperature air must be cooled to, at constant pressure, to reach 100 percent relative humidity, and notes that relative humidity alone can mislead because it depends on temperature too.

A toilet tank is a ceramic box full of water that arrives from a cold supply line, often much colder than the room in summer. The ceramic wall sits between the cold water and the warm room, so its outer surface ends up somewhere in the middle. If that outer surface is colder than the dew point of the room air, the thin layer of air touching it is cooled below its dew point and droplets form. The same thing happens on a cold glass of water and on bare cold supply pipes. A federal environmental agency guide to mold and moisture lists condensation on windows, walls or pipes as a sign of high humidity.

Worked example (our own)

This example is our own. It uses a standard dew point approximation for dew point, which is accurate to a degree or so in ordinary indoor conditions. The assumptions are ours, not measurements from a particular house.

  • Assumed room air: 78 F. In Celsius that is (78 - 32) / 1.8 = 25.6 C.
  • Assumed tank outer surface: about 58 F in summer. This is an assumption; the real value depends on supply water temperature, how long the water has sat, and how much humid air is moving past.
  • Formula: with a = 17.62 and b = 243.12 C, first compute g = ln(RH) + a x T / (b + T), then dew point = b x g / (a - g). RH is entered as a fraction.

Case 1, 60 percent RH. a x T / (b + T) = 17.62 x 25.56 / (243.12 + 25.56) = 450.4 / 268.68 = 1.676. ln(0.60) = -0.511. So g = 1.676 - 0.511 = 1.165. Dew point = 243.12 x 1.165 / (17.62 - 1.165) = 283.2 / 16.455 = 17.2 C, which is about 63 F. The tank surface at 58 F is about 5 F below the dew point, so the tank sweats.

Case 2, 40 percent RH. ln(0.40) = -0.916, so g = 1.676 - 0.916 = 0.760. Dew point = 243.12 x 0.760 / (17.62 - 0.760) = 184.8 / 16.86 = 10.96 C, which is about 52 F. The tank surface at 58 F is about 6 F above the dew point, so it stays dry.

Check. The room temperature is the same in both cases, so the whole difference is moisture. A quick check of the crossover: at 50 percent RH the same formula gives a dew point near 58 F, right at our assumed tank temperature. So in this example the tank starts to sweat once the room climbs past about 50 percent RH. That sits near the middle of the 30 to 50 percent range that the federal moisture guide gives as a target for indoor humidity. A colder tank would need even drier air.

Condensation or leak? Two simple checks

These checks are our own, built from common sense, and need nothing but paper and a few drops of dye.

  • Dry-paper check. Wipe the tank, the supply line and the floor completely dry. Tuck a strip of dry paper towel under the tank edge, around the shutoff valve and at each pipe joint. Check after 30 to 60 minutes. Wet paper everywhere along a cold surface, worse in the muggiest hours, points to condensation. A single spot that keeps getting wet, even on a dry day, points to a leak at that spot.
  • Food-coloring check. Put several drops of food coloring in the tank water (the tank, not the bowl). Wipe the outside dry and wait. Colored water on the outside or on the floor means the water is coming from inside the tank, such as a bolt or gasket leak. Clear water that tracks the humidity is condensation. If color shows up in the bowl without flushing, the flap at the bottom of the tank is leaking, which is a separate problem from sweating.

Also look at where the water is. Beads all over the outside of the tank and along the full length of a cold pipe are condensation. Water at the base of the toilet or from a joint is more likely a leak. Water at the shutoff valve or supply connection is a leak. If you are unsure, leave it to a plumber.

What the water does to the floor

A little sweat that dries is harmless. A daily puddle is not. Water can soak flooring, seep through gaps into the subfloor, and stain the ceiling below. Look for floor tile that lifts, vinyl that bubbles, a soft spot when you press with a screwdriver handle, or a musty smell. The federal mold cleanup guide says to fix plumbing leaks and water problems as soon as possible and to dry items completely, and it warns that absorbent materials such as carpet may have to be thrown away if they become moldy. Mold on a hard surface can often be cleaned, but a soft or stained subfloor is a job for a contractor. Mats and rugs around a toilet that stay damp are an easy place for growth to start.

Fixes, from easiest to a plumber's job

  1. Bring humidity down first. The federal moisture guide says to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, and says an inexpensive humidity meter can measure it. Our example shows why: the tank in our example sweats at 60 percent and not at 40.
  2. Use the bathroom exhaust fan. The guide says to run the fan or open a window while showering. Turn it on before the shower and leave it running for a while afterward. See the bathroom fan lesson below for sizing and run times.
  3. Close the bathroom door during long showers so steam does not spread through the house, and open it only after the fan has pulled the room down.
  4. Run the air conditioner or a dehumidifier. The guide lists air conditioners and dehumidifiers as ways to bring humidity down when needed, and says to keep drip pans clean and drain lines open. Cooling air removes moisture, but an oversized unit that cycles off quickly may not dry the air well; see the related lessons.
  5. Insulate exposed cold pipes. The guide says to cover cold surfaces such as cold water pipes with insulation. Foam sleeves must be closed at the seams so humid air cannot reach the cold metal. The pipe insulation lesson below covers materials and when not to insulate.
  6. Ask a plumber about the tank itself. Two options exist. A tank liner is a foam layer fitted inside the tank to keep the cold water away from the ceramic wall. An anti-sweat mixing valve blends a little warm water into the fill line so the tank water is less cold. Both involve opening the tank or changing plumbing, so we leave them to a plumber. Check local plumbing rules before changing a supply line.

When to call for help

Call a plumber if the paper check shows a single wet spot, if the food coloring shows up outside the tank, or if the water keeps returning on dry days. Call a contractor if the subfloor is soft or a ceiling below is stained. If humidity stays high everywhere in the house even with the fan and AC running, an HVAC technician can check the cooling system.

Field card: Sweating Toilet Tank and Cold Pipes

Sweat means the surface is colder than the room's dew point.

  • Our example: 78 F air has a dew point near 63 F at 60 percent RH and near 52 F at 40 percent RH.
  • A 58 F tank surface sweats at 60 percent and stays dry at 40 percent (our assumptions).
  • Dry paper and food coloring tell condensation from a leak.
  • Fan, closed door, AC or dehumidifier, and pipe insulation lower the moisture.
  • Tank liners and anti-sweat mixing valves are plumber jobs.
  • Soft floors or musty smells need a contractor.
