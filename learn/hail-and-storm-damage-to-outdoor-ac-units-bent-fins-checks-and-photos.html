Hail and Storm Damage to Outdoor AC Units: Bent Fins, Checks and Photos

Last updated: 2026-10-16

After a hailstorm the outdoor unit often looks dented and a little ugly, and the owner wonders whether it still works. Mostly the damage is to the thin metal fins on the coil. This lesson covers what hail does to those fins, what you can safely look at yourself, what a fin comb does, what hail guards are, and how to photograph damage in an orderly way.

What hail does to the coil

The outdoor coil is a grid of copper or aluminum tube with very thin aluminum fins pressed onto it. Air passes between the fins, and the fins carry heat from the refrigerant into that air. A forensic engineer writing about hail says fins are thin enough that impact, such as a hailstorm, can easily damage them, and that owners may not notice until the power bill rises. The equipment maker MRCOOL's support page says bent or compressed fins restrict airflow across the coil surface, which reduces heat exchange and makes the compressor work harder. Carrier's owner booklet says "do not damage or bend coil fins" and that bent fins may affect operation. Flying branches and thrown lawn debris can do the same thing as hail.

How much does it matter? The best published measurement we found is a laboratory study by Sitzmann, Lu and Smith in ASHRAE Transactions (2010). They flattened fin areas on the condensers of two residential split systems. The abstract says performance first dropped appreciably when 60 percent of the fin area was flattened. With 100 percent flattened, one system lost 27 percent of its capacity and 34 percent of its SEER. Combing the fins back out restored capacity to within 1 percent and SEER to within 6 percent of the undented condition, and the second unit gave similar results. Those are lab numbers on two units, so treat them as a rough picture, not a promise for your unit.

Safe checks for an owner

Start from a distance and stay outside the cabinet. Carrier's owner booklet says to turn off power to both the indoor and outdoor units before any maintenance, and that there may be more than one disconnect. It also says everything beyond the simple owner tasks must be done by a qualified service technician.

  • Look first. Is the unit standing level? Are the refrigerant lines (the insulated and bare copper pair) kinked, pulled loose or crushed? Is the disconnect box cracked, and are wires cut, chewed or hanging? Is the fan guard dented into the fan?
  • If lines, wiring or the disconnect look damaged, do not run it. Trane warns that flying debris can damage a unit and that turning it on can make the problem worse. Trane also says wind can loosen internal connections, which may cause refrigerant leaks, and that a storm-damaged system should be checked by an HVAC professional. Leave the power off and call for service.
  • Look at the fins. In our own experience, dents in a few spots are common. Large flattened areas or fins crushed against the tubes need a professional's eye. See the example below for a way to size what you see.
  • Leave the tools in the drawer. Do not pry at the coil with a screwdriver, and do not pressure-wash it. Carrier's booklet says no high-pressure water or harsh chemicals on coils. For gentle cleaning of dirt, see Cleaning an Outdoor AC Condenser Coil.

For storm steps before and after a big weather event, including flood and generator cautions, read Getting Your HVAC Ready for a Hurricane or Nor'easter.

Fin combs and straightening

A fin comb is a small tool with teeth that slide between the fins so a technician can pull bent fins back into line. MRCOOL's support page calls combing the standard way to correct bent fins, and says the comb has to match the coil's fins per inch (FPI) rating so the teeth line up. It adds that excessively damaged sections may need coil replacement instead of straightening, and recommends that a licensed technician do the work. MRCOOL also suggests that if you are unsure, you send the maker clear photos and the model number before attempting repairs. We found no maker page that tells owners to comb fins themselves, so this lesson does not teach the technique. A wrong-size comb can make things worse.

Combing helps airflow and heat transfer, but it cannot fix a leaking tube. If a fin is bent hard enough to dent or cut the tube beneath it, that is a refrigerant leak, which is technician-only work.

Hail guards

A hail guard is a louvered or mesh panel that sits over the coil. Carrier's installation guide for its accessory kits says louvered hail guards are designed to protect the condenser coil from hail and general contact damage. Lennox's guard manual describes a panel placed on the intake side of the condensing unit, and says to use extreme care not to damage the coil, with two people needed to install it. Trane's guard instructions say the panel must cover the entire coil opening. Carrier warns never to drill or drive screws through the top cover, because coil damage could occur, and to turn off power and lock out the unit first.

What about airflow? Any panel in front of a coil is something the air must pass through. The maker documents we read, from Carrier, Lennox and Trane, give no airflow or capacity figure for their guards, so we will not state one. Ask the installer or the unit's maker how a specific guard affects airflow and ratings on your model, and ask whether it is an approved accessory.

Documenting damage with dated photos

This is general information, not advice about any insurer or policy. The New Jersey Department of Banking and Insurance, in its storm-claims guidance, says to photograph damaged property before you throw any of it away, to report the loss promptly and have your policy number ready, to make necessary temporary repairs to prevent further loss, and to keep every receipt. It also says to hold on to damaged property until the insurer has a chance to inspect it, and to record your claim number and adjuster's contact details. The same department's home insurance page adds that claim requirements vary by company, so ask which documents and forms yours needs, and that permanent repairs should wait until the adjuster has reviewed the damage. Other states publish similar pages, so check yours.

Our own advice, a simple routine for an outdoor unit:

  1. Photograph the whole yard side first, showing the unit and where hail or debris landed.
  2. Take a wide shot of each side of the unit, then close-ups of damaged fins, the fan guard, the lines and the disconnect. Put a coin or tape measure in a few frames for scale.
  3. Photograph the nameplate (model and serial number) so the unit is identified.
  4. Let your phone stamp the date and time, and keep the original files. Write the date and what you did next in a note.
  5. Keep receipts for any inspection, temporary repair or part.

When a technician should test performance

Call one when the unit was hit and any check above turned up damage, or if cooling seems weaker or the bill rises after a storm. Hidden problems, such as a loosened connection or a slow refrigerant leak, will not show in a photo. A technician can open the unit, inspect the coil and fan, and judge whether combing is enough. For how a technician reads condenser airflow problems on the gauges, see Dirty Condenser and Head Pressure. Ask the technician to write down what was measured and what was found. That report can help with a claim, though the insurer decides what it covers.

Worked example: how much of the face is flattened?

These numbers are made up to show the arithmetic. Suppose the visible coil face is 36 in. wide and 28 in. tall, and you can see two flattened patches (see the figure).

  1. Coil face area: 36 × 28 = 1,008 square inches.
  2. Patch A: 14 × 12 = 168 square inches.
  3. Patch B: 6 × 9 = 54 square inches.
  4. Flattened total: 168 + 54 = 222 square inches.
  5. Share of the face: 222 ÷ 1,008 = 0.2202, about 22 percent.

Label: this is a flattened-area share, not an airflow loss. The sources we read give no formula that converts a percent of flattened face into a percent of airflow, so we do not make one up. The only comparison we can fairly make is to the lab abstract above, where performance first dropped appreciably at 60 percent of fin area flattened: 22 is well below 60. That tells you the damage in this example is much smaller than the lab's threshold, but it does not mean the unit is fine. Your coil may wrap around two or three sides, so measure each face and add the areas, and the tube or line damage that a tape measure cannot see still needs a technician.

Field card: Hail and Storm Damage to Outdoor AC Units

Look, photograph, leave it off, call.

  • Power off at every disconnect before touching or cleaning anything.
  • Bent lines, cut wires or a cracked disconnect: do not run the unit.
  • Flattened fins restrict airflow; a lab study found a clear drop at 60 percent of fin area flattened.
  • Fin combing is technician work; the comb must match the coil's FPI.
  • Hail guards: ask the maker about airflow and ratings for your model.
  • Photograph first, keep receipts and damaged parts, report promptly.
  • Area share = flattened area ÷ face area (222 ÷ 1,008 is about 22 percent in the example).
