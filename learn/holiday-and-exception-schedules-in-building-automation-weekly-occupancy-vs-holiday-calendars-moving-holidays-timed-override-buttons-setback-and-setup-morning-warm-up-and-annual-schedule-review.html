Holiday and Exception Schedules in Building Automation

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A building automation system can run a perfect weekly schedule and still waste fan hours every holiday, because nobody told it the building is empty. This lesson covers the layer that sits on top of the weekly schedule: holiday and exception calendars, after-hours override buttons, setback setpoints and the checks that keep all of it honest.

Weekly schedule versus exception calendar

The weekly occupancy schedule is the repeating pattern: for example, occupied 6:00 to 18:00 Monday through Friday, unoccupied the rest of the time. The exception calendar is a separate list of dates that replace the normal pattern for that day. Most systems give the exception list higher priority than the weekly schedule, so a holiday entry wins on its date and the weekly pattern comes back the next day. A timed override from a room sensor sits above both and expires on its own. The diagram shows that order of priority. Controllers differ, so read the priority rules for the system you are working on instead of assuming.

One-day, date-range and moving holidays

An exception can be a single date, such as the day after a holiday that the owner gives as a bonus day off, or a range, such as a summer shutdown week or a long closure for renovation. Fixed-date holidays only need to be entered once if the system supports yearly repeats. Holidays that move, such as those defined as "the third Monday" or "the last Thursday" of a month, are the common trap. A system that only repeats by calendar date will put them on the wrong day every year after the first. Check whether the controller can define a holiday by weekday-and-week-of-month, and if it cannot, enter the actual dates for the next year or two and set a reminder to renew the list. A national lab building tuning guide says to check whether federal holidays are programmed into the scheduler and add any that are missing, and it notes that buildings that run around the clock or do not change operation on holidays can skip this step.

Also list the days that are quiet for your own building even if they are not public holidays: a seasonal break at a training center, a plant shutdown week, or a campus closure. Ask the building staff which days the building is actually empty. The calendar that matters is the occupancy calendar, not the printed one.

After-hours requests and timed overrides

When someone wants cooling at 8 p.m., the usual tool is a button or a software command on a room sensor that forces occupied mode for a set time. Two things go wrong. If the override has no limit, or a very long one, a single request can keep a large air handler running all night. And if people hit the button repeatedly "to be safe," the building runs after hours on most evenings. A federal energy-laboratory report on using analytics in commercial buildings describes a case where excessive after-hours use traced to a cleaning crew's override. Notifying the crew only partly reduced the load, and a repeating monthly event later raised the expected after-hours load. The system was then programmed to limit override times, and the waste was eliminated.

How long should an override last? A small-building scheduling guide from a national lab gives an example setting that lets occupants adjust the setpoint by up to 5 degrees F with the override holding for 4 hours, then resetting to the building policy. That is an example, not a rule. Our own starting suggestion, which you should adjust to the building: pick an override length that covers the typical real need (an evening meeting, a cleaning shift), such as 1 to 2 hours, let people extend it deliberately, and make sure it counts down and cancels itself. Then review how often it fires. Frequent overrides on the same zones usually mean the weekly schedule is wrong, not that people are careless.

Setback and setup setpoints

Unoccupied heating and cooling setpoints are often called setback (heating lowered) and setup (cooling raised). The national lab scheduling guide lists starting points for small and medium offices of 65 degrees F heating and 80 degrees F cooling when unoccupied, and 70 heating and 75 cooling when occupied, and says to adjust them with occupant feedback. A national lab building tuning guide suggests average-zone night setback options of 60 to 65 degrees F heating and 80 degrees F cooling, and a worst-case option of 55 to 60 heating and 85 cooling. It also warns that more aggressive night setback values may require earlier morning start-up. The wider the gap, the longer recovery takes. In very cold or hot climates, another federal lab report advises setback rather than full shutoff because recovery from a cold or hot soak needs substantial energy or can fail to finish. For hot water plants and slow, heavy buildings, see Setpoint Scheduling and Optimal Start Basics.

Morning warm-up after a long weekend

A three-day weekend means the structure has been at setback for about 72 hours, not 48. Slab and masonry cooled further, so the same start time that worked on a normal Monday may leave the building cold at opening. The tuning guide says that without optimal start, schedules should begin 1 to 2 hours before occupancy and that some buildings need longer. It also says that with optimal start, the earliest allowed start can be 1 to 2 hours earlier than the historical start when night setback is aggressive, and that you should check that most zones are within 1 to 2 degrees F of setpoint 30 minutes before occupancy. If the answer after a long weekend is no, widen the earliest-start limit or soften the setback for holiday periods. Keep outdoor air dampers closed during warm-up, as that guide recommends, so you are not heating outdoor air for an empty building.

Checking schedules after a controller replacement or clock error

Schedules and exception lists live in the controller or the server, and a swapped controller, a restored backup or a lost clock can quietly bring back a default calendar. Our own checklist, based on general practice:

  • Before the work, print or export the weekly schedule, the exception list and the override settings.
  • After the work, confirm the controller date, time and time zone, and that daylight saving handling matches the others.
  • Compare the new schedule to the exported one, line by line, including holiday dates and the override length.
  • Watch for a "temporary" setback or schedule disable that is never turned back on. The federal lab analytics report above describes a setback control that was disabled for special meetings and never re-enabled, and re-enabling it significantly reduced nighttime load.
  • Trend the equipment status for a week or more, and confirm the fans stop overnight and on the next exception date. The tuning guide says to use trend data to confirm equipment shuts down overnight.

For trend logs and alarms, see BAS Alarms, Trend Logs and How Techs Use Them, and for equipment left in hand, Hand-Off-Auto Switches and BAS Operator Overrides.

Worked example (our own, with assumed numbers)

These inputs are assumptions for teaching, not measurements from a real building.

  • Supply fan electrical input: 15 kW (assumed, treated as constant).
  • Normal weekday run: 12 hours (assumed).
  • Holidays that fall on weekdays and were missing from the calendar: 10 (assumed).
  • After-hours override requests: 150 per year (assumed), each running its full timer.
  • Override timer before the change: 4 hours. After the change: 2 hours.

Holiday hours saved: 10 days × 12 h = 120 h.

Override hours saved: 150 requests × (4 h − 2 h) = 150 × 2 = 300 h.

Total fan hours saved: 120 + 300 = 420 h per year. For scale, a year has 8,760 h, so this is about 4.8 percent of the hours.

Fan energy saved: 420 h × 15 kW = 6,300 kWh per year.

Check: 15 kW × 120 h = 1,800 kWh and 15 kW × 300 h = 4,500 kWh, and 1,800 + 4,500 = 6,300 kWh. The result counts fan energy only. Heating, cooling and pump savings are extra and depend on the plant. If the fan has a variable speed drive running slowly, the real number is lower. If some of those 150 requests were truly needed for the full 4 hours, people will extend the override, and the saving shrinks.

Review schedules each year with the building staff

Set a yearly date, ideally in the fall before the heating season and before the next year's holidays, to sit down with the people who use the building. Bring the exported schedule, the exception list and a trend of after-hours run time. Ask what has changed: new tenants, new shifts, a closed wing, a new event schedule. The small-building guide recommends quarterly reviews to confirm schedules match occupancy, which is a good habit when the use of the building changes often. Update next year's moving holidays, check the override length against how it is actually used, and write the changes down.

Field card: Holiday and Exception Schedules

Weekly pattern, exception on top, override on the very top and it must expire.

  • Enter holidays, bonus days and shutdown ranges; moving holidays need new dates each year unless the system defines them by weekday.
  • Keep override timers short and self-cancelling; many overrides on one zone means the weekly schedule is wrong.
  • Setback and setup widen savings but lengthen recovery; allow earlier start after long weekends.
  • After a controller swap or clock fault: check date, time, calendar, overrides, and trend the fans.
  • Worked example (ours, assumed): 420 h x 15 kW = 6,300 kWh per year.
  • Review every year with the building staff.
