Air-to-Water Heat Pumps for Homes: Replacing a Boiler on Existing Baseboard

Last updated: 2026-10-16

An air-to-water heat pump can take the place of an oil or gas boiler, but it does not behave like one. It makes cooler water, its output drops as the weather does, and old baseboard was sized for much hotter water. This lesson shows how to check whether a house can make the switch.

How it works: monobloc or split

The unit pulls heat from outdoor air with a refrigeration circuit and hands it to a water loop. A California design guide for these systems describes two layouts. In a monobloc, the refrigerant-to-water heat exchanger sits inside the outdoor unit, so only water pipes enter the house. In a split system the heat exchanger is indoors, and the refrigerant lines and heat exchanger are charged with refrigerant on site. The same guide says splits remove the risk of freeze damage to outdoor pipes, while monobloc units can be protected with an antifreeze solution.

Water temperature is the limit

A New York state savings white paper says these products typically run leaving water temperatures from 90 to 140 F. It lists typical supply temperatures by emitter: 90 to 100 F for a premium radiant floor, 120 to 130 F for baseboard paired with an air handler, 140 to 150 F for modified baseboard, and 160 F or more for conventional baseboard, which it says most heat pumps cannot supply. A boiler loop often runs at 180 F, so a straight swap leaves a gap. The design guide recommends 120 F as a heating supply target.

Two rules of thumb from that white paper show why the target matters. Its sensitivity slopes are about 1.5 percent more COP per degree F of warmer outdoor air and about 1.1 percent less COP per degree F of hotter leaving water. Our own arithmetic: water 20 F cooler is worth roughly 22 percent in COP, and outdoor air 10 F colder costs roughly 15 percent. Capacity falls too. The federal ENERGY STAR cold-climate criteria for air-to-air heat pumps ask for at least 70 percent of the 47 F heating capacity at 5 F. That is a label minimum for a different product type, so use it only as a feel for the trend and read the maker's water-side capacity table for the real unit. Defrost takes more: the white paper estimates a 7.5 to 11 percent penalty in cold weather.

Can the existing emitters do it?

The test compares two numbers: the room's design heat loss and what the emitters give off at the water temperature you can actually make. Fin-tube output is rated per foot of active element, not per foot of cover. A U.S. Department of Energy Building America report on residential hydronics prints rated outputs versus average water temperature (its Table 15, from 2009 industry ratings). For a single-element fin-tube row it lists 524 Btu/h per foot at 180 F, 408 at 160 F, 311 at 140 F, 208 at 120 F. The same report found upstairs baseboards in one house delivering only about 50 percent of rated output, so measure real length and look for air or poor flow before trusting a rating. Radiators and panels follow the same idea, but get their numbers from the maker's table. See Heat Emitters: Baseboard and Output vs. Water Temperature.

Worked example (our own)

These inputs are assumptions we made up for teaching: a house with a design heat loss of 24,000 Btu/h at 10 F outdoors and 70 F indoors, 60 ft of active fin-tube measured room by room, output taken from the single-element row above, load rising in straight proportion to the indoor-outdoor temperature difference, and a 10 F water drop, so supply is 5 F above the average water temperature.

  1. At 180 F average water: 60 ft x 524 = 31,440 Btu/h, which is 131 percent of the 24,000 load. The old boiler system had room to spare.
  2. At 130 F average, we interpolate halfway between the 120 F and 140 F table values: (208 + 311) / 2 = 259.5 Btu/h per foot. So 60 x 259.5 = 15,570 Btu/h, only 65 percent of the load. Supply would be about 135 F.
  3. What the design day needs: 24,000 / 60 ft = 400 Btu/h per foot. Interpolating between 140 F (311) and 160 F (408) gives 140 + 20 x (400 - 311) / (408 - 311) = about 158 F average, roughly 163 F supply. That is beyond the 140 F end of the white paper's range.
  4. Where 130 F carries the house: 15,570 = 24,000 x (70 - T) / 60 gives T = about 31 F. Above that outdoor temperature the baseboard alone is enough. Below it there is a shortfall, 8,430 Btu/h at 10 F (about 2.5 kW).

Decision: as it stands, this house does not work on the design day with a heat pump alone, but it works above about 31 F. There are three fixes, which can be mixed. Add emitters: 24,000 / 259.5 = 92.5 ft, about 33 ft more. Cut the load by about 35 percent (8,430 / 24,000) with air sealing and insulation. Or keep backup heat for the cold hours. How much of the season falls below 31 F depends on local weather data we did not use, so a real job needs a bin-hour check.

Buffer tank and minimum flow

The design guide says buffers should always be used with single-speed and two-speed units to limit compressor short cycling. The white paper adds that variable-speed units may or may not need one. The guide's minimum-volume equation is gallons = (minimum heat pump capacity in Btu/h x minimum run time in minutes) / (60 x temperature swing in F x 8.3). Our example: assume 10,000 Btu/h minimum output, 5 minute run time and a 10 F swing. 10,000 x 5 / (60 x 10 x 8.3) = about 10 gallons. The guide defers to the maker's minimum volume if it is larger. For flow, the white paper uses about 3 gpm per installed ton and notes that a standard four-port tank can deliver water up to 10 F cooler than the heat pump's leaving temperature. Our arithmetic: that tank penalty can wipe out the margin in the 130 F case, so ask how the tank is piped.

Freeze protection

The design guide says a 10 percent propylene glycol solution protects down to 26 F, and that a glycol loop needs pumping about 2 percent higher than plain water. Ten percent is clearly not enough for a 10 F night, so the percentage must come from the maker and the glycol data. The guide also says insulated short outdoor runs may not need antifreeze for the piping. We did not find a government source on freeze valves or drain-down procedures, so follow the maker's installation manual.

Backup heat and outdoor reset

The design guide says backup heat is better put on the water side than the air side, as gas or electric, and says electric resistance is not needed when the unit is properly sized. If the old boiler stays, protect it. A Building America report warns that older boilers may not tolerate low return temperatures for long, because flue gas condensation can corrode the heat exchanger. Outdoor reset helps the heat pump: the white paper's example of a 130 F design with a 40 F reset range raises seasonal COP about 16 percent, and it uses 1.1 for a typical job. See Outdoor Reset and Weather Compensation and Sizing a Replacement Hot-Water Boiler.

Domestic hot water

The design guide says heat pump domestic water is usually limited to about 125 F, so an indirect tank needs a large coil surface, since the water coming from the heat pump is cooler than a boiler's. It notes a larger storage volume helps and prefers tank-within-a-tank designs. The white paper leaves hot water out of its scope. For hotter or faster recovery, ask the maker.

Field card: Air-to-Water Heat Pumps for Homes

Check the emitters before you pick the unit.

  • Typical heat pump water range: 90 to 140 F. Conventional baseboard wants 160 F or more.
  • Measure active fin length, then compare rated Btu/h per foot at the planned water temperature with room heat loss.
  • Our example: 130 F average gave about 49 percent of the 180 F output; fine above about 31 F outdoors, short on the design day.
  • Fixes: more emitters, a smaller load, or backup heat on the water side.
  • Use a buffer for single-speed units; check how it is piped.
  • Glycol percentage and freeze rules come from the maker. Ten percent protects only to 26 F.
  • Heat pump hot water is usually limited to about 125 F.
