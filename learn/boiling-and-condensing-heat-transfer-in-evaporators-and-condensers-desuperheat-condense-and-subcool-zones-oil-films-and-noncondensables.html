Boiling and Condensing Heat Transfer in Evaporators and Condensers

Last updated: 2026-11-02

A condenser coil is not one thing. Along the tube the refrigerant is a hot gas, then a gas-and-liquid mix, then a liquid, and the film of refrigerant against the tube wall behaves differently in each stage. This lesson explains why the phase-change stage is so effective, where a coil is weak, and how oil, air and dry-out make it worse.

Where this fits

We already cover sensible vs latent heat and the readings in superheat and subcooling basics, so we do not repeat them. Here the question is different: how fast does heat cross from the refrigerant to the tube wall in each part of the coil, and what changes that?

Why boiling and condensing films work so well

Heat leaving or entering a coil passes through three resistances in series: the refrigerant-side film, the metal, and the air-side film. In a university lecture chapter on evaporators and condensers, the authors state that the two-phase heat transfer coefficient can be up to twenty times larger than that for a single-phase liquid flow, and that condensation coefficients are much higher than for single-phase convective heat transfer. The same chapter calls the coefficient for the desuperheating vapor low. They do not give a number for it, and neither do we.

The reason is mechanical, and we can say it without any coefficient. Open lecture notes on boiling from a university nuclear engineering department explain that bubbles forming and leaving the wall stir the fluid near the surface, and that this agitation is the main reason boiling beats natural convection. In the forced-flow regime, heat crosses a thin liquid film and evaporates at its surface. A lot of energy leaves with each pound that changes phase, so the fluid right at the wall stays near one temperature while taking in heat. Dry gas has none of that stirring and carries little energy per pound, so its film is a weaker conductor.

Do not read this as "the refrigerant side is always the bottleneck." In one DX coil example in a university lecture, the air side is given a coefficient of 50 over 360 ft² of surface and the refrigerant side 1000 over 18 ft² (the slide's units). Multiply each pair and both come to 18,000, so in that example the two films resist about equally. That is why cleaning an air-side coil and fixing a refrigerant-side problem both matter. See the three ways heat moves for the air-side basics.

The zones in a condenser

The same lecture chapter describes a condenser as three zones in order: desuperheating (vapor cooled to saturation), condensing (constant temperature) and a short subcooling zone at the end. A refrigeration heat-reclaim device that grabs only the first zone is covered in desuperheaters and reclaim coils.

Real condensers blur the lines. A 2012 conference paper from a university refrigeration lab (CO2 and a blend in a small horizontal tube) found that when the tube wall was below saturation temperature, condensation had already started in the "superheated" region, and its coefficients there were much higher than single-phase correlations predicted. The authors conclude that the simple zone model can oversize a condenser. So treat the three zones as a teaching model, not a boundary you can see with a gauge.

The zones in an evaporator

An evaporator has a boiling zone, where the refrigerant is at its saturation temperature, followed by a superheat zone where the refrigerant temperature rises. In the university chapter's example the superheat zone is only about 0.75% of the surface area. A coil does most of its work while the refrigerant is boiling, which is one reason a coil that runs with excess superheat is giving up capacity (see superheat basics).

Why the gas zones need attention

The area a zone needs for a given load is its heat divided by its overall coefficient and its average temperature difference to the air or water. A weak vapor film pushes the area up. A large temperature difference pulls it back down. In the university chapter's condenser example, the desuperheater needed a much smaller UA than the rest of the condenser because its temperature difference was large. The chapter also gives the superheated vapor's share of a condenser's heat as 10 to 30% of the total.

So the honest summary is this: gas zones have the weakest refrigerant film, but whether they need more or less area per Btu depends on the temperature difference. In an evaporator the superheat zone is worst, because the vapor film is weak and the refrigerant warms toward the air temperature, so the temperature difference shrinks. In a condenser the subcooling zone is often the one with the smallest temperature difference. The worked example below puts numbers on this.

Worked example (our own, with assumed values)

Our own example. Every property below is an assumed round number, not data for any named refrigerant. A condenser rejects 36,000 Btu/h. Refrigerant enters as superheated vapor at 160 F, condenses at 115 F and leaves as liquid at 105 F. Assume vapor specific heat 0.25 Btu/lb-F, liquid specific heat 0.35 Btu/lb-F and latent heat 70 Btu/lb.

  1. Desuperheating: 0.25 × (160 − 115) = 0.25 × 45 = 11.25 Btu/lb.
  2. Condensing: 70 Btu/lb.
  3. Subcooling: 0.35 × (115 − 105) = 0.35 × 10 = 3.5 Btu/lb.
  4. Total per pound: 11.25 + 70 + 3.5 = 84.75 Btu/lb.
  5. Mass flow: 36,000 ÷ 84.75 = about 425 lb/h.
  6. Shares: desuperheating 11.25 ÷ 84.75 = 13.3%; condensing 70 ÷ 84.75 = 82.6%; subcooling 3.5 ÷ 84.75 = 4.1%. Together 100%.
  7. Heat by zone: about 4,780 Btu/h, 29,730 Btu/h and 1,490 Btu/h, which add to 36,000.

So condensing is about 83% of the heat. The 13% desuperheating share also sits inside the 10 to 30% range from the university chapter.

Now add an assumed airflow that warms from 95 F to 105 F, idealized as flowing against the refrigerant, and split the air rise in proportion to each zone's heat. The log-mean temperature difference between refrigerant and air comes to about 14 F in subcooling, 15 F in condensing and 28 F in desuperheating. Area per Btu is proportional to 1 ÷ (U × mean difference). Dividing 15.1 by 27.6 gives 0.55, so the desuperheating zone would need as much area per Btu as the condensing zone if its overall U were about 55% of the condensing zone's. If its U is lower than that, it needs more. We state no U values, because we have no source for them. The point is the method.

What cuts performance

Oil films

A 2016 university conference paper on an automotive air-conditioning system found that the UA of both the evaporator and the condenser decreased as the oil circulation ratio rose, over a tested range of 1% to 8%. The authors attribute it to a lower refrigerant-side coefficient: oil raises surface tension, which suppresses nucleate boiling, and it forms a thicker film that adds resistance. The text gives no UA values, so we do not quote a percentage loss. For how oil gets stuck in a plant, see oil separators and oil logging.

Air and other noncondensables

The university chapter says air and other noncondensables can adversely affect the coefficients. A university multiphase-flow text adds the mechanism: in a still vapor a gas layer builds up at the film and inhibits vapor from reaching the liquid, and moving vapor weakens the effect through a sweeping action. In the field this shows up as high head pressure on a condenser that otherwise looks clean; the checks are in air and noncondensables.

Dry-out

In a boiling tube the liquid film thins as more of it turns to vapor. The university boiling notes describe dry-out as the point where the liquid film at the wall dries out, after which the flow becomes mist and then vapor and the heat transfer coefficient drops sharply. In a coil the same idea applies in practice (our own reasoning): a starved or unevenly fed circuit has less liquid along its length, so more of it behaves like the weak gas zone. See also air-cooled condenser TD for the condenser side.

Why a tech cares

  • A clean coil with normal airflow and a high condensing temperature points toward the refrigerant side: air or oil, as above.
  • Large superheat or large subcooling means a bigger share of the coil is in a weak zone, so the same coil moves less heat.
  • A dirty air side can hide a refrigerant-side problem, or the reverse, because the two films add.

Field card: Boiling and condensing heat transfer

Phase change is strong; gas film is weak.

  • Boiling and condensing films give much higher coefficients than gas-only films.
  • Condenser order: desuperheat, condense, subcool. Evaporator: boil, then superheat.
  • Our own example: 84.75 Btu/lb total, about 425 lb/h, condensing about 83% of the heat.
  • Area needed is heat divided by U times temperature difference.
  • Oil thickens the film and suppresses boiling; air blankets the condensing film.
  • Dry-out turns a wet wall into a gas-film wall and the coefficient falls.
