Guard Shacks, Parking Booths and Toll Booths HVAC: Small Space Units, Vehicle Exhaust Intake, Booth Pressurization and Window Condensation

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A guard shack, parking booth or toll booth is a tiny glass box with one person in it and a lane of running engines a few feet away. The equipment is small, but the problems are not: too much sun through the glass, a unit that is too big, exhaust that finds its way in, and windows that fog in winter. This lesson walks a facility tech through each one.

Why a small booth is hard

A booth has very little floor and a lot of glass. Sun, one person and a little equipment add little heat in absolute terms, but the volume is tiny, so temperatures swing fast. The booth also sits beside the thing that creates the worst outdoor air on the property: idling vehicles. Our own rule of thumb: treat a booth as two jobs, a small thermal load and an air quality problem.

Sizing: why the usual unit is too big

Through-wall units (see PTAC units) and ductless heads (see mini-split basics) are the common choices. The U.S. Department of Energy says mini-splits suit room additions and small apartments, and that correct sizing of each indoor unit is crucial because oversized or poorly placed air handlers can short cycle, which wastes energy and hurts temperature and humidity control. A DOE Building America study of oversized residential air conditioners found a more nuanced picture: extra cycling by itself did not add much energy use, but off-cycle parasitic power did, giving penalties of roughly 10% to 21% at capacity ratios of 1.8 and 2.2. That study did not examine dehumidification. In a booth, a big unit cools fast, shuts off, and the glass loads the space back up.

Worked example: our own 8 x 6 ft booth

These are our own assumed numbers for teaching, not a real booth. Check them against a real load calculation before you pick equipment.

  1. Volume. 8 ft x 6 ft x 8 ft = 384 cubic feet.
  2. Air changes from a 60 cfm pressurization fan. 60 cfm x 60 min/h = 3,600 cubic feet per hour. 3,600 / 384 = 9.4 air changes per hour (ACH). That is plenty for one person; the flow is there to push outward.
  3. Solar gain through glass. The DOE window guide describes the solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) as how much of the sun's heat comes through, on a scale of 0 to 1. Assume 50 sq ft of glass, SHGC 0.6, and 150 Btu/h per sq ft of sun (an assumption). 50 x 0.6 x 150 = 4,500 Btu/h.
  4. One person. Assume 400 Btu/h total heat (assumption).
  5. Equipment. Assume 500 W of lights, register and radio. 500 W x 3.412 Btu/h per W = 1,706 Btu/h.
  6. Total. 4,500 + 400 + 1,706 = 6,606 Btu/h. Divide by 12,000 Btu/h per ton: 6,606 / 12,000 = 0.55 ton.
  7. Add the 60 cfm of hot outdoor air. Assume 95°F outside and 78°F inside, a 17°F difference. Sensible heat = 1.08 x 60 cfm x 17 = 1,102 Btu/h, bringing the total to about 7,708 Btu/h, or 0.64 ton. (We ignore the latent load of outdoor moisture here.)

A 1-ton unit is 12,000 Btu/h. That is 12,000 / 6,606 = 1.8 times the load, or 1.6 times with the ventilation air. Peak sun lasts a few hours, so for most of the day the ratio is far worse. This is why a small ductless head with a low minimum output, or a half-ton class through-wall unit, is the better fit, and why a technician should ask for the load before quoting the nearest size. Shade, tint and orientation can cut the 4,500 figure sharply.

Vehicle exhaust: what the NIOSH reports show

NIOSH published reviews of toll booth work, and we describe them here without naming any site. A 1995 NIOSH report compared booths at several U.S. toll authorities. At one tunnel plaza, forced ventilation in the booths reduced exposures by 80%. At another site, the booth ventilation showed no effect. Where booths had no forced air, the report said they gave no discernible protection from exhaust gases. At a ventilated bridge plaza, personal exposures averaged 1.5 ppm, about 75% below the outside concentration. Several newer booths took their air from a side of the building away from the booths and traffic, with louvered slot flows of about 100 to 150 cfm. The report also said an older air conditioner with a filtered purifier probably had no effect on carbon monoxide, which makes sense because a particle filter does not stop a gas.

A 1981 NIOSH survey found intake placement mattered: one plaza had moved its intake from under the canopy, where exhaust could accumulate, to several feet above it. That survey also noted booth exposure rose in summer when workers opened rear doors or leaned out of the booth. For a tech, that means fresh air that comes from the lane side, or a door propped open for comfort, can defeat the system.

Know the numbers. The NIOSH Pocket Guide lists a recommended exposure limit for carbon monoxide of 35 ppm as an 8-hour average with a 200 ppm ceiling, an OSHA permissible limit of 50 ppm, and 1,200 ppm as immediately dangerous to life or health. Symptoms include headache, nausea, weakness, dizziness and confusion. The 1981 survey found hourly toll lane averages up to 94 ppm with peaks above 400 ppm, so short peaks matter even when an 8-hour average looks fine. Those were historical conditions with older vehicles. Our own advice is to put a CO alarm in the booth and test it on the schedule in the garage detection lesson.

Diesel exhaust adds fine particles. EPA says diesel exhaust contains fine particulate matter and nitrogen oxides, that it can damage lungs and worsen asthma and other respiratory disease, and that its guidance centers on cutting emissions, including shutting engines off during stops. A filter in the booth intake can help with particles; it does nothing for CO. Our own approach is to keep the intake away from the lane, use the best filter the fan can pull through, and post a no-idling reminder for drivers.

Pressurizing the booth with filtered outdoor air

The idea is simple. Pull outdoor air from the far side of the booth, away from the traffic lane, through a filter and a small fan, and let the slight overpressure leak out through the window gap, door seal and any pass-through slot. Air leaves outward instead of exhaust leaking inward. In the example above, 60 cfm gives 9.4 ACH. The figure below shows the layout.

Winter: fogging, condensation, heat, freeze and pests

Fogging. The EPA mold guidance says condensation appears when warm, humid air meets a cold surface, and recommends keeping indoor relative humidity below 60%, ideally 30% to 50%. A booth has the coldest glass in the building and a person breathing into a small volume, so the glass fogs first. Our own tips: dry winter air from the pressurization fan helps, and so does supply air aimed across the glass. See the winter condensation lesson. Do not seal the booth airtight to stop drafts; the fan is also your humidity control.

Electric heat and cords. The Consumer Product Safety Commission says never power a portable heater from an extension cord or power strip, keep combustibles at least 3 feet from the front, sides and rear, set it on a stable surface, and never leave it running unattended. In a booth that is a hard rule to keep, so the better fix is a fixed heater on its own circuit, or the heat mode of the unit. Our own note: check the cord of every radio and heater for damage, since cars can clip cables.

Freeze and pests (our own field notes). Drain lines from a ductless head or through-wall unit should drain outdoors and not freeze in the winter. Check that an outdoor unit is clear of snow and plow piles, and that wall sleeves and cable holes are sealed so mice do not get in. Rodents chew wires, so check sleeves and cable runs each season. Field card: Booth HVAC

Small load, big glass, bad air outside.

  • Volume 8 x 6 x 8 ft = 384 cu ft; 60 cfm = 9.4 ACH (ACH = cfm x 60 / volume).
  • Example load about 6,606 Btu/h (0.55 ton); about 7,708 Btu/h with outdoor air. A 1-ton unit is 1.6 to 1.8 times that.
  • Pull intake from the side away from the lane. A filter helps with particles, not CO.
  • CO: NIOSH REL 35 ppm, ceiling 200 ppm; OSHA PEL 50 ppm.
  • Keep doors and window gaps closed except as designed.
  • No portable heaters on cords or strips; keep 3 ft clear.
  • Fog on glass: lower humidity, move air across it.
