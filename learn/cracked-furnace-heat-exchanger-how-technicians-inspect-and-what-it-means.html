Cracked Furnace Heat Exchanger: How Technicians Inspect and What It Means

Last updated: 2026-10-16

"Your heat exchanger is cracked" is one of the most serious things a homeowner can hear from a furnace technician, and one of the hardest to check for yourself. This lesson explains what the part does, why a crack matters, how it is inspected, what does not prove one, and how to get a fair second opinion.

What the heat exchanger does

In a gas furnace, the flame and its exhaust gases stay inside the heat exchanger, a set of metal passages. The blower pushes house air across the outside of those passages, and heat moves through the metal wall into the house air. The two airstreams are meant to share heat but never mix. That is the general idea covered in Heat Exchanger Basics, and in a furnace the wall between the streams is thin metal.

Why a crack matters

A crack or hole is a possible path for combustion gases to reach the air that goes to the rooms. Those gases can include carbon monoxide (CO). The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) lists malfunctioning furnaces among sources of CO in the home. A CPSC staff review of 83 in-scope investigations of CO incidents involving modern gas furnaces and boilers (2002 through 2009) grouped heat exchanger holes with vent breaks and disconnects. In 16 of the 83, that group was the main failure, and CPSC said these conditions created a leakage path for CO to enter living space. It noted the data are not a statistical sample. A British Columbia safety agency also published a case where a hole in a furnace heat exchanger, plus a loose vent joint, was found after 500 ppm of CO was measured at the home's front door. Lennox's homeowner manual calls CO invisible, odorless and toxic. See Carbon Monoxide Alarms and What to Do When One Sounds for what to do if an alarm sounds. An alarm is the right first response, not a diagnosis.

How technicians inspect

The industry guide is AHRI Guideline X-2023, for induced draft furnaces and meant for trained technicians. It is a step-by-step method, not one test. Its steps, as we read them:

  • Flame watch. Start the furnace and watch the flame when the circulating blower starts. Floating, rolling out or distorted flame that appears after the blower starts can point to a heat exchanger problem. Fix nearby air leaks first. If it persists, go on to a physical inspection. Bacharach, in a trade article reprint, lists flame color or shape change at blower start as a method too.
  • Airstream CO. Take a room reading, then return and supply air readings after the furnace has run at least five minutes. AHRI says under 9 ppm with no difference between return and supply means go on to the flue check. Over 9 ppm with no difference points to a dislodged or blocked vent, or CO from somewhere else.
  • Flue CO. Under 200 ppm needs no further action under the guideline. At 200 ppm or more, the technician checks the installation against the maker's instructions and codes (vent, orifice, gas pressure, combustion air), corrects problems and rechecks. If it is still 200 ppm or more, the next step is a physical inspection.
  • Analyzer watch at blower start. Bacharach's article describes watching O2, CO2 and CO after the blower starts. If a crack lets house air (about 20.9 percent O2) into the combustion side, O2 may rise or CO2 fall, and if CO rises to excessive levels when the blower starts the unit is likely unsafe. The article gives no CO threshold. Read the analyzer lesson, Combustion Analyzer Readings, for what those numbers mean.
  • Physical inspection. AHRI says to open the furnace enough to see every outside surface of the exchanger and that a crack or hole large enough to affect combustion should be visible to the naked eye. A home inspection association article notes that mirror viewing works only where openings allow it, and that sealed-combustion furnaces cannot be seen into. AHRI also says not to use water, cameras or smoking agents to check for leaks.
  • Older tests. Bacharach's article describes smoke, oil of wintergreen, salt and tracer-gas tests and a sealed pressure test. A 2000 ACHR News article describes draft-gauge tests where a change when the blower runs suggests a connection between the flame side and the air side. Those are trade-press descriptions, not a method recommended by AHRI.

What does not prove a crack

  • One odd reading. Bacharach's article says loose clean-out ports on oil-fired equipment, or a mechanical room pulled to lower pressure by return-side leaks, can change analyzer readings at blower start and look like a crack. Treat a change as a reason to look further.
  • A clean test. The same article says no technique finds a crack every time, and that some cracks open only when hot. A 2000 ACHR News article quotes a Bacharach trainer saying he had never found a gadget effective 100 percent of the time. AHRI adds that if the physical inspection shows nothing, further investigation is advised to find the CO source.
  • A sign that is not a hole. Scorch marks, staining, rust or soot, which a home inspection article lists as warning signs, are reasons to look closer, not proof.
  • A CO alarm by itself. It shows CO is present. AHRI lists other sources to check: other gas appliances, a car running in a garage, or a fireplace.

Worked example: reading the change at blower start

These numbers are invented for teaching; no maker or standard gives a pass or fail O2 step. Assume a technician samples the flue after the burner has run steadily and sees 7.0 percent O2 and 40 ppm CO. When the blower starts, the analyzer settles at 8.5 percent O2 and 34 ppm CO.

  1. O2 change: 8.5 − 7.0 = 1.5 points higher.
  2. CO as measured: 34 − 40 = 6 ppm lower. That looks like an improvement.
  3. CO air-free before: 20.9 − 7.0 = 13.9; 20.9 ÷ 13.9 = 1.50; 40 × 1.50 = about 60 ppm.
  4. CO air-free after: 20.9 − 8.5 = 12.4; 20.9 ÷ 12.4 = 1.69; 34 × 1.69 = about 57 ppm.
  5. Excess air before: 7.0 ÷ 13.9 × 100 = about 50 percent. After: 8.5 ÷ 12.4 × 100 = about 69 percent.

The air-free formula is the one in the UEi manual, used in the analyzer lesson. The corrected CO barely moved (about 60 to 57), while O2 jumped and the as-measured CO fell, which is what extra air in the sample does. Both flue readings are under AHRI's 200 ppm flue figure, so by that step alone nothing would be flagged. The step in O2 is the clue Bacharach describes. It is a reason to run the physical inspection and rule out look-alikes (loose ports, room pressure), not a verdict. A rise in CO at blower start is a different and more serious signal in the same article. AHRI does not say whether its 200 ppm figure is as measured or air-free, so confirm that in the guideline.

Red tags: what they are

A red tag is a "do not use" notice. Technical Safety BC says a red or danger tag means a serious safety issue was found with a gas appliance, that the equipment is unsafe to keep running, and that the tag is not a punishment or a fine. It says not to turn the appliance back on and to hire a licensed gas contractor. In the case above, the gas utility tagged the furnace after it measured CO at the vent outlet. A retailer's safety page in the U.S. says only the gas utility can place its red tag and cut service, and that after repairs the owner asks the utility for a follow-up inspection. Practice varies, so ask your own gas utility. A technician may also tag a unit, so ask who tagged it and what rule it relied on.

Warranty language

Heat exchanger coverage is set by each maker and model. A Rheem warranty document covering packaged gas units lists 10 years for factory-standard heat exchangers. It also lists lifetime coverage for the original owner on selected stainless steel models, if the unit is registered within 90 days of installation. It excludes failure to install or maintain per the instructions, and the remedy is a replacement exchanger, not labor. The document shows two differing warranty texts, so check your own model and install date. Read yours before you assume a crack is covered. See How Long Do HVAC Systems Last: Repair or Replace for how this feeds into a repair or replace decision.

Getting a fair second opinion

  • Ask what was found, where, and how: flame, analyzer readings, a physical sighting. A good answer names the step, such as "I saw a crack at this seam" or "CO rose from X to Y when the blower started."
  • Ask to see it, with the panel off and a light. A visible crack is the kind AHRI says should be seen by the naked eye.
  • Do not run a unit that is tagged or shut off. A second opinion is for understanding options, not for overriding a confirmed safety finding.
  • Have a second licensed contractor repeat the checks, ideally under the same conditions, and bring any written readings from the first visit.

Field card: Cracked Furnace Heat Exchanger

A crack is a path between flame side and house air. One test rarely settles it.

  • The exchanger keeps exhaust and house air apart. A breach can let CO reach the rooms.
  • AHRI Guideline X-2023: flame watch at blower start, airstream CO (9 ppm), flue CO (200 ppm), then physical inspection.
  • Analyzer clue: O2 up or CO2 down at blower start; CO rising at blower start is worse.
  • Look-alikes: loose clean-out ports, room depressurization, other CO sources.
  • No test finds every crack; some open only hot.
  • Red tag means do not use. Do not restart it. Call a licensed contractor.
  • Warranty: check the maker, model and registration.
  • Second opinion: ask what, where and how it was found.
