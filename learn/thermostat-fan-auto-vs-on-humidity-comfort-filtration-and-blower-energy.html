Thermostat Fan AUTO vs ON: Humidity, Comfort, Filtration and Blower Energy

Last updated: 2026-10-28

Almost every thermostat has a fan switch with two positions, AUTO and ON, and many also have a third mode with a name like circulate. People argue about them online. This lesson explains what each one does, when ON helps, when it quietly hurts, and what continuous blower running costs on the electric bill.

What AUTO and ON actually do

AUTO runs the indoor blower only while the system is heating or cooling (plus any short off-delay the equipment adds). A thermostat maker's support page puts it as the fan running only while heating or cooling is active. ON runs the blower all the time. A circulate mode sits between them: it adds fan-only time so the blower runs a set share of each hour. Terminals and wiring for the fan call are in Thermostat Wiring and Smart Thermostats.

Why ON can raise humidity in cooling

A cooling coil is cold enough that water vapor in the passing air condenses on it. The water runs down into the drain pan. When the compressor stops, the coil is still wet and cold for a while. If the blower keeps running, that air movement pulls some of the water back off the coil and returns it to the house as vapor. The coil then does less net dehumidifying.

A University of Central Florida research center measured this in a paper from the late 1980s. In one 20-minute test with 10 minutes of compressor run and 10 minutes of fan only, about 19 percent of the water condensed during the compressor run evaporated back into the space. Over longer runs, at about 25 percent compressor run time, fan AUTO removed more than 2.5 times the moisture of fan ON. At about 50 percent run time, ON removed roughly 27 to 30 percent less. At 80 percent run time the two settings performed nearly the same. In that test house, held near 80°F, indoor humidity settled around 55 percent with AUTO and around 65 percent with ON. The paper also found condensation took more than 5 minutes to restart after the compressor came back with the fan ON, versus about 2 minutes with AUTO.

Those are old tests on older equipment in a hot, humid climate, so treat them as the direction and rough size of the effect, not a prediction for your house. The pattern still makes sense: the shorter the compressor runs each cycle, the more off time there is for re-evaporation. A national lab guide for air conditioning says to set the fan to AUTO for the most efficient operation, and for humid climates to keep the time-delay relay at 30 seconds or less so moisture on the coil is not blown back into the airstream. If you already fight damp air, see Basement Dehumidifier Sizing, Setpoint and Drainage, and for how cooling cycle length ties in, Thermostat Cycle Rate Settings.

Heat pump heating with the fan ON

Heat pump supply air is already gentle. Our lesson on heat pump lukewarm air in winter reports maker figures of roughly 85 to 100°F at the register while heating runs, close to skin temperature. Now add the fan ON setting. Our own reasoning, not a measured result: between heating calls the blower is moving air that has no heat added, so what comes out of the registers is about room temperature, or cooler if the ducts run through cold spaces. Air near 70°F that is moving across your hand or neck feels cool next to the 85 to 100°F air a minute earlier. That swing is why many people say the heat pump blows cold air with the fan ON, even though nothing is wrong. AUTO avoids the cool stretch; the cost is more uneven room temperatures, which is what ON or circulate try to fix.

What ON does well: mixing and filtration

Constant airflow evens out temperature between floors and rooms, and it keeps air passing through the filter. The EPA says duct-mounted filters clean the air only while the fan runs, and that low system run times can greatly limit their effectiveness. Its technical summary also says some MERV 12 and most MERV 13 filters are almost as effective as HEPA filters for most particle sizes, but adds that field studies have not confirmed fan run times long enough to show health benefits. It cautions that a higher-rated filter adds airflow resistance, so a technician should confirm the system can handle it. Our takeaway: a good filter with longer fan hours helps a little, and it is not a substitute for fixing sources or leaks.

Circulate modes

Circulate or similar modes run the fan a set percentage of every hour. One thermostat maker's support page says the setting is in 5 percent steps from 10 to 100 percent, that percent equals minutes per hour (50 percent is 30 minutes), and that time the heating or cooling already ran counts toward the total. Another maker's page says its circ mode runs the fan about 33 percent of the time. Circulate is a compromise: less re-evaporation and less electricity than ON, some mixing and filtering. It still adds fan-only time after a cooling call, so in a humid month AUTO remains the safer choice. Variable-speed systems often have their own low-airflow continuous setting and a dehumidify option; see Setting Airflow on Variable-Speed Furnaces and Air Handlers.

Blower electricity: PSC versus variable speed

A PSC (permanent split capacitor) motor runs on fixed speed taps, so slowing it down saves little. A Lawrence Berkeley lab paper says PSC motors are reasonably efficient, above 70 percent, at high speed, but efficiency may fall to about 20 percent at low speed, while brushless permanent magnet (BPM, often sold as ECM or variable-speed) motors can stay above 80 percent over a wide speed range. A DOE lab study measured eight homes where PSC blower motors were replaced with BPM motors. Fan-only power on the original PSC motors ran from 256 to 870 W, and on the replacements from 44 to 99 W. The report says fan-only flow is about half of full speed while using roughly a quarter of the power.

Worked example: a year of nonstop blower

Our own example. All inputs are assumptions chosen to sit inside the measured range above; they are not your equipment.

  • Assumption A: PSC blower in fan-only, 400 W (0.4 kW).
  • Assumption B: variable-speed blower at low speed, 100 W (0.1 kW).
  • Assumption C: runs every hour of the year, 24 × 365 = 8,760 h.
  • Assumption D: electric rate $0.20 per kWh. Use the rate on your own bill.

PSC: 0.4 kW × 8,760 h = 3,504 kWh. 3,504 × $0.20 = $700.80 per year.
Variable speed: 0.1 kW × 8,760 h = 876 kWh. 876 × $0.20 = $175.20 per year.
Difference: 3,504 − 876 = 2,628 kWh, or $525.60.

For scale, the U.S. Energy Information Administration reported average residential use of about 10,791 kWh per customer in 2022. Our arithmetic: 3,504 ÷ 10,791 is about 32 percent of that average for the PSC case, and 876 ÷ 10,791 is about 8 percent for the variable-speed case. A circulate mode at 33 percent (assumption E) scales both: 0.33 × 3,504 = 1,156 kWh ($231) PSC, and 0.33 × 876 = 289 kWh ($58) variable speed. The cooling or heating hours would run the blower anyway, so the true extra cost of ON is smaller than the full-year number; this is an upper bound. Check against the study: averaging the eight sites in its fan-only table gives about 508 W for PSC and 67 W for the replacements (our averaging), so 400 W and 100 W are reasonable round numbers. Also, every watt the blower uses ends up as heat in the airstream. 400 W is about 1,365 Btu/h (400 × 3.412, a standard conversion), which is a small extra load in summer.

What we would tell a homeowner

Leave the thermostat on AUTO as the default, especially in cooling season and in a humid house. Use ON or circulate when you have a variable-speed system with a low continuous setting, in mild weather, or when you want more filtration and the humidity is under control. Watch a humidity reading (a basic hygrometer is enough) after changing the setting. If the house feels clammy on ON, switch it back.

Field card: Thermostat Fan AUTO vs ON

AUTO follows the compressor; ON keeps moving air over a wet coil.

  • AUTO: blower runs only during heating or cooling calls.
  • ON: blower runs all the time; circulate: a set share of each hour.
  • Cooling plus ON: some coil water evaporates back (about 19 percent in one older 20-minute test), so humidity can rise.
  • Heat pump plus ON: off-time air is about room temperature, so registers feel cooler.
  • Filters only work while air moves; MERV 13 or higher filters add resistance.
  • Our own example: 400 W for 8,760 h = 3,504 kWh; 100 W = 876 kWh; at $0.20 that is $700.80 versus $175.20.
