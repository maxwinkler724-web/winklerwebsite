Differential-Pressure Flow Meters in Plant Piping: Orifice Plates and Venturi Tubes

Last updated: 2026-11-04

Many plant water and steam lines have no fancy meter at all. They have a plate or a tapered section in the pipe and a small transmitter that reads the pressure difference across it. This lesson explains how that works, why the reading is good at high flow and poor at low flow, and what a tech checks to keep it honest.

How a differential-pressure meter works

A flow element narrows the pipe on purpose. The fluid speeds up as it squeezes through, and its pressure drops. A transmitter compares the pressure upstream with the pressure at the narrow point and reports the difference. A university lecture on flow measurement states that for orifice, nozzle and venturi meters the volume flow rate is proportional to the square root of the pressure difference. Our own plain-language version: to double the flow you need four times the pressure drop.

The two elements you will meet most are:

  • Orifice plate. A thin plate with a round hole, clamped between pipe flanges. It is compact and easy to swap, but the sudden narrowing is rough on the fluid.
  • Venturi tube. A short section that narrows gradually and then widens gradually. It costs more and takes more room, but it is gentler on the flow.

Why the square root causes trouble at low flow

Because flow follows the square root of the pressure drop, the pressure drop falls much faster than the flow does. Cut the flow to one fifth and the pressure signal falls to one twenty-fifth. The transmitter has a fixed amount of noise and zero drift, and that error is a much bigger share of a tiny signal. A federal nuclear regulatory agency training text on a plant feedwater control system notes that its flow signals are biased at a 10 percent minimum because of inaccuracy at small pressure-difference signals. A federal water-metering report from a national laboratory lists a turndown ratio of less than 5:1 for both venturi and orifice meters and says orifice meters belong where flow varies very little. A federal water agency manual puts the practical range of an orifice plate at about one to three for a single hole size.

Worked example (our own)

Our own example with assumed numbers. Assume a chilled-water line fitted with an orifice plate. The meter is scaled so that 100 inches of water of pressure difference equals 400 gpm. We assume the transmitter has a zero offset of 0.5 inch of water (an assumed value for illustration, not a rating of any device).

  1. Flow at a given pressure difference = 400 x sqrt(dP / 100).
  2. At 100 inches: 400 x sqrt(1.00) = 400 gpm.
  3. At 25 inches: 400 x sqrt(0.25) = 400 x 0.5 = 200 gpm. Half the flow, a quarter of the signal.
  4. At 4 inches: 400 x sqrt(0.04) = 400 x 0.2 = 80 gpm. One fifth of the flow, one twenty-fifth of the signal.
  5. Now add the 0.5 inch offset. At 100 inches the reading is 100.5 inches, so flow reads 400 x sqrt(1.005) = 400.9 gpm, an error of about 0.2 percent.
  6. At 4 inches the reading is 4.5 inches, so flow reads 400 x sqrt(0.045) = 400 x 0.2121 = 84.9 gpm instead of 80, an error of about 6 percent.

The same half inch of error is harmless at the top of the range and large at the bottom. That is why a meter that looks fine at design flow can give poor numbers on a mild day when the plant runs at a fraction of full flow. A government gas-distribution manual we read gives a similar rule for orifice meters, saying differential readings should be at least 5 inches of water, and describes using a low-range and a high-range instrument together on one meter.

Permanent pressure loss

Every element leaves some pressure loss that does not come back downstream. University lecture notes call the orifice the largest loss of the three common elements, because of its abrupt shape, and the venturi the smallest, because its gradual widening recovers pressure. The federal water agency manual agrees that venturi meters cause very little head loss. In a pumped loop that loss is real energy, so a venturi can make sense on a big, always-running line, while an orifice plate suits a line where a little extra pump head does not matter.

Straight pipe in front of the element

Elbows, valves and reducers make the flow lopsided, and a lopsided flow shifts the reading. The federal water agency manual lists upstream lengths that range from about 5 diameters after a reducer to about 35 diameters after bends in different planes, depending on the fitting and on how small the hole is compared with the pipe, and it asks for roughly four to eight diameters downstream. It warns that poor installation of an orifice can cause as much as a 20 percent error. If the pipe layout cannot give the straight run, the reading may be wrong in a repeatable way, so note it when you compare against another meter such as a clamp-on ultrasonic meter.

Impulse-line care

The small tubes from the taps to the transmitter are called impulse lines, and most bad readings come from them rather than from the plate. The points below are general field practice that we offer as our own guidance. Always follow the instructions for the specific transmitter and piping.

  • Water service. Air or gas trapped in an impulse line acts as a spring and a false weight. Route the lines so gas can rise back to the pipe or to a vent, and vent the transmitter body as the maker instructs.
  • Steam service. The lines must be full of condensate, at equal levels on both sides, so the transmitter sees only the real pressure difference. Condensate pots at the taps hold the levels equal. The federal nuclear regulatory text above describes a condensate pot and reference leg that keep a constant liquid level for the same reason. Fill the lines with water before the first start, because a dry leg gives a wrong reading until condensate forms.
  • Freezing. Water-filled lines on cold outdoor or unheated runs can freeze and split. Check the heat tracing and insulation before the cold season.
  • Plugged taps. Scale, sludge or rust can block a tap. A plugged line often shows a reading that is stuck, drifts slowly or snaps oddly when you open the manifold. Blowing down or rodding a tap needs the line isolated and depressurized first, and steam lines are hot.

Zero check with the three-valve manifold

A three-valve manifold has a high-side valve, a low-side valve and an equalizing valve between them. To check zero in the usual way, our own steps are: (1) confirm the loop is not in a control or safety function that will react, or place it in manual per site procedure; (2) close one side valve, then open the equalizer so both sides of the transmitter see the same pressure; (3) the transmitter should read zero pressure difference, or 4 mA on a 4 to 20 mA loop; (4) adjust zero only if the instructions allow it, then close the equalizer and reopen the side valves in the order the maker lists. Opening an equalizer on a live loop changes the signal, so tell operations first. Follow the transmitter instructions and site procedures. If the zero is off, the example above shows how much it costs you at low flow.

Field card: Differential-Pressure Flow Meters

Double the flow, four times the pressure drop.

  • Flow follows the square root of the pressure difference.
  • Our example: 400 gpm at 100 in, 200 gpm at 25 in, 80 gpm at 4 in.
  • Turndown is limited; low-end readings carry the most error.
  • Orifice: higher permanent loss. Venturi: lower.
  • Keep the straight pipe the layout was designed around.
  • Water lines: vent air. Steam lines: equal condensate legs, filled before start.
  • Check zero with the equalizing valve, after telling operations.
