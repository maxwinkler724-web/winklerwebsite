Portable Spot Coolers and Evaporative Coolers in Warehouses and Server Emergencies

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A hot warehouse corner or a failed server-room unit gets a portable cooler rolled in. Two very different machines get called by the same name: the self-contained spot cooler, which is a real air conditioner on wheels, and the evaporative ("swamp") cooler, which is not. This lesson covers how each works, where the heat goes, and how to count units for a given load.

Check the sources

Check the maker's sheet. Ratings, plugs and breaker sizes below come from specific model pages read in 2026 and are examples, not recommendations. Always size the circuit and duct from the nameplate and manual of the unit in front of you.

How a self-contained spot cooler works

A spot cooler is a complete refrigeration circuit in one cabinet: compressor, evaporator, condenser and condensate collection. One fan moves room air across the evaporator and out a cool-air outlet. A second airstream moves across the condenser and carries away the heat that was removed from the space, plus the heat of the compressor. That second stream is the hot exhaust, and it is the thing that trips people up.

The heat has to go somewhere that is not the space you are cooling. A distributor guide on duct routing lists the usual choices: outdoors through a window, wall or door, a ceiling space that is part of an operating building return, a large open area that can absorb it, or water for water-cooled models. It points out that the ceiling route only works while the building air system is running; at night or on weekends the heat can build up above the tiles and drift back down, and server rooms often need cooling most at exactly those hours. An AirPac FAQ says plainly that you have to duct the warm air out of the space you are cooling, and that for many commercial jobs the plenum is the simple answer.

Why venting the exhaust into the same room cancels the cooling

Think of the cabinet as a heat mover, not a heat destroyer. The cool outlet takes some heat out of the room air. If the hot condenser air is released into that same room, the heat comes right back, plus the compressor's own electrical heat. The result is a loud fan and a room that is no cooler, or warmer. A single-duct unit that pulls its condenser air from the room has a second problem: it exhausts room air, so a tight room is pulled negative and draws warm air in from somewhere else.

Duct length matters too. The same guide says one-duct units tolerate anywhere from about 10 to 100 ft of hot-air duct depending on the model, and each 90 degree bend costs roughly 6 to 10 ft of allowance, depending on the maker. Count straight run plus bend allowances and compare the total to the manual. For the single-hose versus dual-hose idea in small units, see Window and Portable Air Conditioners.

Condensate: tank or pump

Cooling air below its dew point pulls water out of it (see Psychrometrics for Technicians). Where that water goes depends on the model. The KwiKool KPAC1411-3 page lists a self-contained tank (2.5 or 3.5 gallons, the page gives both) with float shutoff and an audible overflow alarm, and a condensate pump as an option. The KwiKool KAM14 page lists a 3.5 gallon tank that someone must empty. AirPac says its COOLIT has two 2.5 gallon tanks and an optional automatic pump to a drain line. A full tank stops the unit, so in an unattended room a pump and a drain are worth planning.

Electrical: 115 V versus 208/230 V

Small units plug into a standard 115 V outlet; larger ones need a higher-voltage circuit. A rental-fleet listing for MovinCool shows the Classic Plus 14 (13,200 Btu/h) at 115 V, 11.9 A on a NEMA 5-15 plug, and the Classic Plus 26 (24,000 Btu/h) at 208-230 V, 15.6/14.0 A on a NEMA 6-20 plug. KwiKool's KAM14 page lists 115 V, 12.9 A, a minimum circuit ampacity of 14.725 A, a recommended 15 A breaker and a 20 A maximum. Note the point: running amps are not the circuit size. Use the minimum circuit ampacity and breaker figures on the nameplate, and give each unit its own circuit rather than sharing one outlet with servers or a second cooler. See Electrical Basics for HVAC if that is unfamiliar.

Evaporative (swamp) coolers are a different machine

An evaporative cooler has no compressor. It pulls outdoor air across a wet pad; water evaporates, which cools the air, and the air arrives indoors wetter than it started. The most it can ever do is bring air down toward the outdoor wet-bulb temperature. A real cooler gets only part of the way. That fraction is called effectiveness (or saturation efficiency):

Supply temperature = dry bulb − effectiveness × (dry bulb − wet bulb)

The DOE Energy Saver page says evaporative coolers lower air temperature by 15 to 40°F and are only suitable for areas with low humidity. A pad test paper from EVAPCO reports measured saturation efficiency of about 74% at best at 600 fpm for the pads it tested, and says none reached 90%. Portacool's own article ties cooling to the gap between wet and dry bulb, and describes drops near 30 degrees in arid places versus about 10 degrees in humid ones. Because they bring in outdoor air, they need open windows or exhaust relief; DOE gives 1 to 2 sq ft of opening per 1,000 cfm as a starting point, adjusted by trial.

Worked example 1: a humid NJ day against a dry day

These weather numbers are invented for practice. Use effectiveness = 0.74 (the EVAPCO best-case pad figure above).

  1. Humid NJ summer day: 90°F dry bulb, 75°F wet bulb.
  2. Wet-bulb depression = 90 − 75 = 15°F.
  3. Temperature drop = 0.74 × 15 = 11.1°F.
  4. Supply = 90 − 11.1 = 78.9°F, and that air is now close to saturated, so a hot, sticky room.
  5. Dry day: 90°F dry bulb, 60°F wet bulb.
  6. Depression = 90 − 60 = 30°F. Drop = 0.74 × 30 = 22.2°F.
  7. Supply = 90 − 22.2 = 67.8°F.

Same dry bulb, same cooler, a 11°F difference in supply. On the humid day the wet-bulb depression is half as large, so the cooler can do half the work, and the moisture it adds makes the space feel worse.

Worked example 2: how many spot coolers for a heat load

Invented case: a failed unit leaves a room with about 8.8 kW of equipment running, and no other significant load.

  1. Heat load = 8,800 W × 3.412 Btu/h per W = 30,026, call it 30,000 Btu/h. (3.412 is the same watts-to-Btu/h factor used in the server closet lesson.)
  2. Option A, the 115 V class: KwiKool lists 13,700 Btu/h at 95°F and 60% RH. 30,000 ÷ 13,700 = 2.19, so round up to 3 units. Each needs its own 15 A circuit, at 12.9 A running.
  3. Option B, the 208-230 V class: 24,000 Btu/h. 30,000 ÷ 24,000 = 1.25, so 2 units, each on a 20 A 208-230 V circuit (NEMA 6-20 plug).
  4. Check: three small units give 41,100 Btu/h, two large give 48,000 Btu/h, both above 30,000. A spare unit for redundancy would be one more of either.
  5. Check the exhaust: each unit needs its own duct to the plenum or outdoors, within the duct limit in its manual.

Rated capacity is quoted at specific entering conditions; the maker's table for your actual room temperature may give a different number.

Spot coolers for a server emergency

A portable unit is a stopgap when the permanent system fails, not a replacement. Plan three things before the equipment arrives: where the hot exhaust goes (plenum that stays active at night, window panel, or a duct outdoors), which circuits are available at the right voltage, and how the water gets out. Do not substitute an evaporative cooler for the spot cooler here. It adds moisture to the air, and DOE says these coolers suit only low-humidity areas. AirPac's FAQ also says a unit you must fill with water is an evaporative cooler, not an air conditioner, and should not be used indoors. Targets and alarms are covered in Cooling a Small Server Room or Network Closet. For why any cooling gets weaker as outdoor air gets hotter, read AC Can't Keep Up on the Hottest Days.

Field card: Spot Coolers and Swamp Coolers

Hot exhaust out of the room, or no cooling at all.

  • A spot cooler is a real air conditioner; its condenser exhaust must be ducted outdoors or to a plenum that is actively cooled.
  • Exhaust into the cooled room cancels the cooling.
  • Tank full means unit off; plan a pump and drain for unattended rooms.
  • Use nameplate minimum circuit ampacity and breaker size, one circuit per unit.
  • Units = load (Btu/h) ÷ rated capacity, rounded up.
  • Swamp cooler supply = DB − effectiveness × (DB − WB); small depression means little cooling.
  • Not a substitute for air conditioning in a server room.
