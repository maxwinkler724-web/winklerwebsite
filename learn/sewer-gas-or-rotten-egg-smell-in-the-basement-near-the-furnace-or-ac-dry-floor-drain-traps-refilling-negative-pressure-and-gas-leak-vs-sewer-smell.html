Rotten-Egg Smell in the Basement: Dry Drain Traps vs a Gas Leak

Last updated: 2026-11-04

A rotten-egg or sewer smell near the furnace or AC is common in basements, and most of the time it comes from a floor drain that dried out. But the gas used to flag natural gas leaks smells much alike, so the first job is to decide which one you may have. This lesson walks through both.

Safety first. If you think you smell natural gas, do not look for the source. Leave the building on foot right away, avoid anything that could spark (flames, light switches, starting a vehicle), and call the gas utility and emergency services from outside or from a neighbor's home. Sort out sewer smells only after gas has been ruled out by the gas utility.

Sewer smell or gas leak?

A federal pipeline safety page explains that the odorant added to natural gas is a harmless chemical chosen for a skunk-like smell so that leaks are easy to notice, and it tells people who suspect a leak to leave the area by foot, not to hunt for the source, to avoid open flame and other ignition sources, and to call 911 and the gas company from a safe location. A federal public health page says hydrogen sulfide, which is found around municipal sewers, smells like rotten eggs, and that people can smell it at very low levels. Our own caution: both can be described as sulfur or rotten-egg smells, so do not rely on your nose to tell them apart. Treat any smell you cannot place, especially one near a furnace, water heater or gas piping, as gas until the gas utility says otherwise.

Clues that point away from gas, to use only after the utility has checked: the smell is strongest right at one drain, it comes and goes, it gets worse after the drain has sat unused, and nothing else in the house smells. The same public health page notes that at high concentrations people may lose the ability to smell hydrogen sulfide, so a smell that seems to fade is not proof that it is gone.

Cause 1: a dry floor drain trap

A trap is a U-shaped bend that keeps a pool of water in the drain. That water is the seal that blocks sewer gas. A university safety page describes the odor from a dried-out trap as usually rotten-egg or sewer-like, and an extension page says sewer gas can enter through dried-out traps in basement floor drains and in rarely used sinks, showers and tubs. Floor drains near a furnace, water heater or AC are easy to forget, because nothing runs into them. Winter makes it worse: heated basements have dry air, and the water evaporates faster.

Cause 2: condensate lines that feed a drain

A high-efficiency furnace and an AC coil both make condensate, and installers often run that line to a floor drain, a standpipe or a laundry sink. The equipment's own trap can also dry out between seasons. See AC coil condensate traps and condensate pump problems. Our own point: an AC makes condensate only in cooling season, so in winter a floor drain fed by that line may get no water at all and can dry out.

Cause 3: moving the smell around the house

A federal environmental agency report on soil gas explains that when the lowest part of a building sits at lower pressure than the ground or its surroundings, air flows in through cracks and openings, and that negative pressure can be made worse by heating and cooling equipment and by exhaust fans. It lists an HVAC return duct with a hole among the entry routes it shows. In plain terms: a leaky return in the basement pulls basement air, and whatever it smells like, into the duct, and the blower sends it upstairs. A closed utility room with an exhaust fan or a combustion appliance competing for air can add to the pull. For the garage version of this, see attached garages and return leaks. A sewer smell that is stronger when the blower runs is a clue to look at the return side.

Refilling a dry trap and keeping it full

  1. Rule out gas first, as above.
  2. Pour water slowly into the floor drain. An extension page advises about a quart every three to four weeks for basement drains.
  3. If the drain has a cleanout or a removable cover, make sure it is back in place afterward.
  4. Check again the next day. A smell that returns within days points to a trap that is losing water fast, a blockage or a leak instead of simple evaporation.
  5. A university safety page notes mineral oil can be used on top of the water to slow evaporation. We suggest you ask a plumber before using it, as it is not suited to every drain.
  6. Put a note on the calendar. Monthly is the simplest habit, and the page above also recommends about once a month.

Worked example (our own)

These numbers are assumptions we made for teaching, not measurements. Assume a floor drain trap holds a 2 inch water seal when full. Assume dry winter basement air evaporates 0.1 inch per day from it.

  1. Seal depth to lose: 2 inches.
  2. Loss rate: 0.1 inch per day.
  3. Days to empty: 2 ÷ 0.1 = 20 days.
  4. Check: 0.1 inch per day × 20 days = 2.0 inches, which is the whole seal. It works.

What it tells us: with these assumptions the trap is dry in about three weeks. The practical limit is shorter, because a thin film of water does not make a reliable barrier, so treat 20 days as an upper limit, not a safe schedule. A refill interval of three to four weeks, as in the extension page, would be too long for this assumed rate. Real rates depend on air temperature, humidity and airflow, so the number in your basement may differ a lot.

Who to call

  • Gas utility and emergency services, from outside: any smell you cannot rule out as gas.
  • Plumber: a drain that stays smelly after refilling, a gurgling or slow drain, a missing or cracked trap, a floor drain with no trap at all, a roof vent blocked by frost (an extension page describes ice plugging a vent in very cold weather), or sewage backing up.
  • HVAC technician: smell stronger when the blower runs, a furnace or AC condensate line that leaks, backs up or lacks a proper trap, a leaky return duct, or a basement that seems starved of air.
  • Not sure? Call the gas utility first, then whichever trade the findings point to. Headaches, nausea or dizziness along with a smell are a reason to get outside to fresh air and call for help, as the extension page lists them among symptoms.

Field card: Rotten-egg smell in the basement

Gas first, drain second.

  • Suspect gas? Leave on foot, avoid sparks, call the gas utility from outside.
  • A dry floor drain trap is the most common sewer smell source.
  • Refill with water; check again the next day.
  • Our example: 2 in seal at 0.1 in per day is gone in 20 days (assumed numbers).
  • Smell worse when the blower runs? Look at return leaks and negative pressure.
  • Plumber for drains and vents; HVAC tech for condensate and ducts.
