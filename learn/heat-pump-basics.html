Heat Pump Basics

Last updated: 2026-10-16

A heat pump does not make heat by burning something. It moves heat from one place to another, like a bucket brigade that can run in either direction. This lesson covers the idea, the key parts and a safe way to think about problems.

Moving heat instead of making it

The U.S. Department of Energy explains that a heat pump uses electricity to move heat. In summer it takes heat from inside the house and puts it outside. In winter it pulls heat from outdoor air and delivers it inside. Even cold air contains some heat, which is why this works. Because it moves heat rather than creating it, a properly installed unit can deliver up to about three times as much heat energy as the electricity it uses, according to DOE.

The basic parts

An air-source heat pump has a compressor, an indoor coil and an outdoor coil, connected by refrigerant lines. In heating, refrigerant in the outdoor coil absorbs heat and evaporates, and the indoor coil releases that heat as the refrigerant turns back to liquid. Fans move air across the coils.

The reversing valve

The reversing valve is what lets one machine heat and cool. It sits near the compressor and changes the direction the refrigerant flows. When the direction flips, the two coils swap jobs. DOE notes this same valve also lets the unit defrost its outdoor coil in winter.

Defrost, in general terms

In heating mode the outdoor coil runs colder than the air, and moisture in the air can freeze on it as frost. Frost blocks airflow and hurts performance. To clear it, the control system occasionally runs a defrost cycle that warms the outdoor coil. You may notice the outdoor unit change sound or look different for a few minutes. DOE recommends models with demand-defrost controls, which start a cycle only when needed instead of on a fixed clock. Details differ among manufacturers, so check the manual. Ice that does not clear, or a unit buried in snow or ice, is worth a service call.

ENERGY STAR and cold-climate ratings

ENERGY STAR sets minimum efficiency numbers for air-source heat pumps. As examples from its current criteria page: split systems must reach at least 7.8 HSPF2, 15.2 SEER2 and 11.0 EER2. HSPF2 measures heating efficiency over a season, and SEER2 and EER2 measure cooling. A separate ENERGY STAR cold-climate designation asks for more. Ducted split systems must reach at least 8.1 HSPF2, and non-ducted (ductless) split systems at least 8.5 HSPF2. The unit must also deliver a coefficient of performance of at least 1.75 at 5 degrees Fahrenheit, and at least 70 percent of its 47-degree-Fahrenheit capacity at 5 degrees. Requirements change over time, so check the ENERGY STAR page before relying on these numbers. For colder regions, DOE points to units designed for cold climates and says to look for the ENERGY STAR label and a high HSPF.

A note on refrigerants

New heat pumps are moving to refrigerants in the A2L class, which are mildly flammable. That changes some handling, tools and ventilation habits for technicians. Read our lesson on A2L and A3 refrigerants, and always follow the label and manufacturer instructions. Only certified technicians should handle refrigerant.

A sensible troubleshooting order

Technicians often work through problems in layers. For a unit that heats or cools poorly, a general order is:

  • Airflow first. Dirty filters, blocked coils and closed vents restrict airflow. DOE says clogged filters cut efficiency, and suggests checking filters every month or two while the system is working hard. Keeping plants and debris back from the outdoor unit also helps.
  • Refrigerant charge second. Low or high charge hurts performance. Checking and adjusting charge is a task for a certified technician using manufacturer data and proper tools.
  • Electrical third. Power supply, controls and components like contactors and capacitors can fail. Work on electrical parts only if trained, because stored energy can remain after power is off.

Whatever the order, measure instead of guessing, change one thing at a time, and turn power off before opening panels.

What homeowners can do

Replace or clean filters on schedule, keep the outdoor unit clear of leaves and snow, and call a professional for anything involving refrigerant or wiring.

Field card: Heat Pump Basics

A heat pump moves heat; the reversing valve picks the direction.

  • Heat pumps move heat with electricity instead of burning fuel.
  • The reversing valve swaps coil roles and supports defrost.
  • ENERGY STAR and cold-climate labels are published criteria; check the current page.
  • New units often use mildly flammable A2L refrigerants.
  • Check airflow, then charge, then electrical; only trained techs touch refrigerant or live wiring.
