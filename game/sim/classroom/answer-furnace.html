Answer key: Furnace

Teacher copy. Student worksheet | Open the simulator module

Learning goals
  • Follow the ignition sequence (inducer, pressure switch, igniter, gas valve, flame sensing) and say where it stopped.
  • Use a meter safely: de-energize and verify dead before any ohm test, then measure voltage only on a live, guarded circuit.
  • Separate a failed part from a failed condition (dirty filter, blocked vent, bad ground) before replacing anything.

Answers are the simulator's own explanations, so wording in a student's own words will differ. Accept any answer that names the cause and a repair that matches. Scores and numbers in the sim are practice examples.

Call 1: Cold house on Maple Street

Mrs. Rivera
Cause and explanation: A hot surface igniter that reads open (OL) cannot warm up, so the board tries to ignite and locks out. The 120 V was present, so the fault was the igniter, not the board. Hold igniters by the base and never touch the element.
Repairs needed: Replace igniter.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Run a heat cycle and note where the sequence stops and what the board LED says. / The draft is proven and the board tried to light. Check what lights the gas, with power off.

Call 2: Furnace shuts off after seconds

Mr. Chen
Cause and explanation: Oxide on the flame rod lowers flame current below what the board needs (often under about 1 microamp), so it shuts the gas. Cleaning with a fine abrasive pad, not sandpaper, usually restores a few microamps.
Repairs needed: Clean flame sensor (fine abrasive pad) or Replace flame sensor.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Burners light, so ignition and gas are fine. What proves flame to the board? / Measure flame current in microamps while the burners are lit.

Call 3: Inducer runs, no heat

Ms. Patel
Cause and explanation: The pressure switch stayed open because condensate and debris blocked its tube, so the board never got proof of draft. Replacing a good switch would not have helped. Always check tubes and vent before parts.
Repairs needed: Clear pressure-switch tube.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): The inducer runs. What proves the draft to the board? / Check the voltage across the pressure switch contacts, then look at its tube.

Call 4: Total silence

Mr. Okafor
Cause and explanation: 120 V at the inducer plug with no rotation means the motor itself failed. Its windings read open. The pressure switch also stays open as a result, which is why the board shows the same code as a blocked tube.
Repairs needed: Replace inducer motor.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Is power reaching the inducer connector during the call? / 120 V present but no rotation points at the motor. Confirm with power off.

Call 5: Dead furnace, dead board

The Garcias
Cause and explanation: The 3 A low-voltage fuse blew because a thermostat wire was pinched in the cabinet and shorted. Replacing only the fuse would blow it again. Find the cause first. Never replace it with a bigger fuse.
Repairs needed: Replace 3 A fuse; Repair pinched thermostat wire.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Is power reaching the board? Compare transformer secondary with the board. / The transformer is fine. Check the fuse with power off. Then ask why it blew.

Call 6: Heat quits after a couple of minutes

Ms. Brooks
Cause and explanation: A clogged filter restricts airflow, the heat exchanger overheats, and the high limit shuts the burners. The blower keeps running to cool down. Check the simple airflow causes before replacing a good limit.
Repairs needed: Replace air filter.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Burners run at first. What trips them after a short time? / Check the air filter and the high limit.

Call 7: Heat quits, smells like exhaust

Mrs. Dubois
Cause and explanation: The flame rollout switch opened because a bird nest blocked the vent termination, so exhaust backed up into the burner area. It is a safety device. Never jumper it out or just reset it: that hides a carbon monoxide hazard. Clear the vent, then verify a normal cycle and test CO.
Repairs needed: Clear the blocked vent termination.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): The LED code is not one of the usual ones. Look at the legend. / A rollout switch opens when flame or heat escapes the combustion chamber. Look for a blocked vent.

Call 8: Igniter glows, no flame

Mr. Nowak
Cause and explanation: The igniter glowed and the valve had 24 V, but the coil was open (OL) so the valve never opened. Replacing the board would not help. Shut off the gas, replace the valve, and check for leaks and proper manifold pressure after.
Repairs needed: Replace gas valve.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Igniter and inducer are fine. What opens the gas? / Check for 24 V at the gas valve during trial, then its coil with power off.

Call 9: Dead thermostat, cold house

Ms. Alvarado
Cause and explanation: A battery-powered thermostat with dead batteries cannot call for heat, so the furnace never started. The most common cause is the simplest. Check at the thermostat first and save the customer a bill for a service call that is not needed.
Repairs needed: Replace thermostat batteries.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Start at the thermostat before you open the furnace. / Look at the display.

Call 10: Fan runs, then nothing

Mr. Hughes
Cause and explanation: The pressure switch must close during pre-purge. A manometer showed draft below the switch's closing pressure, and a bird nest in the vent termination was the cause. The switch itself is the least common failure. Check the inducer, tubing, flue and drain before parts. A blocked flue can mean carbon monoxide: never jumper the switch.
Repairs needed: Clear the blocked vent and flue.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Is the switch open or closed? Measure the draft. / Draft is weak. Look outside at the vent.
Source basis: HVAC School: properly diagnosing a gas furnace pressure switch.

Call 11: Dies on the coldest mornings

Ms. Reyes
Cause and explanation: In a condensing furnace the condensate path can restrict or freeze in cold weather. Water backs into the secondary heat exchanger, draft drops and the pressure switch will not prove. Clear and refill the trap, pitch and insulate the drain or reroute it inside the conditioned space, and add a heat cable only where the maker allows. Do not use boiling water on plastic fittings you will reuse.
Repairs needed: Clear and refill the trap; pitch and insulate the drain.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): The switch is not proving at start. What restricts draft in a condensing furnace? / Look at the condensate trap and the drain outside.
Source basis: HVAC School pressure switch article.

Call 12: The last guy put in a wire

Mr. Baptiste
Cause and explanation: A jumpered pressure switch reads closed even when the inducer is off, so the board correctly flags a switch that did not open. The jumper hid a partly blocked vent, which also let carbon monoxide rise. Remove the jumper, clear the blockage, replace the switch only if it tests bad, warn the owner and document it. Never bypass a safety control.
Repairs needed: Remove the jumper wire from the pressure switch; Clear the blocked vent and flue.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Check the switch with the inducer off. / A bridged switch hides the real problem. Find it outside.
Source basis: HVAC School article (stuck-closed switch example); US Boiler air-proving switch logic.

Call 13: Burners on, no warm air

Mr. Lee
Cause and explanation: A weak run capacitor (about 1.4 uF against a 7.5 uF rating) lets the motor hum without starting. The limit trips for lack of airflow, a similar symptom to a dirty filter. Discharge capacitors safely before touching them.
Repairs needed: Replace blower run capacitor.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): The filter looks clean. Is the blower getting 120 V when it should run? / You hear a hum. Check the run capacitor with power off.

How numbers are labeled. Readings, prices, set points, timers, blink codes and trip values in the calls are practice examples made up for the game unless a call says a source gives them. Words like about, example and practice mark them. Where a number comes from one maker's document (for example Hoshizaki timers or Trane flash codes), it is that maker's example and other makers differ. Rules of thumb (such as coolers about 35 to 38 F) are common practice, not rules. Laws and codes are cited by name: confirm the current text. Always follow the equipment maker's instructions and local code.
