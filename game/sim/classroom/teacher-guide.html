What this is

The Service Call Simulator puts a student in a service truck at a fictional house or building. They read the work order, test with a virtual meter and gauges, find the cause, repair it, and verify. Safety and method are scored, not just the fix. The kit adds paper to go with the screen: worksheets, answer keys, a progress card, and this guide.

Learning goals (whole course)

  • Diagnose before replacing: read, test, decide, then repair.
  • Work safely: PPE, lockout, verify dead, never bypass a safety device, never cap a relief valve, recover refrigerant instead of venting.
  • Verify every repair with the right reading and the panel back on.
  • Explain a problem and the fix in plain words to a customer and in writing on a work order.

Module goals

ModuleStudents will be able to
Furnace (13 calls)
  • Follow the ignition sequence (inducer, pressure switch, igniter, gas valve, flame sensing) and say where it stopped.
  • Use a meter safely: de-energize and verify dead before any ohm test, then measure voltage only on a live, guarded circuit.
  • Separate a failed part from a failed condition (dirty filter, blocked vent, bad ground) before replacing anything.
Cooling (split system) (8 calls)
  • Read gauges, line temperatures and amps together and explain what superheat and subcooling say about charge, airflow and restrictions.
  • Choose a cause from the readings before touching the refrigerant circuit.
  • Explain why a leaking system is never topped off and why recovery, repair, evacuation and a weighed charge come first.
Heat pump (7 calls)
  • Describe the heating-mode cycle, defrost and what the reversing valve does.
  • Tell auxiliary heat running by itself from a working heat pump using amps and temperatures.
  • Recognize a normal behavior (defrost, a wait) and explain it to a customer instead of replacing parts.
Mini-split (4 calls)
  • Use fault history and communication checks to separate wiring faults from a failed board.
  • Explain why filters, coils and clearances cause icing and overload trips.
  • Describe why ice is thawed and never chipped, and why an overload is never bypassed.
Hot-water boiler (7 calls)
  • Trace water flow: boiler, circulator, zone valves, piping and radiation, and use pressure and temperature readings to find where it stops.
  • Explain what the expansion tank, fill valve, air vent and relief valve each do, including where the tank connects.
  • Run the post-service safety test (low-water cutoff and high limit) and record the set point and trip value.
Condensing boiler (3 calls)
  • Explain why a condensing boiler needs a drained condensate path, a sealed vent and a clean intake.
  • Work a lockout in order (gas, electrodes and ground, valve power, drain, combustion) instead of resetting it again and again.
  • Explain why a proving switch is never jumpered and why a blocked flue can mean carbon monoxide.
One-pipe steam boiler (7 calls)
  • Explain why a low-pressure steam system runs at about 0.5 to 2 psi and why the relief valve is never capped.
  • Connect symptoms (hammer, spitting, cold radiators) to causes: water level, Hartford loop piping, pitch, vents and valves.
  • Run the post-service safety test and explain why cold water is never poured into a hot, nearly dry boiler.
Steam heat exchanger (3 calls)
  • Explain how a steam trap works, what a stall is, and how a vacuum breaker or pump trap fixes a flooded coil.
  • Name the main causes of steam hammer (pockets, sag, bad traps, fast valve opening) and warm a cold line up safely.
  • Explain why a trap that has failed open is repaired, not valved off.
ECM blower (3 calls)
  • Tell a failed ECM motor from a failed control, a loose pin or a restriction by testing in order.
  • Explain why a short wait is needed before opening an ECM motor, and why the control and harness are checked before the motor is condemned.
  • Describe how a thick filter or duct restriction makes the blower hunt.
Rooftop economizer (4 calls)
  • Explain what an air-side economizer does and what the changeover setpoint and minimum position are for.
  • Use controller alarms and test menus before touching linkage, and record as-found and final settings.
  • Practice rooftop safety: fall protection, lockout and pinch points.
Commercial refrigeration (5 calls)
  • Explain defrost (timer, termination, fail-safe) and fan delay for a walk-in.
  • State the cold-holding limit (41 F or below) and why product held above it is evaluated, not served.
  • Follow an ice machine through fill, freeze and harvest and test the controls before the board.

Setting up

  • Any current browser on a computer, Chromebook or tablet. It runs offline once the page is open. There is nothing to install and nothing to log in to.
  • Scores and progress are saved in each browser on that device (local storage). On shared computers, have students open their Progress card at the end of class, save or print it, then press the card's clear button so the next class starts fresh.
  • Keyboard use works: Tab to a part, Enter to select. Touch works on tablets. For a projector demo, use a wide window.
  • The first call of each module is a free demo, so you can show the interface to a class before anything else.

Running a session (45 minutes)

MinutesWhat happens
0-5Safety talk. Pick two rules from the module's answer key and say why they exist. Hand out the module worksheet.
5-12Demo the first call on the projector. Think aloud: PPE, power, call for heat, run the cycle, gather data, then touch parts. Use a hint on purpose and show the point cost.
12-35Pairs, one device each. Roles swap every call: one is the technician at the screen, one is the partner who reads the worksheet prompts and challenges any part swap without a test. Students fill one worksheet box per call.
35-42Debrief. Ask: which call took the most tests? Which part did someone replace that was not broken? What would you tell the customer? Use the answer key for the cause and the sim's own explanation.
42-45Exit ticket: the wrap-up box on the worksheet. Students save or print their progress card.

Longer labs (90 minutes). Do two modules that contrast (for example furnace then boiler), or run a module and have teams of three present one call to the class as a "customer explanation".

A four-week unit. Week 1 furnace and ECM blower. Week 2 cooling and heat pump. Week 3 boilers (hot water, condensing, steam). Week 4 commercial (rooftop, refrigeration, steam heat exchanger) plus a review. Adjust to your course. Not every program covers every system.

Scoring

Each call gives a score out of 100. Points are lost for skipping PPE, working on live circuits, hint use, unneeded part replacements, unsafe shortcuts, extra tests beyond a reasonable number, and skipping verification (and, on boiler calls, the post-service safety test). Grades in the sim: 90 and up master tech, 75 and up journeyman, 50 and up apprentice, below that needs more practice. A call that was not finished is capped low. Students may replay a call; the best score is kept. These grades are a game, not a course grade: you choose how to count them (completion, best score, or the worksheet).

Using the worksheets and keys

  • Worksheet boxes are the same on every call so students learn the pattern: safety, tests, cause, repair, verify.
  • Answer keys copy the simulator's own explanations and repair lists. Accept the student's own wording.
  • Keep the keys away from students. Print them separately.

The progress card and handing in

  • The card reads the best scores saved in this browser, shows completed and not completed calls, and lets the student type a name and class. The name is typed on the page and is not stored in the browser.
  • The student prints the card (or saves it to PDF) or presses Save card file to download a small .json file, then hands it in by whatever your school uses (printout, LMS upload, USB). The simulator never sends anything.
  • Teacher: open "Check cards" and pick the saved card files. It shows a class table and a CSV you can download for your gradebook. All of that happens in your browser.
  • Honest limit: the card comes from the student's own device, so it is an honor-system record and not proof. A student could edit the file. Spot-check by watching a call, asking for a verbal explanation of a worksheet answer, or having the student replay a call in front of you.

Accessibility and language

The simulator uses large touch targets, visible focus, keyboard control, and text that works with screen magnification. The diagram has text equivalents for every part. A Spanish beta of the simulator is available (EN/ES buttons on every page); it needs review by native speakers, and the stories of many calls are still English. This kit is English only for now.

Privacy

No accounts, no tracking, no ads, no network calls. Everything stays on the device. Do not ask students to put personal details on the card beyond a name or ID you choose.

What this is not

It is not a certification and does not replace EPA 608 or Black Seal preparation, hands-on lab work, manufacturer instructions, or local code. Numbers in the calls are practice examples. Real gas, electric, refrigerant, steam and roof work needs qualified supervision. The kit does not claim alignment with any particular state or national standard; check your own program's outcomes.

Discussion questions

  1. When is replacing a part the right answer, and what test proves it?
  2. Why do these calls punish resetting a lockout again and again?
  3. A customer says "just top it off." What do you say?
  4. What does verifying with the cover on and the system running catch?
  5. Describe a call where doing nothing but explaining was the best repair.

Sources

How numbers are labeled. Readings, prices, set points, timers, blink codes and trip values in the calls are practice examples made up for the game unless a call says a source gives them. Words like about, example and practice mark them. Where a number comes from one maker's document (for example Hoshizaki timers or Trane flash codes), it is that maker's example and other makers differ. Rules of thumb (such as coolers about 35 to 38 F) are common practice, not rules. Laws and codes are cited by name: confirm the current text. Always follow the equipment maker's instructions and local code.

Each answer key ends with the sources for its module. All modules together: Sources page. Check the maker's current document and your local code before you teach a number as fact.
