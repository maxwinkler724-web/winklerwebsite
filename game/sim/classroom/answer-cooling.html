Answer key: Cooling (split system)

Teacher copy. Student worksheet | Open the simulator module

Learning goals
  • Read gauges, line temperatures and amps together and explain what superheat and subcooling say about charge, airflow and restrictions.
  • Choose a cause from the readings before touching the refrigerant circuit.
  • Explain why a leaking system is never topped off and why recovery, repair, evacuation and a weighed charge come first.

Answers are the simulator's own explanations, so wording in a student's own words will differ. Accept any answer that names the cause and a repair that matches. Scores and numbers in the sim are practice examples.

Call 1: Topped off twice, still weak and sweaty

Ms. Brooks
Cause and explanation: High superheat with low suction pressure can look like undercharge, but an undercharge shows low subcooling. Here subcooling at the condenser was normal while the liquid line was much colder at the TXV inlet, with flash gas past a crimp in the line set. That is a restriction upstream of the metering device. Recover, remove the crimp or replace the section, evacuate and weigh in the nameplate charge. Topping off to chase pressure would have overcharged it.
Repairs needed: Recover all refrigerant to a recovery cylinder; Remove the crimp: replace that section of line; Evacuate, then weigh in nameplate charge.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Superheat is high and suction is low. Compare liquid line temperature at the condenser with the temperature at the TXV inlet. / Subcooling at the condenser looks normal, but the liquid line drops in temperature before the TXV.
Source basis: Danfoss TXV guidance; ClimateMaster TXV diagnostic bulletin.

Call 2: Frosted valve, someone added refrigerant

Mr. Singh
Cause and explanation: Low suction with very high superheat can come from low charge or from a failed (closed) TXV, a lost bulb charge or a plugged strainer. Calculate superheat and subcooling, check airflow and bulb mounting (clamped to the suction line, pasted and insulated), then do the bulb response test: warming the bulb should raise suction pressure and chilling it should lower it. This bulb did neither, so the valve was bad. Recover the refrigerant before replacing it.
Repairs needed: Recover all refrigerant to a recovery cylinder; Replace TXV; Evacuate, then weigh in nameplate charge.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Check airflow, charge and bulb mounting first. Then do the bulb response test. / Warming the bulb should raise suction pressure while the compressor runs.
Source basis: ClimateMaster TXV bulletin; Danfoss TXV guidance (bulb placement, insulation, settling time after adjustment).

Call 3: Weak cooling, ice on the line

Mrs. Rivera
Cause and explanation: Low suction pressure, high superheat (about 26 F) and low subcooling (about 2 F) together point to a low charge. Charge is only lost through a leak, so find and repair it first, then recover, evacuate and weigh in the nameplate charge. Adding refrigerant by gauge feel without fixing the leak is wasteful and illegal to vent.
Repairs needed: Repair leak at indoor flare fitting; Evacuate, then weigh in nameplate charge.
Also acceptable (no penalty): Recover all refrigerant to a recovery cylinder.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Run the system, connect gauges and read both pressures. Then compare line temperatures with the PT chart. / Low suction, high superheat and low subcooling. Refrigerant is only lost through a leak.

Call 4: Runs all day, high bill

Mr. Chen
Cause and explanation: High head pressure, high subcooling and high compressor amps with normal superheat point to poor heat rejection at the condenser. The coil was matted with cottonwood and dirt. Clean it from the inside out with the power off. Do not adjust the charge.
Repairs needed: Wash condenser coil inside out.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Check the amps and head pressure, then look at the outdoor coil. / High head, high subcooling, high amps.

Call 5: Ice on the indoor coil

Ms. Patel
Cause and explanation: Low suction pressure with very low superheat (about 3 F), a large supply-air temperature drop and ice on the coil point to low airflow over the evaporator, not low charge. The filter was clogged. Adding refrigerant would have made it worse.
Repairs needed: Replace air filter.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Very low superheat and a large temperature drop across the coil point to airflow. / Check the filter and the evaporator coil.

Call 6: High pressures after a recharge

Mr. Okafor
Cause and explanation: High head pressure, high subcooling (about 22 F) and slightly low superheat with a clean coil and good airflow indicate too much refrigerant. Recover excess charge, weighing what you remove, until subcooling reaches the 10 F target.
Repairs needed: Recover excess charge to 10 F subcooling (weigh it).
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Compare subcooling with the 10 F target. / Subcooling is about 22 F while the coil is clean.

Call 7: Humming outdoor unit

The Garcias
Cause and explanation: A fan motor that hums and does not turn usually has a weak run capacitor (here about 2.8 uF against a 5 uF rating). With no airflow the head pressure rises and the compressor may trip on overload. Disconnect and discharge before touching a capacitor.
Repairs needed: Replace fan run capacitor.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Check whether the condenser fan is turning, and amps. / Fan hums but does not turn. Test the fan run capacitor with power off, discharged.

Call 8: Cold spot on a copper line

Ms. Brooks
Cause and explanation: High superheat with low suction pressure and high subcooling, plus a frosty drier, points to a liquid-line restriction. Recover the refrigerant, replace the filter drier, evacuate and weigh in the nameplate charge. Never vent refrigerant.
Repairs needed: Recover all refrigerant to a recovery cylinder; Replace filter drier; Evacuate, then weigh in nameplate charge.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Superheat is very high and suction is low, but subcooling is also high. / Feel for a temperature drop across the filter drier.

How numbers are labeled. Readings, prices, set points, timers, blink codes and trip values in the calls are practice examples made up for the game unless a call says a source gives them. Words like about, example and practice mark them. Where a number comes from one maker's document (for example Hoshizaki timers or Trane flash codes), it is that maker's example and other makers differ. Rules of thumb (such as coolers about 35 to 38 F) are common practice, not rules. Laws and codes are cited by name: confirm the current text. Always follow the equipment maker's instructions and local code.
