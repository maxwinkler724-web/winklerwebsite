Answer key: One-pipe steam boiler

Teacher copy. Student worksheet | Open the simulator module

Learning goals
  • Explain why a low-pressure steam system runs at about 0.5 to 2 psi and why the relief valve is never capped.
  • Connect symptoms (hammer, spitting, cold radiators) to causes: water level, Hartford loop piping, pitch, vents and valves.
  • Run the post-service safety test and explain why cold water is never poured into a hot, nearly dry boiler.

Answers are the simulator's own explanations, so wording in a student's own words will differ. Accept any answer that names the cause and a repair that matches. Scores and numbers in the sim are practice examples.

Call 1: Hissing, spitting radiator vents

Mr. Costa
Cause and explanation: A low-pressure steam system only needs a pound or two to push steam through the pipes. The pressuretrol cut-out was set to about 9 psi, so the boiler kept firing, the relief valve (15 psi) lifted and the vents spat. Reset the cut-out to about 1.5 psi with a small differential, and confirm the pressure stays low. Do not cap or change the relief valve.
Repairs needed: Reset pressuretrol cut-out to about 1.5 psi.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Read the pressure gauge and the pressuretrol setting. / A residential steam boiler should run under about 2 psi. This one reads about 9 psi.

Call 2: Boiler locked out, no heat

Ms. Ivanova
Cause and explanation: The low-water cut-off did its job and shut the burner off because the sight glass was almost empty. The auto-feeder had failed and was not making up water. Fix the feeder and bring the water to the middle of the glass in small amounts. Never run the boiler dry, and never pour cold water quickly into a hot, nearly dry cast-iron boiler: it can crack.
Repairs needed: Replace auto water feeder, bring level to mid-glass.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Look at the sight glass first, then the low-water cut-off and feeder. / The glass is nearly empty. The auto-feeder is not adding water.

Call 3: One cold radiator

Mrs. Fischer
Cause and explanation: In a one-pipe steam system, steam enters only as fast as air leaves. The air vent on this radiator was clogged with paint and rust, so the air could not escape and steam never filled it. Replace the vent with the right size for the radiator and check it fills evenly.
Repairs needed: Replace radiator air vent.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Feel the radiator and listen at its air vent. / Steam cannot enter if air cannot leave. Check the vent.

Call 4: Banging and knocking

The Marchettis
Cause and explanation: One-pipe radiators must slope slightly toward the supply valve so condensate drains back down the same pipe. This radiator leaned the wrong way, so water pooled and slugs of steam banged into it and leaked at the valve. Shim the legs to slope toward the valve and check the valve is fully open (never half open).
Repairs needed: Shim radiator legs to slope toward the valve.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Look at how the radiator sits and where the valve is. / The radiator should slope back toward the supply valve so condensate runs back.

Call 5: Water level bounces, wet steam

Mr. Ahmed
Cause and explanation: Oil and dirt on the water surface made the boiler prime: it carried water into the steam lines and made the level bounce. The fix is to skim the surface and flush and refill following the maker's instructions, then confirm the level holds steady. Raising the pressure or adding water would not cure it. Very dirty water or pH above about 9 (7 to 9 is cited) also promotes priming, so test it.
Repairs needed: Skim the surface and flush, refill to mid-glass.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Watch the sight glass while the boiler is steaming. / Cleaning left oil and dirt on the water surface.

Call 6: Banging in the pipes after a boiler swap

Mr. DeLuca
Cause and explanation: Three problems combined. The new boiler's near-boiler piping did not match the manual, and the Hartford loop return joined the equalizer through a long nipple, which can cause water hammer. The steam main also sagged, so a low spot held water that steam slugged through. The water line sat low. Fix: re-pipe the loop with a close nipple at the height the boiler maker gives (a trade article puts it about 2 inches below the lowest operating waterline: follow the manual), re-pitch the main so it drains (a trade article cites about 1 inch per 20 feet when steam and condensate flow the same way), set the water level near the top of the gauge glass as the maker advises, and check the water pH (7 to 9 is cited). Sources: Holohan in Plumbing & Mechanical (2014) and HeatingHelp (trade articles). A pump-fed return changes the loop rules, so use the maker's drawing. Numbers are practice examples.
Repairs needed: Re-pipe the Hartford loop with a close nipple, at the maker's height; Re-pitch the sagging steam main; Set the water level near the top of the gauge glass; Test the water pH and treat or flush if it is out of range.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Compare the near-boiler piping with the boiler manual, then sight along the steam main. / Look at the nipple on the Hartford loop and for a low spot in the main.

Call 7: Spitting radiator valves, rooms that never heat

The Whitfields
Cause and explanation: In a one-pipe steam system the supply valve must be fully open or fully shut, never half closed: a half-closed valve makes steam and returning condensate fight in the same pipe, so it spits and gurgles. Sticky, clogged air valves kept the back radiators from venting, and the wet return was partly blocked with scale, so condensate drained slowly. Fix: open the valves fully, replace the clogged air valves, and flush the return. Sources: Holohan (HeatingHelp, 2014) for the valve rule; a 1929 maker catalog for air valve behavior (historic: use a current maker table for vent sizes). Numbers are practice examples.
Repairs needed: Open the half-closed radiator supply valves fully; Replace the clogged radiator air valves; Flush the partly blocked wet return.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Check the supply valves on the radiators, then the air vents. / Steam and water cannot share a half-closed valve. Also look at how the wet return drains.
Wrap-up answers. Look for a specific safety rule from this module (de-energize and verify dead, PPE, no jumpers on safeties, recover refrigerant, never cap a relief valve, or the module's own). Look for a reading tied to a decision, and a concrete mistake to avoid, such as replacing a part before testing.

How numbers are labeled. Readings, prices, set points, timers, blink codes and trip values in the calls are practice examples made up for the game unless a call says a source gives them. Words like about, example and practice mark them. Where a number comes from one maker's document (for example Hoshizaki timers or Trane flash codes), it is that maker's example and other makers differ. Rules of thumb (such as coolers about 35 to 38 F) are common practice, not rules. Laws and codes are cited by name: confirm the current text. Always follow the equipment maker's instructions and local code.
