Answer key: Heat pump

Teacher copy. Student worksheet | Open the simulator module

Learning goals
  • Describe the heating-mode cycle, defrost and what the reversing valve does.
  • Tell auxiliary heat running by itself from a working heat pump using amps and temperatures.
  • Recognize a normal behavior (defrost, a wait) and explain it to a customer instead of replacing parts.

Answers are the simulator's own explanations, so wording in a student's own words will differ. Accept any answer that names the cause and a repair that matches. Scores and numbers in the sim are practice examples.

Call 1: New heat pump won't restart

Mr. Tanaka
Cause and explanation: Nothing was wrong. The control enforces a delay (about 5 minutes on one maker's board) between compressor off and on, the fan waits about 20 seconds after defrost, and defrost only starts when the outdoor air is below about 50 F, the timer interval (about 90 minutes default on that board, with options) has elapsed and the coil is near freezing. The customer short-cycled the thermostat. Replacing the board or compressor would have cost money and fixed nothing. A forced-defrost or bypass jumper is a test aid only.
Correct action: explain the wait or normal behavior to the customer; replace nothing (Wait 5 minutes, then explain the built-in delays to the customer).
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Is there a fault code? Check the control's timers. / Compressors have a built-in off-delay.
Source basis: Carrier 25HNB6 heat pump manual (one maker's example timings, shown as about).

Call 2: Steaming, defrosts every 15 minutes

The Whitfields
Cause and explanation: Defrost starting about every 15 minutes and ending in seconds points to the control or the sensor: a bad control, a bad sensor, wrong sensor placement, or wind preventing normal termination. Here the sensor was hanging out of its well on the wrong tube. Reposition and secure it, shield the unit from wind without blocking coil airflow, and check refrigerant distribution if the coil frosts unevenly.
Repairs needed: Reposition and secure the defrost sensor.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Note the defrost interval and how long it lasts. What tells the board to defrost? / Look at the defrost sensor's position.
Source basis: Trane defrost control installation sheet.

Call 3: Blowing cool air in heating mode

Mr. Alvarez
Cause and explanation: In heating the reversing valve coil is not energized on this system, so no voltage there is normal. Pressures that are too close together (high suction, low head), a very low temperature rise across the compressor and cool air at the registers mean the valve is leaking internally and is stuck between positions. The valve must be replaced: recover the refrigerant, braze in the new valve, evacuate and weigh in the nameplate charge. Never vent refrigerant.
Repairs needed: Recover all refrigerant to a recovery cylinder; Replace reversing valve; Evacuate, then weigh in nameplate charge.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Run it, read both pressures and compare them with a normal heating system. Then check what the reversing valve coil is doing. / The coil has no voltage, which is right for heating. But the suction and discharge pressures are too close together and the two valve lines feel almost the same temperature.

Call 4: Outdoor unit covered in ice

Ms. Kowalski
Cause and explanation: A heat pump collects frost in cold, damp weather and defrosts by briefly reversing the cycle. The defrost sensor on the outdoor coil tells the control when to do that. This sensor read open, so the control never started defrost and the coil iced over. That caused very low suction pressure and high superheat. Replace the sensor, then let the unit run a defrost cycle.
Repairs needed: Replace defrost sensor.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Look at the outdoor coil, then at suction pressure and superheat. / Check the defrost sensor with power off. A cold sensor should read a definite resistance, not open.

Call 5: Weak heat, runs a lot

The Nguyens
Cause and explanation: Low suction pressure, high superheat (about 20 F) and low subcooling (about 3 F) point to a low charge. Charge is only lost through a leak, here a bad Schrader core at the outdoor service valve. Repair the leak first, then recover, evacuate and weigh in the nameplate charge. Topping off again would just repeat the problem.
Repairs needed: Repair leak at service valve core; Evacuate, then weigh in nameplate charge.
Also acceptable (no penalty): Recover all refrigerant to a recovery cylinder.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Read gauges and temperatures, then calculate superheat and subcooling. / Low suction, high superheat and low subcooling. Only a leak removes refrigerant.

Call 6: Heat trips on high pressure

Mr. O'Brien
Cause and explanation: In heating the indoor coil rejects heat. A clogged filter cuts airflow, so head pressure and subcooling rise and the supply air runs very hot. The fix is airflow, not refrigerant. Never adjust the charge to cover an airflow problem.
Repairs needed: Replace air filter.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Compare head pressure and subcooling with normal, then check airflow. / In heating the indoor coil is the condenser. Check the filter.

Call 7: Outdoor unit silent, huge electric bill

Ms. Washington
Cause and explanation: The contactor coil had 24 V and was pulling in, but the contacts were pitted and passed no voltage to the compressor and fan. The system fell back on electric aux heat, which costs far more than a working heat pump. Verify power is off, replace the contactor, and confirm the compressor and fan run.
Repairs needed: Replace contactor.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Is the outdoor unit running? Check the thermostat call, then voltage at the contactor coil and load side. / Coil has 24 V but the load side stays at 0 V.

How numbers are labeled. Readings, prices, set points, timers, blink codes and trip values in the calls are practice examples made up for the game unless a call says a source gives them. Words like about, example and practice mark them. Where a number comes from one maker's document (for example Hoshizaki timers or Trane flash codes), it is that maker's example and other makers differ. Rules of thumb (such as coolers about 35 to 38 F) are common practice, not rules. Laws and codes are cited by name: confirm the current text. Always follow the equipment maker's instructions and local code.
