Answer key: Steam heat exchanger

Teacher copy. Student worksheet | Open the simulator module

Learning goals
  • Explain how a steam trap works, what a stall is, and how a vacuum breaker or pump trap fixes a flooded coil.
  • Name the main causes of steam hammer (pockets, sag, bad traps, fast valve opening) and warm a cold line up safely.
  • Explain why a trap that has failed open is repaired, not valved off.
Safety note from the module. Educational simulation only. Steam and condensate cause severe burns. Isolate, bleed and lock out before opening a trap, strainer or valve. Warm up cold lines slowly. If a trap fails open, repair it: do not just valve it shut. Follow the plant's procedures and the maker's instructions.

Answers are the simulator's own explanations, so wording in a student's own words will differ. Accept any answer that names the cause and a repair that matches. Scores and numbers in the sim are practice examples.

Call 1: Small steam coil floods whenever the load drops

Facilities lead Mr. Okoro
Cause and explanation: At low load the control valve throttles, the steam in the coil condenses faster than it is replaced, and the coil pressure falls to or below the trap's outlet pressure (a vacuum here). The trap cannot discharge, so condensate stands in the steam space and floods the coil. With a gravity return to a vented receiver, a vacuum breaker on the coil lets air in so condensate drains by gravity to the trap. If condensate had to be lifted after the trap, a breaker would not help: use a pump trap, per the maker. Replacing the trap fixed nothing, because it was working. Small exchangers are the usual victims; large ones avoid breakers because air in the steam space lowers heat transfer, so follow the maker. No set pressures are given in the sources: values here are examples.
Repairs needed: Fit a vacuum breaker at the top of the coil, as the maker specifies or Fit a pump trap (when condensate must be lifted or the maker calls for it).
Also acceptable (no penalty): Check the trap size against the maker's table.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Compare the steam pressure at the coil with the trap outlet at low load. / At low load the coil is at or below the pressure it must drain into. How could air get in?
Source basis: Forbes Marshall note on stalling small heat exchangers; Armstrong TAVB bulletin (maker); Spirax Sarco water-hammer article (pump trap). Numbers are practice examples. (research/hvac-books/32)

Call 2: Hard banging on cold start-up

Maintenance foreman Ms. Pruitt
Cause and explanation: Condensate pooled in a sagging pipe with no drip leg, and the operator opened the isolation valve fully onto a cold, wet line. Steam collapsing in subcooled condensate creates condensate-induced hammer, which can be far more violent than flow-driven hammer (illustrative figures run to over 1,000 psi). Fix the sag, add a drip leg and trap at the low point, and warm the line up gradually with the bypass. Other causes to check: failed or undersized traps, boiler carryover and rapid valve opening. Source: Spirax Sarco in Plant Engineering (2006). Numbers are illustrations.
Repairs needed: Replace the failed hanger and fix the sag; Add a drip leg and trap at the low point; Write a warm-up step: crack the bypass and open the isolation valve slowly.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Look at the pipe and at how the valve is opened on a cold start. / Where does condensate collect before the control valve?
Source basis: Spirax Sarco, Water Hammer in Steam Systems (Plant Engineering, 2006); HeatingHelp steam water hammer guide (trade). (research/hvac-books/32)

Call 3: Steam plume at the receiver, return line too hot

Plant engineer Mr. Haas
Cause and explanation: The trap had failed open: live steam was blowing through to the receiver, wasting energy and heating the return. Debris past a fouled strainer had held the seat open. Replace the trap with the right type sized per the maker, and clean the strainer. Do not just valve the trap off: a closed trap leaves the coil to flood. Source: Spirax Sarco guidance (failed-open: repair, do not valve it shut); trap testing methods in the DOE steam tip sheet and TLV guidance (file 14). Numbers are practice examples.
Repairs needed: Replace the failed steam trap with the same type, sized per the maker.
Also acceptable (no penalty): Clean the strainer screen.
Teacher prompts (the sim hints): Test the trap and watch the receiver vent. / What can hold a trap seat open? Look at the strainer.
Source basis: Spirax Sarco, Plant Engineering 2006; DOE steam trap tip sheet; TLV trap testing (research/hvac-books/14 and 32).

How numbers are labeled. Readings, prices, set points, timers, blink codes and trip values in the calls are practice examples made up for the game unless a call says a source gives them. Words like about, example and practice mark them. Where a number comes from one maker's document (for example Hoshizaki timers or Trane flash codes), it is that maker's example and other makers differ. Rules of thumb (such as coolers about 35 to 38 F) are common practice, not rules. Laws and codes are cited by name: confirm the current text. Always follow the equipment maker's instructions and local code.
