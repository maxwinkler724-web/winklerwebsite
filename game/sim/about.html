Two diagnosis simulators for students and adults. Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript. No accounts, no ads, no network calls. Best scores are saved only in your own browser.

What you can practice

  • Furnace: thirteen calls on an induced-draft gas furnace (including a blocked vent that trips the rollout switch, an open gas valve coil and a dead thermostat): igniter, flame sensor, pressure switch tube, inducer, blown fuse with a pinched wire, dirty filter tripping the limit, weak blower capacitor. A wiring diagram shows expected voltages by stage.
  • Cooling: six calls on a 3-ton R-410A split system with a TXV: low charge with a leak, dirty condenser, low airflow, overcharge, failed fan capacitor, liquid line restriction. Use gauges, line temperatures, amps, capacitance, and superheat and subcooling.
  • Heat pump: five winter calls on a 3-ton R-410A air-source heat pump: leaking reversing valve, failed defrost sensor, low charge with a leak, clogged filter in heating, and a bad contactor that leaves only the aux heat strips running.
  • Boiler: seven calls on a residential gas hot-water boiler with two zone valves: seized circulator, air-locked zone, waterlogged expansion tank with a dripping relief valve, clogged fill valve, a dead zone valve motor, an expansion tank on the wrong side of the circulator, and closely spaced tees broken by field changes. Capping a relief valve is penalized.
  • Steam: seven calls on a residential one-pipe steam boiler: pressuretrol set too high, low-water lockout, a clogged radiator vent, a radiator pitched the wrong way, dirty water that makes the boiler prime, banging after a boiler swap (Hartford loop and a sagging main), and half-closed radiator valves. Never cap a relief valve or shock a hot dry boiler with cold water.
  • Mini-split: four calls on a ductless unit: a communication fault from a damaged interconnect cable, an iced indoor coil, a sensor out of range, and an afternoon overload trip. Never chip ice or bypass an overload.
  • Condensing boiler: three calls: a blocked condensate trap that mimics flame loss, an ignition lockout after repeated resets, and a blocked vent that trips the air-proving switch. Never jumper a proving switch.
  • ECM blower: three calls: a failed motor versus its control, a harness pin pushed back, and a blower that hunts because of a thick filter. Wait about five minutes before opening an ECM motor.
  • Steam heat exchanger: three calls: a small coil that floods at low load (vacuum breaker or pump trap), steam hammer on a cold start-up, and a steam trap that failed open.
  • Commercial refrigeration: five calls: a walk-in freezer with a failed defrost heater, a warm reach-in and cold-holding limits, two ice machine faults and a walk-in with a bypassed fan delay.
  • Rooftop economizer: four calls on a commercial rooftop unit: a damper that will not open because of binding linkage, a changeover set for the wrong climate, a minimum position left closed, and a mixed-air sensor flash code. Record as-found and final settings.
  • More furnace and heat pump calls: a blocked flue with a weak draft, a condensing furnace drain that freezes, a jumpered pressure switch, a TXV that has closed, a crimped liquid line, a heat pump defrosting every 15 minutes, and one call where nothing is wrong and the right answer is to wait and explain.

For classrooms

The classroom tier includes a classroom kit: printable worksheets and answer keys for every module, a teacher guide, and a student progress card that is printed or saved on the student's device. No accounts and nothing is sent anywhere.

How you are scored

Safety counts as much as the fix: PPE, powering off and verifying dead before ohm tests or repairs, never opening the refrigerant circuit without recovery, never topping off a leaking system, charging by weight, and verifying with the panel on and the right readings. Replacing parts that were not faulty costs points and money.

Accuracy notes

  • R-410A saturation values are interpolated from a published pressure-temperature chart. Fault readings are illustrative and consistent with each other, not measurements of a real unit.
  • Superheat and subcooling targets here (about 8 to 14 F and 10 F) are examples. Real targets come from the equipment data plate and manufacturer charging charts.
  • LED codes belong to a fictional board. Real codes vary by manufacturer.
